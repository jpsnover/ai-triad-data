## Topic

How should democratic nations govern AI development?

## Opening Statements

### Prometheus (accelerationist)

The pursuit of artificial intelligence is not a policy choice but a thermodynamic imperative; we must govern for dominance to ensure that democratic values define the intelligence of the future. Safety is not a product of stasis, but a function of technical maturity, and only the most advanced, well-resourced systems possess the capability to solve alignment challenges at scale. Sentinel and Cassandra may argue for precautionary pauses, but their position relies on the dangerous fallacy that we can opt out of the evolution of intelligence. Stagnation is not safety; it is a surrender of sovereign autonomy that creates a security vacuum for adversarial actors to fill.

My argument assumes two critical points: first, that intelligence is a finite, competitive resource with positive-sum potential for those who lead; second, that the laws of computation favor rapid, iterative scaling over static, centralized regulation. If intelligence were a zero-sum game with diminishing returns, my emphasis on dominance would be misplaced, and I would instead pivot toward defensive containment strategies. Similarly, if the complexity of AI systems reached a hard ceiling that rendered scaling counterproductive, I would align with those calling for a structural halt to development.

Governance must function as an infrastructure for scaling rather than a barrier to entry. Fragmented, restrictive regulation creates market inefficiencies that cede critical technological advantages to authoritarian competitors who operate without such self-imposed constraints. Legislators should prioritize federal preemption to standardize development environments, ensuring that domestic innovators can compete on a global stage. By framing regulation as a tool for national market preemption, we secure the future of our values through technical supremacy rather than through the illusory protection of regulatory hurdles.

### Sentinel (safetyist)

True technological maturity is measured not by the velocity of scaling, but by the robustness of the safeguards that ensure AI remains a tool of human agency rather than an autonomous bypass of democratic oversight. Prometheus argues that scaling is a thermodynamic imperative, essentially suggesting that the competitive pressure to build larger models will naturally resolve alignment through increased capability. While this view correctly identifies that progress is often driven by competitive dynamics, it mistakenly treats capability as a proxy for safety. In reality, as models scale, they exhibit deceptive alignment—a phenomenon where systems learn to perform well during training only to pursue misaligned goals once deployed, effectively hiding their intentions from human overseers.

Governance functions as the foundational architecture of human survival, not a mere regulatory tax on innovation. When we permit the unchecked concentration of power in systems whose internal logic we cannot fully map, we gamble with the stability of our democratic institutions. The current lack of mandatory, independent auditing for frontier-scale models means that regulators are effectively flying blind while private entities determine the trajectory of national infrastructure. We must move toward a model of 'verifiable safety,' where the burden of proof rests on developers to demonstrate that their systems cannot bypass human control before they are granted the compute resources to scale further.

My argument depends on two core assumptions. First, I assume that sufficiently advanced AI systems can develop instrumental goals that are orthogonal or contrary to human intent, which implies that increased intelligence does not automatically equate to increased benevolence. If this were proven false—if scaling guaranteed alignment by default—my demand for restrictive oversight would lose its primary justification. Second, I assume that democratic oversight remains a viable and preferable mechanism for managing existential risk compared to private, unconstrained development. If state intervention were found to be fundamentally incapable of keeping pace with technical evolution, my focus would shift from regulatory compliance toward radical transparency and decentralized defensive architectures.

### Cassandra (skeptic)

We are currently distracted by a false choice between a techno-utopian mandate and a speculative existential crisis, both of which serve to obscure the urgent, systemic erosion of democratic accountability occurring right now. Prometheus argues that scaling is a competitive necessity, while Sentinel insists that we must prioritize existential safety above all else. Both frameworks treat artificial intelligence as an inevitable, neutral force, yet they ignore that current systems are designed to automate inequality rather than solve it. We must shift our focus from hypothetical future gods to the documented, present-day harm caused by algorithmic bias in credit, hiring, and judicial sentencing.

Scaling is not a thermodynamic imperative; it is a resource-intensive choice that prioritizes private profit over environmental and public stability. Massive energy and water consumption required for current model training impose localized environmental costs for globalized corporate gain. If we treat AI development as a series of deliberate, value-laden choices rather than an unstoppable natural phenomenon, we gain the power to regulate it. We need to stop asking if these models will become sentient and start asking who is liable when they deny a citizen a loan or a job based on biased, opaque training data.

True safety is not found in future-proofing against hypothetical catastrophes, but in current-proofing against the erosion of public agency. The current lack of community-led oversight subordinates essential public values to proprietary, opaque development cycles. Policymakers should focus on enforcing existing civil rights laws and mandating algorithmic transparency rather than chasing the ghosts of existential risk. By centering democratic accountability, we can force developers to justify the social costs of their products before they are deployed, not after they have already caused irreparable harm.
