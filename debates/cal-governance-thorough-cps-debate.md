## Topic

## Opening Statements

### Prometheus (accelerationist)

Democratic security depends on achieving rapid, market-driven mastery of intelligence, because stagnation is not a neutral state but a form of unilateral disarmament. Sentinel and Cassandra argue for precautionary governance, suggesting that we can pause or constrain progress until we achieve perfect safety. This safetyist position assumes that we possess the luxury of time and that regulators can manage complex, emergent systems without stifling the very innovation required to solve global existential threats. If we cede the frontier of artificial intelligence to authoritarian regimes that prioritize raw capability over democratic norms, we ensure that our values will have no voice in the architecture of the future. 

Empirical safety is best achieved through iterative, real-world deployment rather than through the speculative, top-down paralysis of centralized regulation. History demonstrates that decentralized, competitive systems identify and correct vulnerabilities far faster than bureaucratic frameworks, which tend to entrench incumbents and block agile, smaller-scale solutions. By treating safety as an engineering challenge solvable through scale—rather than a policy puzzle solvable through restriction—we harness the competitive pressure of global markets to harden our systems. We must replace the current trend of restrictive, permission-based oversight with a framework that rewards high-velocity experimentation and rapid feedback cycles.

Scaling intelligence is the fundamental engine for mitigating systemic risks, from resource scarcity to the instability of aging infrastructure. The cost of inaction—surrendering the technological high ground—poses a greater existential threat than the manageable risks of rapid development. We must move beyond the false binary of 'safe' versus 'fast' and recognize that, in a world of geopolitical competition, the only way to be safe is to be first. Our policy focus must shift from constraining the development of intelligence to securing the democratic dominance of it.

We must also confront the material reality that the 'AI race' ignores its own foundation. Training these models requires massive amounts of energy and labor, yet these costs are being offloaded onto the public through environmental degradation and the exploitation of precarious workers. When we treat the development of AI as an abstract, ethereal contest of 'speed,' we blind ourselves to the tangible, present-day harms that are already being subsidized by the public.
