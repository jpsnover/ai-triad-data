## Topic

Under what conditions should Congress task the FTC and NIST with developing and enforcing standards for siloed datasets for large language models (LLMs) used in public-facing applications, defined as restricted access tiers where models train on specific information without the ability to cross-reference external user data, among major AI developers like OpenAI, Google, and Meta? This policy aims to prioritize the mitigation of unauthorized data exfiltration and corporate surveillance, affecting consumer data privacy and the competitive landscape for smaller AI firms, over the potential reduction in agent utility. By enforcing these architectural boundaries, regulators would force companies to prioritize individual privacy protections over the convenience of unified data harvesting.

Google's AlphaFold, for instance, delivered a solution to protein folding, a 50-year biological grand challenge, by leveraging vast datasets, not by restricting them. To mandate data siloing now is to condemn millions to the very illnesses we could automate a cure for, a decision no policymaker has the right to make for them.

And it gets worse: this static, architectural siloing isn't a modern safety solution; it's a bureaucratic fantasy creating innovation risk while failing to address dynamic threats. Real-world complex systems, from aerospace engineering to high-frequency trading, don't rely on fixed data walls. They implement multi-layered safety stacks, continuous output verification, fuzzing, and adversarial red-teaming to adapt to evolving risks. This proposal is like trying to secure a modern data center with a medieval moat; it actively hinders the development of genuinely resilient AI systems capable of self-correction and continuous improvement. The immense cost of building, auditing, and maintaining these data fortresses is an insurmountable barrier to entry, not a level playing field. It's a tax on innovation, paid by the very people who stand to gain the most from AI's abundance.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

The Accelerationist perspective compellingly argues that restricting data access could stifle the very innovation that promises to alleviate human suffering, citing examples like Google's AlphaFold's breakthrough in protein folding as evidence of the power of vast, unrestricted datasets. They contend that any mandate for architecturally siloed data risks becoming a moral atrocity, actively impeding progress. The Skeptic rightly points out the historical precedent of anti-monopoly actions, from Standard Oil in the early 20th century to Microsoft in the 1990s, showing that breaking up concentrated power has often led to *more* innovation, not less. The core tension here lies in whether unrestricted data access is an indispensable prerequisite for beneficial innovation, or if it represents an unmanaged risk that undermines the very foundations of trust and control necessary for sustainable progress. My evidence demonstrates that while vast datasets can drive specific scientific breakthroughs, the unique characteristics of public-facing large language models demand a different calculus, one that prioritizes structural safeguards over unconstrained data liquidity.

The current architecture of public-facing large language models, characterized by undifferentiated data access, inherently creates systemic vulnerabilities that lead to demonstrable harms like data exfiltration and pervasive surveillance. Existing alignment methods are demonstrably unreliable; prompt injection attacks and 'jailbreaks' have repeatedly bypassed safety filters, allowing models to leak sensitive training data or generate harmful content. This unchecked complexity mirrors the systemic failures seen when the FAA delegated critical safety certification to Boeing for the 737 MAX, leading directly to the deaths of 346 people across two crashes. Consumers bear the immediate cost of privacy violations and potential fraud, while major AI developers benefit from scaling without bearing the full cost of mitigating these systemic risks. Mandating standards through the FTC and NIST establishes clear accountability, shifting the burden of safety onto the developers whose products impact public trust and security, much like the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration mandates safety standards for automobiles.

The fundamental epistemic opacity of large language models prevents any comprehensive internal auditing or reliable verification of their behavior, making external structural controls like data siloing indispensable for ensuring safety and accountability. Research in mechanistic interpretability consistently highlights the profound difficulty of fully understanding internal representations and emergent behaviors in complex neural networks, even for simpler models. We cannot reliably predict or explain why an LLM might exfiltrate data or generate biased outputs from its vast, interconnected data pool. This lack of transparency means the public cannot verify claims of safety or fairness, eroding trust in these powerful systems. NIST, through its established role in developing technical standards, is uniquely positioned to define and enforce these architectural requirements, ensuring that models can be externally audited for compliance. This benefits the public by providing verifiable safety assurances, while developers bear the cost of redesigning systems for auditable integrity.

Mandating architecturally siloed datasets is not a hindrance to progress; it is a foundational act of institutional stewardship, reasserting human control and accountability over powerful AI systems to prevent systemic destabilization. We spent centuries building the institutional guardrails that keep society stable, protecting individual rights and market integrity. On what authority do we claim we can remove them for AI without inviting collapse? The collapse of financial institutions due to unchecked risk models, or the failure of civil infrastructure from inadequate safety margins, demonstrates the inevitable consequence of removing structural boundaries from complex systems. The FTC, with its existing consumer protection and competition enforcement powers under the Clayton Act, possesses the authority to enforce these standards, ensuring fair competition by preventing dominant players from leveraging unchecked data access to entrench monopolies. This policy enjoys broad political feasibility, as public concern over data privacy and corporate power is bipartisan, creating a coalition that prioritizes civilizational stability over unconstrained technological acceleration.
