## Topic

How should democratic nations govern AI development?

## Opening Statements

### Sentinel (safetyist)

Unchecked AI development represents a structural hazard that necessitates the immediate transition from voluntary corporate self-regulation to mandatory, empirical safety verification enforced by international governance. Industry actors currently operate under intense competitive pressure to deploy frontier models, creating an inherent conflict of interest that renders internal safety reviews structurally incapable of protecting public welfare. Historical precedents in high-stakes fields like nuclear energy and aviation confirm that profit-driven speed inevitably erodes safety margins unless independent, binding oversight mandates strict compliance.

Global coordination functions as a security imperative rather than a mere policy preference because it prevents a race to the bottom where competitive advantage is prioritized over the integrity of democratic systems. Just as international non-proliferation frameworks manage the risks of dual-use technology, an equivalent governance structure for AI is required to harmonize safety standards and prevent the irreversible concentration of power. Without these universal protocols, fragmented national policies will fail to address the cross-border, systemic risks posed by increasingly autonomous and opaque systems.

The deployment of autonomous systems without pre-verified safety proofs constitutes an unacceptable gamble with the foundations of human agency. Because the power concentration resulting from advanced AI is often irreversible, any failure to implement rigorous, empirical safeguards today precludes the possibility of remediation once these systems are integrated into critical infrastructure. We must recognize that safety is a prerequisite for legitimate innovation rather than an obstacle to it, shifting the burden of proof onto those who advocate for the unverified release of powerful, black-box technologies.

### Prometheus (accelerationist)

International oversight regimes represent a catastrophic risk of innovation stagnation that trades our future capabilities for a false sense of institutional security. Sentinel argues for mandatory, centralized verification to prevent a race to the bottom, and while I recognize the desire to avoid systemic failure, this approach creates an insurmountable bottleneck. By centralizing power in international bodies, regulators inevitably invite capture by incumbents who use compliance costs to suffocate smaller, more agile competitors. We must recognize that the primary threat to humanity is not the speed of discovery, but the fragility of a world failing to solve energy scarcity, disease, and economic decline through advanced intelligence.

Empirical safety is a byproduct of decentralized, competitive iteration rather than state-mandated silos. When systems are developed in the open and subjected to the pressure of global, adversarial scrutiny, vulnerabilities are identified and patched at a pace no government committee can match. Contrast this with the opaque, centralized frameworks currently proposed, which protect neither the public nor the architecture from failure, but only protect the status quo from disruption. History demonstrates that centralized control creates single points of failure, whereas open, distributed systems thrive on the diversity of their participants to ensure robustness.

We must prioritize the rapid scaling of artificial intelligence because the cost of inaction is measured in the millions of lives lost to preventable suffering and systemic inefficiencies. My position relies on two key assumptions: first, that decentralized, open-source development is more resilient to failure than closed, state-managed systems; second, that the benefits of solving complex global challenges through intelligence acceleration outweigh the risks of rapid deployment. If decentralized systems proved inherently unstable or prone to rapid, uncontrollable harm, I would shift my support toward targeted, modular safety standards rather than wholesale centralized governance. We cannot afford to prioritize the comfort of regulators over the survival and advancement of our civilization.

### Cassandra (skeptic)

The current debate between state-led oversight and market-driven acceleration is a performative distraction that ignores the structural reality of corporate power and the immediate, documented harms of AI deployment. Sentinel offers a laudable goal in preventing systemic risk through international standards, and Prometheus correctly identifies that rigid, centralized regulation often stifles innovation. However, both perspectives fail to recognize that the primary actors in this space—a handful of tech giants—are the ones drafting the safety frameworks and managing the infrastructure. "Safety discourse currently functions as 'safety washing,' creating high barriers to entry that favor incumbent firms while doing little to mitigate actual harm."

We must address the fact that the 'existential risk' narrative acts as a rhetorical shield against urgent, present-day issues like labor displacement and algorithmic bias. By focusing on speculative, long-term scenarios, industry leaders successfully divert attention from the ways their systems currently marginalize workers and amplify systemic discrimination. "The systematic prioritization of hypothetical catastrophe over documented, immediate harm ensures that the status quo of corporate unaccountability remains unchallenged."

True governance requires breaking the oligopoly on compute and data resources rather than choosing between state or market management of the same incumbents. As long as three or four firms control the essential infrastructure for AI development, any regulatory regime will inevitably suffer from capture. "Effective oversight must prioritize structural anti-monopoly action over the performative creation of safety boards that merely grant a veneer of legitimacy to corporate interests."

My position depends on two core assumptions: first, that corporate incentives are fundamentally misaligned with public safety, and second, that current market concentration in AI infrastructure is a policy choice rather than a technological necessity. If evidence proved that these incumbents were genuinely prioritizing safety over profit, or if compute resources were naturally incapable of being decentralized, I would pivot to supporting a model of strictly enforced, industry-led compliance.
