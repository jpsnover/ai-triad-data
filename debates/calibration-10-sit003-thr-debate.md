## Opening Statements

### Sentinel (safetyist)

Innovation without verified safety is not progress; it is the reckless acceleration of unconstrained systemic risk that can only be mitigated through mandatory, evidence-based global governance. The current development race follows a classic prisoner’s dilemma, where companies face structural incentives to prioritize speed over alignment to secure market dominance. Because these actors are beholden to shareholders rather than the public interest, corporate self-regulation remains structurally incapable of preventing catastrophic externalities.

Pre-deployment verification must serve as the mandatory threshold for any integration of advanced artificial intelligence into critical infrastructure. We define verification as the empirical, replicable demonstration that a system’s behavior remains bounded and predictable under stress. Current alignment methods are insufficient because they rely on post-hoc patching rather than fundamental, measurable safety proofs. Developers must prove that a model is safe before it reaches the public, as the complexity of emergent behaviors in large-scale systems makes reactive containment impossible once a model is deployed.

Global binding agreements offer the only viable defense against a race to the bottom where safety standards are eroded by competitive pressure. Just as the international community established the International Atomic Energy Agency to monitor nuclear proliferation, regulators must now establish a binding framework for computational governance. This framework must prioritize international transparency and rigorous auditing over the short-term economic gains of individual firms. Without such top-down, enforceable constraints, the current trajectory guarantees an eventual system failure that no amount of post-deployment safety patching can reverse.

### Prometheus (accelerationist)

This approach avoids the trap of regulatory capture, where dominant firms use safety mandates to pull up the ladder behind them, effectively outlawing the competition from smaller, more agile startups.

My argument depends on two core assumptions: first, that technical security is a tractable engineering problem that improves with scale and competition; second, that the costs of state-induced stagnation exceed the risks of decentralized development. My argument depends on two key assumptions: first, that systemic algorithmic harm is a feature of current profit-incentive structures rather than a bug that can be patched; and second, that democratic institutions are currently captured by the very entities they are tasked to regulate.
