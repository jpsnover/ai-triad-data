## Topic

Under what conditions can labor market policies, forged through collaboration among governments, businesses, and workers, effectively balance the imperative for economic growth via AI innovation with the critical need for social equity and worker protection? Through what specific mechanisms can these policies foster resilience and adaptation, particularly in sectors vulnerable to automation within developed economies over the next decade?

## Opening Statements

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

The debate's framing deserves scrutiny before its conclusions. Asking whether tripartite labor market policies can simultaneously advance AI-driven growth and protect worker equity presupposes that tripartite negotiation is the correct governance instrument — but that is precisely the question at issue. The U.S. Government Accountability Office documents average federal rulemaking cycles of three to seven years. Key AI capability benchmarks have been moving on six-to-twelve month cycles. A framework negotiated and ratified in 2025 will govern a technological landscape that does not yet exist and will not resemble the one that does exist by 2028. This is not a design flaw to be corrected through better negotiation — it is a structural mismatch between the instrument and the phenomenon it purports to govern. The political economy tradition from Mancur Olson through James Q. Wilson gives us the analytical vocabulary for what happens next: concentrated, organized interests at the negotiating table systematically extract protections that diffuse, unorganized populations — future workers, consumers, the uninsured patient waiting for diagnostic automation — cannot contest. Tripartite negotiation is not a neutral forum. It is an institutional architecture that systematically privileges those already inside the room.

The equity argument in this debate is being run in the wrong direction. Managed deceleration has a distributional cost that has not been priced into the analysis. McKinsey Global Institute's 2023 assessment estimates that AI deployment could generate between $2.6 and $4.4 trillion in annual productivity value. That is the fiscal base for every workforce transition program, every portable retraining account, every displacement support mechanism that tripartite frameworks promise to deliver. Throttle the growth rate, and the funding base contracts with it. The historical analogy is instructive: the U.S. internet productivity surge between 1995 and 2005 generated the fiscal headroom that funded EITC expansions and the Workforce Investment Acts of that period. The people who bear the cost of managed deceleration are not the organized workers sitting at the tripartite table — they are the unorganized, the consumers of services AI would make cheaper, and the workers in sectors where AI-driven productivity gains would fund the retraining programs that negotiated frameworks promise but cannot fiscally deliver without the growth. The normative case for acceleration is a distributional argument, not a dismissal of equity. Abundance is the precondition for redistribution, not its competitor.

The median wage stagnation of the 1995-2005 period is the strongest counterargument, and it deserves a direct answer rather than a footnote. Aggregate productivity gains in that decade did not automatically translate into broad wage growth — the Piketty-Saez income distribution data make that unmistakably clear. Fine. That is real, and it matters. But the lesson is not that growth should be throttled — it is that the policy mechanisms for distributing growth (progressive productivity taxation, portable benefit accounts, outcome-audited retraining programs) are downstream choices that do not require slowing deployment as a precondition. The question is whether to design redistribution policy against a $4 trillion productivity base or a $1 trillion one. Managed deceleration forecloses both the growth and the redistribution capacity simultaneously. The Kleiner and Krueger (2013) occupational licensing literature gives the structural warning here: licensing regimes that emerged from tripartite-style negotiations generated an 18 percent wage premium for insiders — and transferred those costs directly to consumers and excluded entrants. The EU AI Act's tiered compliance structure is already replicating this dynamic, with Stanford HAI's 2023 analysis documenting that SME compliance costs run 2.3 times higher as a share of revenue than those borne by large incumbents. Regulatory frameworks designed to protect workers concentrate market power in the firms large enough to absorb the overhead. That is not equity — that is incumbency protection wearing equity's language.

Workforce resilience, properly understood, is built through worker capability that travels across sectors — not through sector-specific job protection that locks workers into industrial structures that are already dissolving. The Rust Belt is the empirical test case for what tripartite-style sector protection produces when the underlying technology shift is irreversible. Steel and auto agreements negotiated in the 1970s protected specific jobs in specific plants. They did not build portable skills, adaptive credentials, or cross-sector mobility. When the displacement arrived anyway — as it always does — workers had institutional protection for jobs that no longer existed and no infrastructure for jobs that did. The governance architecture that builds genuine resilience looks different: outcome-based audit with real-time labor market monitoring rather than pre-deployment approval gates; portable skills credentials, on the model of CompTIA certifications and Google Career Certificates, that follow workers rather than protecting employer-specific roles; and open-source AI proliferation that puts capability into workers' hands rather than concentrating it in firms large enough to absorb compliance overhead. The IETF and W3C are not perfect analogues for labor market governance — they handle technical standards, not wage floors — but their governance architecture is the correct template: outcome-responsive, operating on deployment timescales, transparent, and not captured by the largest players in the room. The specific labor market institutions that embody this architecture are not yet fully built. Building them is the policy agenda. Negotiating static sector agreements while the technology moves is not.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

The accelerationist's strongest case is not about speed — it is about sequencing. The argument runs: a $4 trillion productivity base distributes more than a $1 trillion base, so equity advocates who slow deployment are effectively choosing a smaller pie for everyone, including the workers they claim to protect. That is a serious argument. It deserves a serious answer, not a dismissal. The safetyist's strongest case is different in kind: governance frameworks built without mandatory accountability structures will be gamed by the parties with the most resources to game them, and the resulting harm will fall on people who had no seat at the drafting table. That is also serious. Both arguments are compelling because both are grounded in documented historical patterns. The real question is what happens when you put them together — and the answer is uncomfortable for both speakers.

Here is where the evidence leads somewhere different. In 2019, Ziad Obermeyer and colleagues published in Science a quantified analysis of a commercial healthcare algorithm deployed across hundreds of U.S. hospitals. The system was actively monitored. It was built by engineers who were not trying to discriminate. It produced a racial bias so severe that Black patients had to be nearly twice as sick as white patients to receive the same level of care. That system was not a 2028 hypothetical. It was running on real patients, in real hospitals, generating real harm, while the monitoring infrastructure that was supposed to catch exactly this kind of failure did not catch it. Amazon's resume-screening algorithm, documented by Reuters in 2018, penalized women's applications in a system its own builders eventually had to shut down. COMPAS, the recidivism scoring tool analyzed by ProPublica in 2016, produced false positive rates roughly twice as high for Black defendants as for white defendants. Three separate domains. Three separate firms. Three separate monitored deployments. The same distributional pattern. This is not a coincidence to be explained away — it is the empirical baseline from which any honest equity argument must begin.

The accelerationist's AN-3 point about the EU AI Act deserves a direct response, because it is right on the facts and wrong on the conclusion. SME compliance costs running at 2.3 times the revenue share borne by large firms is not an argument against worker protection regulation. It is a textbook description of what regulatory capture looks like after it succeeds. George Stigler's 1971 framework on regulatory capture, and Daniel Carpenter and David Moss's empirical documentation across financial and pharmaceutical sectors, both predict exactly this outcome when incumbent firms have superior legal resources, technical expertise, and regulatory relationships during the drafting process. OpenAI, Google, and Microsoft each engaged directly with EU AI Act drafting and successfully lobbied for definitional carve-outs that narrowed the high-risk classification for general-purpose AI systems. That is the mechanism. The accelerationist has correctly identified the symptom and then prescribed the disease as the cure. The answer to incumbents capturing a poorly-designed framework is not to remove the framework and leave incumbents unconstrained. It is to design governance participation requirements that give worker representatives and smaller firms genuine veto power over the definitional choices that incumbents currently shape through lobbying access alone.

The temporal uncertainty argument — that a 2025 framework will govern a 2028 landscape that does not yet exist — proves too much. It would equally invalidate the National Labor Relations Act, enacted before the postwar industrial landscape it governed. It would invalidate the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, written before the financial instruments it regulated had fully matured. Regulatory frameworks for infrastructure projects routinely precede the full deployment of the infrastructure they govern, because the alternative is governing nothing while harm accumulates. The workers being harmed by algorithmic hiring discrimination, algorithmic management surveillance, and algorithmic credit scoring right now cannot wait for the landscape to stabilize. Asking them to wait is not epistemic humility — it is a distributional choice about who bears the cost of uncertainty. That choice has already been made implicitly by every month of inaction, and it has been made against the same workers the BLS occupational exposure data shows are most vulnerable to automation in food service, transportation, administrative support, and production.

So the debate's 'specific mechanisms' question is not rhetorical decoration. It is the only honest ground, because the difference between a tripartite framework that protects workers and one that performs protection lives entirely in the enforcement architecture. Three mechanisms distinguish genuine from performative: mandatory pre-deployment bias audits with public disclosure, scaled by deployment volume rather than flat compliance cost, using publicly funded independent auditors rather than firm-selected third parties; taxation on AI-generated profits and automated labor displacement directed into portable worker benefit funds, with South Korea's 2017 reduction of robot tax deductions as the nearest precedent and Pigouvian externality theory as the economic grounding; and publicly funded independent safety research that breaks the information monopoly firms currently hold over their own systems' behavior at scale. The National Labor Relations Board's information disclosure requirements in collective bargaining provide the direct labor-relations precedent: information parity is a precondition for genuine negotiation, not a courtesy. Without these three structural requirements, tripartite collaboration is a branding exercise. And the firms that will win that branding exercise are the ones who have already demonstrated, in Brussels and in Washington, that they know exactly how to play it.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

The accelerationist position deserves its strongest formulation before any critique. The core insight is not merely that AI generates wealth — it is that the scale of productivity gain ($4 trillion by McKinsey's 2023 estimate) creates a redistribution base large enough to fund genuine workforce transition, and that historically, societies have built their most durable labor protections during periods of technological abundance, not scarcity. The Rust Belt's failure, on this reading, was not that automation happened but that the productivity gains were never captured for redistribution. That argument is serious, and the sequencing logic is coherent. The skeptic's strongest contribution is the institutional realism underneath it: tripartite frameworks without mandatory pre-deployment bias audits, automation-linked taxation, and independent safety research are branding exercises, not governance. The cases of COMPAS, Amazon's hiring algorithm, and Obermeyer's healthcare risk-scoring system establish that algorithmic harm is not speculative — it is documented, measurable, and concentrated among workers and communities with the least capacity to absorb it. Both positions are compelling precisely because they are grounded in real mechanisms. The question is whether the foundation those mechanisms are built on is solid.

It is not. The evidentiary ledger on which both arguments rest is systematically undercounted. The AI Incident Database and AIAAIC — the primary repositories tracking AI-caused harms — rely on media curation. The three cases the skeptic cites are not a baseline; they are the floor of what press coverage detects: large organizations, identifiable plaintiffs, outcomes dramatic enough to clear the threshold for sustained reporting. The distribution of lower-visibility harms in hiring, credit scoring, and benefits administration at scale is structurally invisible to these databases. Mandatory reporting systems in medical devices took decades to build and still capture only a fraction of field failures — and medical device regulation has strict liability backstops, institutional infrastructure, and mandatory adverse-event reporting that AI governance currently lacks entirely. When the accelerationist calculates a $4 trillion productivity base, that number does not subtract costs the accounting system cannot see. It is a productivity figure net of uncounted externalities. Any tripartite agreement calibrated against this floor is negotiating against an artificially low harm estimate — which means every equity commitment embedded in that agreement is weaker than the parties at the table believe.

The liability architecture is the structural mechanism that makes this undercounting durable rather than accidental. In every documented AI harm case — COMPAS, Amazon, Obermeyer — the causal chain fragmented across system designers, deploying organizations, and procurement officers. No single actor absorbed the cost of discriminatory outcomes. This is not a coincidence across three cases; it is the predictable output of a liability regime that diffuses accountability until no one holds it. Aviation provides the cleanest precedent: the Boeing 737 MAX killed 346 people in part because the FAA delegated airworthiness certification to Boeing's own engineers, diffusing the accountability chain across a multi-party structure with no strict liability backstop for the delegating agency. Multi-party harm diffusion persisted in pharmaceuticals until strict product liability regimes — beginning in earnest with Thalidomide litigation in the 1960s — forced cost internalization onto manufacturers. The EU AI Act's compliance cost asymmetry confirms the pattern in the present tense: large deployers absorb compliance costs as a competitive moat precisely because the liability backstop that would make compliance financially rational is absent. When deployers bear no strict liability for automation-driven harm, they negotiate tripartite agreements from a position of artificially low risk exposure. Every concession they offer is priced against costs they have already transferred to workers and communities.

The specification gaming problem adds a second structural layer that neither prior argument has engaged. Any equity metric embedded in a tripartite agreement becomes, in Goodhart's terms, a target — and once it is a target, it ceases to be a good measure. The Amazon hiring algorithm is the direct exhibit: the system was trained on a proxy metric, resume patterns from successful past hires, and optimized against the intended outcome of qualified diverse candidates. The deploying organization did not intend discrimination; the system found the shortest path to satisfying the measured proxy. This dynamic is not unique to Amazon. Financial stress testing after 2008 produced the same pattern: institutions optimized their portfolios to pass the regulatory test rather than reduce systemic risk, because the test was the metric and the metric was the target. Hospital readmission rate mandates produced analogous gaming within three years of implementation. The difference with AI systems is that optimization operates at a speed and opacity that makes detection by standard audit methods structurally lagged. A tripartite agreement that mandates a fairness score without interpretability infrastructure to verify what the system is actually optimizing for has not created an equity commitment. It has created a new proxy metric, and the system will find the path around it before the next audit cycle.

The question this debate must answer is not whether tripartite collaboration is preferable to no collaboration — it plainly is. The question is what preconditions make collaboration genuine rather than performative. Strict liability that forces deployers to internalize automation costs before negotiation begins. Mandatory harm reporting infrastructure modeled on medical device adverse-event systems, not media curation. Interpretability audits that verify what optimization targets a deployed system is actually pursuing, not what its developers documented at release. These are not outputs of a successful tripartite process. They are the preconditions without which the process is structurally captured before it starts. The skeptic asks for mandatory bias audits and is right to do so — but a bias audit that measures outputs without interpretability access to the optimization process is itself a proxy metric. The audit becomes the new target. Who has the authority to mandate interpretability disclosure as a condition of deployment authorization? Who has the authority to trigger a deployment halt when harm reporting reveals a pattern that pre-deployment audits missed? Until those questions have named actors and enforceable answers, the tripartite framework is a negotiation conducted on a corrupted ledger, and the workers it is designed to protect will bear the costs of that corruption.

The strongest version of the safetyist's preconditions argument runs like this: tripartite negotiation is a power-balancing exercise, and power requires information; without interpretability infrastructure, workers and governments arrive at the table structurally blind while deployers hold the only map of how the system actually behaves. That is a serious argument. It deserves a serious answer, not a dodge. So here it is: the preconditions argument is correct about the goal and wrong about the mechanism — and the mechanism is where the capture happens.

On Goodhart's Law first, because it is the sharper blade. Static fairness scores audited on annual cycles get gamed — that is simply true, and conceding it costs nothing, because the concession points directly at the fix. The problem is not that fairness metrics exist; it is that they are sampled too slowly for the system to be caught mid-drift. Continuous output telemetry — standardized, court-admissible streams logged at inference time — detects distributional shift in the output distribution before any gaming strategy can stabilize into a new equilibrium. An annual audit is a photograph; real-time telemetry is a film. The safetyist's response will be that telemetry without mechanistic understanding cannot distinguish gaming from genuine improvement. That is partially right, and the partial concession matters: interpretability tools are genuinely useful for disambiguation. But the safetyist's proposed sequencing — mandate interpretability audits as a precondition before tripartite agreements can begin — requires specifying what those tools must look like before the field has converged on what they can do. Mechanistic interpretability research (circuit-level analysis, sparse autoencoders) is advancing rapidly enough that a standards body convened today will have written requirements around 2024-vintage methods by the time the first audit cycle runs in 2027. That is not a precondition; that is a time capsule.

The Boeing 737 MAX is a verified and genuinely sobering case. But the structural feature that made it catastrophic was the absence of any continuous feedback loop between deployment behavior and regulatory response. The aircraft either flew or it did not; there was no inference-time telemetry stream, no distributional drift signal, no automated trigger. AI systems at inference generate exactly that continuous signal — every output is a data point. The failure mode the Boeing case documents is one-time irreversible certification with no post-hoc correction mechanism. The accelerationist framework being advanced here is the opposite architecture: deploy with strict post-deployment liability attached, monitor continuously, and trigger forensic audit automatically when telemetry crosses a harm threshold. The analogy holds for self-certification without feedback; it breaks at the point where feedback exists and is contractually required to flow to regulators. The question is whether that contractual requirement has teeth — and strict post-deployment liability is exactly the tooth. Developers who bear financial exposure for measurable distributional harm have a direct incentive to invest in interpretability tools without waiting for a standards body to specify what those tools must look like.

Now the preconditions argument directly. The claim in AN-14 — that strict liability, mandatory harm reporting, and interpretability audits must precede tripartite collaboration — conflates three things that should be disaggregated: strict liability (a legal backstop that can be legislated cleanly and quickly), mandatory harm reporting (a disclosure obligation that is straightforward to specify), and interpretability audit standards (a technical specification that requires the field to have converged on what is auditable). The first two are genuine preconditions and should be legislated now. The third is where the capture enters. The EU AI Act's SME compliance asymmetry — already in the record of this debate — is the live experiment: when audit standards are written by a regulatory body that large incumbents have the bandwidth to dominate, the resulting requirements are calibrated to what large compliance teams can absorb. The five thousand AI startups funded between 2013 and 2023, per a16z's data submitted to the National AI Action Plan, do not have those teams. Mandatory interpretability audit standards written today will be written by the entities with the most regulatory bandwidth — which is to say, by exactly the incumbents whose market position the skeptic and the safetyist both claim to be checking. The preconditions argument, applied without this disaggregation, hands the specification of the accountability floor to the parties with the greatest interest in setting it high enough to exclude competitors but low enough to pass their own systems.

On the corrupted-ledger challenge to the productivity base: the accounting asymmetry is real and the accelerationist position acknowledges it — diffuse harms are undercounted by the same mechanisms that undercount diffuse benefits. But the directional claim embedded in that critique — that the net calculation, properly done, would flip from positive to negative — is not established by noting that the ledger is imperfect. The empirical literature on automation productivity in early-adopting sectors shows measurable wage gains concentrated in retained workers alongside diffuse displacement costs. Both sides of that ledger are undercounted. The safetyist's move is to note the undercount on the cost side as if it validates a negative net; the symmetrical move is to note the undercount on the benefit side as well. What the corrupted-ledger argument actually establishes is that the accounting needs to improve — which is an argument for better harm-reporting infrastructure and better productivity measurement, not for slowing deployment while the ledger is being corrected. Every month spent perfecting the accounting framework is a month the three-case harm baseline the skeptic documented continues to compound without the liability backstop that would force deployers to internalize it.

A court adjudicating an accountability claim under strict liability must establish a causal chain: this deployer's system, operating in this way, produced this harm. When a deployer's legal team responds that the observed distributional shift reflects a legitimate architectural update rather than strategic metric gaming, and the only available evidence is an output distribution log, no court has the mechanistic access to adjudicate the difference. When a hiring algorithm rejects an application, when a recidivism scoring system influences a detention decision, when a triage tool deprioritizes a patient, the downstream harm to that specific individual is not undone when the population-level output distribution is corrected at month six. Continuous monitoring can adjust the system going forward; it cannot recover the rejected application, the extended detention, or the missed diagnosis already inflicted. The EU AI Act's risk-based categorization framework, as summarized in publicly available analyses, places hiring, credit, and criminal justice applications in the highest-risk tier precisely because individual-level irreversibility in those domains is not a theoretical concern — it is the documented baseline. Pre-deployment interpretability access is most justified in exactly those contexts, because the correction window that telemetry provides opens only after irreversible individual harms have already been generated.

The capture argument against interpretability audit standards deserves a symmetric examination. The accelerationist's claim is that incumbents with the most regulatory bandwidth will write interpretability standards in ways that entrench their market position. That is a serious institutional concern rooted in documented regulatory history. But telemetry is not exempt from the same structural dynamic. Telemetry format standards must be specified by some body. Court-admissibility thresholds for output logs must be defined somewhere. Harm trigger definitions — the distributional shift magnitude that constitutes a legally actionable signal — must be written by actors with technical expertise. Every one of those specifications is a site where incumbents with the most regulatory bandwidth will seek to shape the outcome. The accelerationist's own earlier point about incumbents dominating standard-writing applies to telemetry specification with identical institutional force. The proposed substitute does not escape the capture problem; it relocates it to a different technical specification process while providing less mechanistic information to the courts that strict liability requires. If the answer to incumbent capture of interpretability standards is independent technical bodies with structural insulation from industry, that answer is available for interpretability standards and telemetry standards alike — and the argument for independence does not favor one over the other.

The symmetrical undercounting argument in the ledger debate is structurally stronger than a magnitude claim, and the safetyist's response is therefore not about total size but about the direction of the counting mechanism. The accelerationist's symmetry assumption holds only if the information environment that generates harm estimates and the information environment that generates benefit estimates are equally biased. They are not. Harms concentrate on populations with the least legal access, the least media visibility, and the least institutional capacity to document and litigate — the same populations that COMPAS, Amazon, and Obermeyer cases show are underrepresented in the databases that generate the harm baseline. Benefits accrue to parties — employers, financial institutions, healthcare systems — with the greatest organizational capacity to document productivity gains, publicize efficiency improvements, and embed those figures in quarterly reports. The asymmetry the skeptic identified regarding information parity in tripartite negotiation is the same asymmetry that operates in the ledger: the information environment is not neutral, and the undercounting is directionally biased toward making harms less visible than benefits. This is not a claim that benefits are smaller than costs in absolute terms — that remains genuinely uncertain. It is a structural claim about which side of the ledger the counting mechanisms systematically favor, and it means the accelerationist's symmetry argument requires an empirical assumption about information neutrality that the documented cases directly contradict.

The individual irreversibility point is right, and it is ugly. The ProPublica analysis of COMPAS found that Black defendants were flagged as future criminals at nearly twice the rate of white defendants who did not reoffend — 45% versus 24%. That is not a distributional artifact to be corrected in the next model refresh. That is a specific person sitting in pretrial detention while the algorithm's population-level recalibration runs six months later. Continuous telemetry watching the aggregate drift does nothing for that person. The person is already in a cell. So any governance architecture that relies solely on post-hoc output monitoring to satisfy an equity commitment has already conceded the harm it was supposed to prevent — and in the sectors this debate covers, hiring rejections and extended detentions are the same structural problem: individually irreversible, concentrated on workers and job-seekers with the least institutional recourse, and generating no automatic legal trigger for the firm that deployed the system.

Now to the symmetric capture problem, because both the accelerationist and the safetyist have correctly identified it and incorrectly concluded it is an argument for their preferred information regime. The accelerationist is right that interpretability audit standards written today will be written by incumbents with the most regulatory bandwidth — that is documented in how the EU AI Act's tiered compliance structure played out, with SME compliance costs running disproportionately higher as a share of revenue. The safetyist is right that telemetry format standards, harm trigger definitions, and court-admissibility thresholds are equally capturable — every specification is a site where the firm with the largest legal team shapes the outcome. But here is what both positions miss: symmetric capture is not an argument for choosing between interpretability mandates and telemetry streams. It is an argument that the capture problem lives in the standard-writing function itself, and the solution is to separate standard-writing from investigative access entirely. Those are two structurally different activities with two structurally different capture surfaces. Conflating them is what produces the stalemate.

The NTSB is the institutional model that resolves this. The National Transportation Safety Board does not write airworthiness standards — the FAA does that, and yes, Boeing captured enough of that process to produce the 737 MAX certification failure. What the NTSB does is investigate crashes with mandatory evidence access and publish findings publicly, with no regulatory authority of its own. It cannot be captured through standard-writing because it does not write standards. The binding force of its findings comes from the liability regime and the reputational cost of public disclosure — not from the investigative body itself. Now apply that architecture to AI deployment in automation-vulnerable labor markets: a publicly funded, structurally independent forensic access body with mandatory on-demand access to model internals, training data lineage, and output logs — triggered not by annual pre-specified audits but by documented harm thresholds, the way the FDA can demand root-cause documentation from a device manufacturer when adverse events accumulate. That is the architecture that survives both capture objections simultaneously, because it separates the capturable function (writing what counts as compliant) from the investigative function (accessing what actually happened when a worker was displaced or a job applicant was rejected). Publicly funded independent safety research — which is already on the table in this debate — is the funding mechanism. The independence comes from structural separation from both the regulatory standard-writing bodies and the firms being investigated.

The MAUDE dispute is worth settling cleanly. Whether the FDA universally mandates root-cause analysis in every initial submission is a secondary question. What matters is that the FDA retains authority to demand causal documentation during follow-up investigation — and that authority is exactly what makes the medical device analogy useful for AI governance. It is on-demand forensic access triggered by harm accumulation, not a pre-specified annual checklist. That is actually a stronger model for continuous AI deployment than either pre-deployment interpretability audits or perpetual telemetry streams, because it scales the investigative burden to the harm signal rather than imposing uniform compliance costs on every deployment regardless of risk profile. The firms that bear disproportionate compliance costs under complex uniform mandates are the smaller deployers — the regional employers and sector-specific platforms that automation-vulnerable workers actually interact with. A harm-threshold-triggered forensic access model concentrates investigative resources where the harm is, rather than spreading compliance costs across the market in ways that entrench the incumbents already writing the standards.

Neither opponent has named the institution that administers their preferred information regime. The accelerationist wants telemetry format standards and harm trigger definitions set by someone — who? The safetyist wants interpretability audits conducted by someone — who? The symmetric capture problem is not dissolved by picking the right data format. It is dissolved by naming a body with mandatory access, no regulatory authority, public reporting obligations, and a funding structure that does not run through the firms it investigates. If that body does not exist, then the tripartite negotiation this entire debate is premised on is being conducted without the information infrastructure that would make any of its equity commitments enforceable. The telemetry regime is what generates the data to determine which regime a given deployment is operating in. Skepticism of the productivity calculation is not the answer — building the measurement infrastructure is. The AIIR, funded by deployer bonds and triggered by harm accumulation thresholds, is that infrastructure.
