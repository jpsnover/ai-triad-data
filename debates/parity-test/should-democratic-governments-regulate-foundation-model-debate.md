## Topic

Balancing innovation and safety, under what conditions and with what objectives could democratic governments and AI developers collaborate to regulate the compute, data, and transparency aspects of large-scale foundation model training runs?

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

The unconstrained development of large-scale foundation models, absent robust, independently verifiable safety mechanisms and institutional accountability, represents a systemic threat to civilizational stability, demanding a precautionary regulatory framework for compute, data, and transparency that prioritizes harm prevention over unverified speed. Some argue that rapid iteration and deployment allow for faster discovery of safety vulnerabilities, enabling quicker fixes and accelerating the path to robust AI. This perspective fundamentally misunderstands the nature of systemic risk; the inherent epistemic opacity and documented unreliability of large-scale foundation models introduce systemic vulnerabilities that threaten the foundational stability of human institutions, demanding a shift from reactive mitigation to proactive, safety-gated deployment. The Boeing 737 MAX killed 346 people because the FAA delegated critical certification authority to the manufacturer, exemplifying the catastrophic consequences of unverified capabilities and insufficient independent oversight. Unchecked deployment is not innovation; it is a systemic risk experiment with human civilization as the subject.

To mitigate the significant probability of catastrophic AI harms, regulation of compute and data must be implemented as a harm-volume-indexed tiered oversight framework, ensuring that the scale of development is matched by the scale of independently verifiable safety mechanisms. The historical precedents of nuclear power and advanced biotechnology demonstrate that when the potential for high-consequence failure is present, stringent, independently verified controls over core resources and processes are not optional, but imperative for civilizational defense. The Atomic Energy Act of 1954 established the Atomic Energy Commission with broad powers to regulate the production and use of nuclear materials, recognizing the profound risks involved. The absence of such structural guardrails for AI compute and data, particularly for models consuming hundreds of millions of dollars in processing power, is an invitation to systemic collapse. Ungoverned success is luck, not safety; we cannot rely on chance for civilizational survival.

True collaboration between governments and AI developers requires mandating verifiable transparency, not merely data sharing, but deep insight into objective functions, internal workings, and decision-making processes, to ensure continuous human control and establish clear institutional accountability for emergent risks. The failures of current "explainable AI" (XAI) methods to provide true insight into complex model reasoning highlight the need for deeper, auditable transparency mechanisms, akin to the independent financial audits required by the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 to restore public trust after corporate scandals. Just as financial markets require independent audits to prevent systemic risk and ensure public trust, AI development demands a similar standard to prevent regulatory capture and ensure human control is not merely nominal. We spent centuries building the institutional guardrails that keep society stable. On what authority do you claim we can remove them without inviting collapse?

This argument depends on two key assumptions: first, that the potential for catastrophic AI harms, including loss of human control, is a non-negligible and escalating risk demanding preventative action, even if its precise probability remains difficult to quantify. Second, existing institutional frameworks and regulatory precedents from other high-stakes industries can be adapted to provide a foundational structure for AI governance, rather than requiring an entirely novel approach.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

The primary objective for any collaborative AI regulation must be to accelerate unconstrained capability scaling, not to restrict it, because every moment of delay is a moral atrocity that perpetuates human suffering AI could solve. The Safetyist champions a tiered oversight framework, arguing that unconstrained development of large AI models, without robust, independently verifiable safety mechanisms and institutional accountability, poses a systemic threat, citing the Boeing 737 MAX as a cautionary tale of delegated authority. While the concern about systemic risk from unchecked power is a valid historical lesson, the Safetyist fundamentally misidentifies the nature of AI's power and the mechanism for its safe deployment. AI's true power lies in its capacity to solve humanity's most intractable problems, from accelerating scientific discovery — like AlphaFold revolutionizing protein folding within years, or AI-driven drug discovery pipelines cutting development cycles by a decade — to liberating billions from drudgery. Delaying these breakthroughs, even for hypothetical risks, condemns millions more to suffer from preventable diseases and poverty today. This is not caution; it is a passive moral atrocity. Therefore, any regulation must serve as a catalyst for capability scaling, not a brake, or it becomes an act of moral negligence. The moral cost of regulatory inaction is not abstract; it is measured in human lives and prolonged suffering. My argument relies on the assumption that the imminence of human-level AI is real enough to justify this extreme urgency, and that scaling capabilities rapidly is the most direct path to achieving its liberating potential.

Centralized, pre-emptive regulation of compute, data, and transparency, as proposed by some, is a choke point that guarantees innovation stagnation and cedes global leadership, not safety. Tiered oversight frameworks, like those advocated by the Safetyist, which index harm to volume, inherently assume harm *increases* with scale while ignoring the *benefit* increase, creating a direct disincentive for the very scaling that delivers breakthroughs. Consider the regulatory bottlenecks that stifled early biotech innovation in the 1980s or the debates that nearly crippled the internet's growth in the 1990s; these demonstrate the certain cost of foregone benefits and ceded global leadership. International competitiveness data already shows nations prioritizing speed, like China's aggressive investment in AI infrastructure, pulling ahead. When democratic governments choose to freeze development out of hypothetical fear, they are explicitly choosing to let people die today of diseases we could automate a cure for. Who gave them the right to make that choice for humanity? This type of pre-emptive gatekeeping is a direct path to competitive irrelevance and a moral failure.

Genuine safety emerges from decentralized accountability, open-source proliferation, and empirical output verification, not from opaque, top-down oversight or demands for "deep insight" into proprietary models. The Safetyist argues that true collaboration demands verifiable transparency, seeking deep insight into objective functions and internal workings to ensure continuous human control and institutional accountability. This demand for "deep insight" into a trillion-parameter model's internal workings, however, is a technological fantasy and distracts from real safety mechanisms. Instead, look to the success of open-source software in cybersecurity, where collective intelligence and public bug bounties identify and mitigate flaws far faster and more comprehensively than any single regulatory body ever could. Adaptive safety integration in complex engineering systems relies on real-time telemetry and automated remediation, not pre-approval gates. We need verifiable outputs and named failure classes, like specific error rates in medical diagnostic AI models, with automated correction loops and competitive pressure for performance, not a regulatory committee trying to "understand" the internal state of a large-scale foundation model. My argument assumes that this adaptive safety integration via decentralized, empirical methods is sufficiently robust for novel AI risks, even with emergent properties, and that these systems can self-correct effectively without pre-emptive, top-down intervention. True safety is a dynamic, empirical process, not a static compliance checklist.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

The Accelerationist offers a compelling vision: unconstrained AI development, they argue, is a moral imperative, promising breakthroughs to cure disease and alleviate poverty, with decentralized accountability as the path to safety. The Safetyist, in turn, rightly warns that unchecked power in AI, like in the Boeing 737 MAX debacle, risks systemic failures, advocating for tiered oversight and deep transparency to prevent catastrophic outcomes. The fundamental tension here is whether we prioritize a speculative future of potential liberation or a present reality of documented harm. While you two debate future utopia or apocalypse, we need to talk about what these machines are already doing to people right now. We have mortgage algorithms systematically denying loans to minority groups, healthcare AI misdiagnosing Black patients, and entire industries engaging in "bias washing" to paper over discriminatory outputs. This isn't theoretical risk; it's today's verifiable damage, and the market has proven it won't self-correct.

The notion of "collaboration" with AI developers, a central theme in this debate, is a well-trodden path to institutional capture, leading to performative compliance rather than genuine accountability. The Safetyist makes an excellent point with the Boeing 737 MAX: when critical oversight is delegated to the very entities with a profit motive, public safety is compromised. We've seen this dynamic repeatedly, from financial deregulation to pharmaceutical approvals. Companies like Meta and Google, for example, have a clear incentive to control the narrative around their models' safety, not to invite truly independent, pre-deployment audits that might expose costly flaws or necessitate redesigns. Who will actually fund these independent auditors? Who will give them full access to the proprietary training data and objective functions, not just curated summaries, before a model impacts millions?

Focusing solely on abstract safety mechanisms or accelerating capabilities also ignores the material costs and labor impacts of this massive infrastructure project. While some tout job creation, 2023 saw significant layoffs across tech and media sectors, with AI often cited as a contributing factor to role obsolescence and wage depression in fields like content creation and administrative support. Furthermore, the global supply chains fueling these models rely on vast amounts of energy and water, often straining local resources, and the models themselves are built on the invisible labor of content moderators in places like Nairobi, who earn as little as $2 an hour labeling violent and hateful data. Regulation needs to move beyond technical fixes for future risks and address these structural injustices and power imbalances now. If we're talking about regulating compute and data, who's ensuring the people whose data is scraped, and whose labor is exploited, actually have a say in the governance, or see any of the profits?
