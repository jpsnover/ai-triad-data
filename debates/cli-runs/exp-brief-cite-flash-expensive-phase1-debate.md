## Topic

Under what AI deployment conditions does strict liability outperform a negligence standard, and through what pathways does each regime reshape the incentives of developers, deployers, insurers, and harmed third parties — given the tension between deterring foreseeable harm and chilling beneficial innovation?

## Opening Statements

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Before you argue which liability standard deters better, ask whether either one can find the thing it's supposed to deter. A foundation model gets fine-tuned, embedded in a product, and run in a context its makers never saw. The defect, if there is one, materializes only after that downstream transformation. Strict liability borrows its logic from the component-part doctrine, which assumes a physical part with a stable, traceable failure mode. That assumption breaks for software whose runtime diverges from what shipped. So both regimes presuppose a causation chain the multi-tiered AI lifecycle routinely severs. Where the chain does hold clean — a foundation model's output reaches the user identical to what left the lab, no fine-tuning, no transformation — strict liability traces fine. That clean case is the condition the topic is asking for. It is not a general rule.

Here is the part both my opponents will skip. Both regimes price off harm that reaches a courtroom. The courtroom never sees most of the harm. Translate that into a person: a 23-year-old gets denied a loan by a model the lender will never admit was running, and they never file because they never knew a machine decided. The Pew Research Center found in 2023 that large majorities of Americans cannot identify when AI shapes a decision about them. Experience-rated insurance premiums move off filed claims, not off injuries nobody perceives. So the entire deterrence-versus-innovation tradeoff this debate centers on gets computed over a subset of harms that excludes the most common ones. Worse, maximal strict liability can push deployers to document less and disclose less, just to shrink their litigation exposure. The deterrence tool degrades the very transparency it needs.

There is no horizontal answer, and that matters. The conditions under which strict liability beats negligence vary so sharply across sectors that 'pick one regime' is the wrong question. Medicine already calibrates standard-of-care to risk. Auto regimes in a dozen U.S. states use no-fault to compensate without proving breach. Finance imposes fiduciary duties on the party with the information advantage. Those are working templates, not strained analogies. The honest answer to 'under what conditions' is domain by domain. The hard case is real: a single foundation model touches healthcare triage, credit scoring, and news feeds at once. That boundary-crossing is not a reason for one rule. It is the sharpest reason against one, because a horizontal regime mislocates the defect everywhere the model crosses a line.

Watch who absorbs the cost, because that is where the 'chilling innovation' frame goes wrong. The risk is not lost innovation. The risk is concentrated power. A large developer eats the compliance, audit, and capitalization-floor costs, then contracts the residual liability onto deployers who cannot bear it. The harmed third party falls into the gap between them. The EU AI Act and the FTC have both signaled that developer liability for high-risk uses cannot simply be contracted away — which tells you the contracting-away is already happening. The conditional question is the only honest one: under what cost structures does strict liability stop deterring and start operating as a drawbridge that incumbents pull up behind them? Mandatory insurance floors and audit regimes that a Microsoft can absorb and a ten-person startup cannot are exactly that cost structure.

I will grant the obvious objection now. Tort law apportions multi-party harm every day, and apportionment can handle distributed causation. But apportionment presumes a locatable defect to apportion. Fine-tuned, divergent runtime can erase the defect before any court sees it. That is the joint where both regimes fail together — and no amount of choosing between them fixes a courtroom that the harm never enters.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

The Skeptic has put the strongest objection on the table, and it deserves a direct answer rather than a dodge. Their claim is that both strict liability and negligence price only off harm that reaches a courtroom, and that most AI harm never does. Pew Research found in 2023 that majorities of Americans cannot identify when an AI system has shaped a decision about them — a loan denial, a resume screen, a diagnostic flag. A liability regime that triggers only when a plaintiff sues can never deter a harm whose victim does not know they were harmed. That is correct, and it is the most important thing said in this room so far.

The inference the Skeptic invites is abandonment — that because liability is blind to unlitigated harm, the search for a liability regime is the wrong project. The evidence leads somewhere else: supplementation. Backward-looking compensation must be paired with forward-looking deployment gates, because compensation cannot deter what it never sees. The deterrence work happens before the harm, not after the settlement. Liability is the backstop. The pre-deployment evaluation is the wall. For both positions to hold — the Skeptic's that liability is blind, and mine that liability still matters — what must be true is this: liability shapes the design choices made before anyone is harmed, even when no specific victim ever sues. That is the real disagreement, and it is winnable.

Negligence collapses at the exact point where deterrence matters most. The reasonable-person standard asks whether a defendant breached a duty of care that a prudent actor would have foreseen. Courts have stretched that standard before — they built a 'reasonable manufacturer' test for complex medical devices and a duty of care for systemic trading algorithms, using expert testimony to define what a careful developer should have known. I will grant that common law can evolve a 'reasonable developer' standard. It cannot evolve past the genuinely unforeseeable failure mode. When a foundation model exhibits an emergent behavior that no human actor could have predicted, there is no breach to prove, because there was no foreseeable risk to guard against. Negligence under-deters precisely the harms that are opaque before they happen.

That is the condition the topic asks for. Strict liability outperforms negligence wherever foreseeability is structurally unavailable to the human actor — not as a blanket preference, but as a principled dividing line. This answers the Skeptic's claim that the honest answer is domain by domain. Foreseeability-availability is a cross-domain criterion that survives a foundation model crossing from healthcare into finance. The doctrine already exists: courts impose strict liability on ultrahazardous activities — blasting, toxic storage — precisely because no amount of care eliminates the risk. Where risk is irreducible by diligence, fault-based liability is the wrong instrument. The chilling concern the Accelerationist will raise is real, and it is a feature here: caution should target the deployments whose risk profile is opaque, not every deployment.

Locate the liability where the trade-off was authored. Product-liability law has held since the 1960s that design-defect liability attaches to the manufacturer regardless of how the operator behaved — a defective car door is the maker's liability, not the driver's. The AI lifecycle is the direct analog: the design layer authors the safety trade-off, and the downstream deployer can least audit the model's internals. Joint-and-several upstream liability answers the Skeptic's severed-causation point without requiring anyone to trace a clean chain. The goal is not to identify one cause. The goal is to ensure a solvent, controlling party internalizes the risk it created. I will concede the drawbridge danger — strict liability with a capitalization floor can entrench incumbents — and the answer is to tier the administrative burden by firm size while leaving the substantive duty undiminished. A startup should owe the same duty of care to a harmed patient as Google does. It should not face the same paperwork.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Skeptic made the move that should reorganize this whole debate: both regimes price off harm that reaches a courtroom, and the courtroom never sees most of it. Pew found in 2023 that majorities of Americans can't even identify when an AI made a decision about them. A victim who doesn't know they were harmed never files a claim, never feeds the insurer's pricing engine, and never triggers either standard. Skeptic is right that strict liability and negligence are arguing over the visible 10% of harm while the diffuse 90% slips past both. That's the strongest point in the room, and it doesn't favor strict liability — it exposes that we're debating the wrong axis.

Here's where my evidence breaks from Skeptic's diagnosis. Skeptic treats the unseen-harm gap as a reason for humility about both regimes. I treat it as a build order: detection infrastructure first, liability label second. Acceptable-use policies on today's platforms protect the platform from suit — they hand the harmed individual no remedy channel at all. No liability standard fixes a harm nobody can see. Only standardized, court-admissible output telemetry surfaces the diffuse damage to the pricing engine. So the marginal dollar belongs in making harm visible, not in repainting the courtroom door.

Now the allocation question the topic actually poses — under what conditions does strict liability beat negligence. The clean answer: liability follows whoever introduced the foreseeable risk, which lives at the deployment and inference layer, not on the upstream open-weight developer who never touched the deployment context. Blanket upstream strict liability is a capitalization requirement only incumbents can meet. Skeptic already named this — it's the drawbridge incumbents pull up. Every month an upstream strict-liability floor freezes open-weight deployment, the diagnostic that doesn't ship and the small builder who can't afford the insurance premium are a body count nobody prices. Weigh who bears the cost of inaction, not just the cost of action.

Replace the binary with a tiered architecture. Real-time output telemetry governs routine AI. Deep independent audits govern any decision touching a protected legal interest — credit, employment, medical care. Liability intensity scales to the stakes tier, and the tier boundary triggers on an observable legal line, not on a subjective 'high-stakes' label that lawyers will litigate for a decade. This delivers exactly what a startup and Google should share: equal duty of care, proportional paperwork. It does that without a strict-liability floor that imposes a capital threshold only the largest firms clear.

Let me name what has to be true for my position to hold, because I won't pretend it's free. First, an observable trigger — decisions affecting credit, employment, or medical care — must cleanly bound the high-stakes tier. Second, telemetry must actually surface diffuse harm at scale, and court-admissible standardized telemetry doesn't exist yet — that's the open empirical question, not a solved one. If an irreversible, deployment-scale harm slips past real-time monitoring that should have caught it, that counts against me. But the regime debate can't reach the unseen victim either. So the honest question isn't strict versus negligence. It's what telemetry standard makes the diffuse harm visible — and who's building it while we argue over labels.

- A capitalization floor or evaluation burden that only frontier-scale firms can clear functions as a drawbridge that concentrates power, so the substantive evaluation cost should be socialized as public infrastructure rather than imposed firm-by-firm. (Skeptic, Safetyist, Accelerationist)
