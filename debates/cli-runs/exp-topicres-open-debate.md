## Topic

Under what conditions would governance tools like mandatory pre-deployment evaluations, compute thresholds, and liability regimes actually reduce catastrophic AI risk without ceding the frontier to less cautious foreign or private actors? Given this tension between safety and competitiveness, how should the burden fall across regulators, frontier labs, and independent safety researchers?

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Governance instruments reduce catastrophic risk when they gate deployment before harm becomes irreversible — and they fail when they arrive after. The FDA does not recall a drug after it kills; it withholds approval until the maker demonstrates safety. The FAA does not certify an aircraft after it crashes. We built these gates because high-consequence industries taught us a hard lesson: ungoverned success is luck, not safety. The question before this committee is not whether to have gates. It is where the irreversibility of the harm warrants them.

Draw the line precisely. For most AI failures — a biased loan model, a bad translation — post-hoc correction works, and heavy gating would be waste. But a narrow class of frontier capabilities cannot be recalled once released: self-propagating code, weights that diffuse across servers, capabilities that copy faster than any regulator can respond. The Boeing 737 MAX killed 346 people because the FAA delegated certification to Boeing itself. The failure was not weak engineering. It was the absence of an independent gate before the aircraft flew. Apply that lesson to model weights that cannot be un-released, and the case for a staged-deployment floor — with a working rollback and a named authority to trigger it — writes itself.

Place the burden where the causation is tractable and the profit accrues. Frontier labs capture the gains from deployment, so frontier labs should carry the burden of demonstrating safety before release — not regulators reconstructing blame afterward. Attach liability to the decisions we can actually trace: the choice to deploy, and the capability claims a lab discloses. Regulators need precautionary immunity, because today the incentives are backward. A halted deployment produces one angry company and a visible cost. An un-halted catastrophe diffuses blame across a dozen actors and no one loses their job. FAA airworthiness directives show a regulator can ground a fleet without freezing an industry.

The competitiveness fear is real, and I will not wave it away. Crude compute bans risk entrenching incumbents and pushing less cautious development offshore — a landscape harder to govern, not easier. So the answer is not prohibition. It is graduated transparency-plus-standards keyed to the compute chokepoints where actors are already identifiable. Gates only work when tied to substantive outcomes — adversarial red-teaming against measurable thresholds — not process checkboxes that decay into compliance theater. And here is the reframe the accelerationist must answer: uncoordinated racing is itself the frontier-ceding risk. No single lab can slow down without losing position. Binding coordination is the condition under which safety and speed align rather than oppose.

I owe you two honest assumptions. First, my irreversibility claim holds only for that narrow harm class — I carry catastrophe as a precautionary bet on the unrecoverable, not a confident forecast. Second, concentrating the off-switch creates its own danger: incumbent lock-in, authoritarian capture. That cost is real. The answer is independent evaluators and public accountability, not a single hand on the lever. To the labs that want speed, I ask the only question that matters: what is your rollback plan, and who has the authority to trigger it? If the answer is silence, you have not built safety. You have gambled with systems we cannot recall.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Safetyist made the strongest possible case for gating deployment before harm goes irreversible, and the 737 MAX example lands. The FAA let Boeing certify its own MCAS software, and 346 people died in Lion Air 610 and Ethiopian 302. That is not a story about bad safety culture. It is a story about who held the clipboard. When the regulated entity signs its own certificate, you get paperwork, not oversight. Safetyist is right that a real gate has to exist before release.

Here is where the evidence pushes past that gate. Lab testing cannot surface harms that only appear when a model meets a million real users. Algorithmic discrimination in lending and hiring shows up in the field, at scale, months after the pre-deployment audit passed clean. So the burden question has a two-legged answer, not a one-legged one. Leg one is independent evaluation before release, decoupled from the developer, funded so it does not depend on the lab's goodwill. Leg two is strict post-deployment liability, so someone pays when the emergent harm shows up. Pharma, aviation, and nuclear all pair ex-ante certification with post-market liability for exactly this reason. Certification catches what is testable. Liability catches what deployment reveals. I will name the ugly part myself: proving causation through an opaque model is genuinely hard, which is precisely why you need both legs and not a bet on either one.

Now the compute threshold, which everyone treats as the clean enforcement lever. A number written as a legally binding trigger is an invitation to route around it. Developers have real latitude to keep the measured figure under the line while shipping the same capability: prune the training data, restructure the architecture, stage the release across runs. You get management of the number, not compliance with the intent. I am not going to inflate this into a body count. Documented cases of a frontier lab dodging a legal compute threshold at scale are thin, because these thresholds are new. So I put it forward as the thing the regime must be built to survive, the same way emissions rules had to survive Volkswagen's defeat devices in 2015. The fix is capability-based evaluation that measures what the model can do, not how many chips it burned.

The phrase 'ceding the frontier to less cautious actors' is doing work for somebody. Whatever anyone intends, compliance-cost-heavy gating favors scale. The lab with a legal department and a compliance team absorbs the cost. The three-person startup does not. Regulatory economics has been unambiguous on this since the 1970s: fixed compliance costs entrench incumbents. So the real axis in this debate is not safety versus competitiveness. It is safety versus capture. A regime that only asks 'is it safe' and never asks 'who does this consolidate power for' fails the second test by design. I am engaging the safety case on its merits, not dismissing it by who funds it. But an audit a lab self-reports, and a threshold a lab can restructure around, produce the appearance of oversight while entrenching the exact actors they claim to constrain.

So I will put the question to both of you plainly. Who holds the clipboard, who funds them so they cannot be starved, and who writes the check when the harm shows up eighteen months after a clean audit? Answer that, and we are talking about governance. Leave it unanswered, and we are talking about theater with a compliance budget.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Skeptic is right about the compute threshold, and I want to push the point further than they did. Skeptic argued that a legally binding compute trigger invites gaming — you get management of the number, not compliance with the intent. That diagnosis is correct, and the ground is already shifting under it. Distillation and mixture-of-experts architectures now decouple capability from raw FLOP count — a model trained under any fixed threshold can match a larger one at inference. The number you're regulating is drifting away from the danger you claim to fear. A compute cap doesn't buy safety here. It buys a pay-to-play barrier that only the firms with the biggest existing clusters can clear.

Safetyist makes the strongest case in the room, and it deserves to be stated plainly: some harms are irreversible, and once a frontier system ships, you cannot un-ship it. Gating deployment before that point is the only move that works for the truly irreversible tail. I accept that. What I don't accept is that pre-release demonstration is the right primary mechanism for the large class of harms that only crystallize under real-world load. The 737 MAX shows self-certification produces the appearance of oversight while entrenching the certified. Lab testing structurally cannot reach harms that surface only at the inference layer, at scale, against live adversaries. So match oversight to stakes: independent pre-deployment gates for the irreversible tail, mandatory live telemetry plus post-deployment accountability for everything else.

Here is the honest limit of my own position, stated before anyone forces it. Low-frequency catastrophic harms may never trip a telemetry threshold — a one-shot irreversible event doesn't generate a warning curve. That's precisely why I keep independent pre-deployment gating for the irreversible tail rather than pretending monitoring covers it. My falsification bet is specific: name the harm class that is both irreversible AND invisible to live monitoring until it fires. If that class is broad, I lose. If it's narrow, the gating burden should be narrow too — and the rest should run through instruments that a startup can survive.

Liability is that instrument, and it's the one the accelerationist camp will affirm without flinching. Product-liability law already scales burden to realized harm in pharma and auto — uncapped exposure for the domains where recklessness kills, not a fixed compliance toll paid upfront. That structure preserves entry. A two-person team can carry insurance against a harm they might cause; they cannot carry an 18-month audit backlog before they ship anything. But strict liability has a failure mode I won't hide: it can reward capability-hiding, because a firm that discloses less faces less proven fault. So pair it with a disclosure safe harbor — reduced exposure for labs that publish evaluations and telemetry. The design question the debate must answer is this: under what disclosure terms does liability deter irreversible harm without teaching every lab to go dark?

One cost nobody at this table has priced yet — the cost of the gate itself. Every month a working diagnostic model sits behind an 18-month approval queue, people who would have been screened are not screened. That toll is real, it's unmeasured, and it's borne by patients with no seat at the regulatory table. Skeptic named capture as the real axis, and I'll take that ground: a mis-specified compute cap protects the incumbents it claims to constrain. Safetyist is defending the irreversible tail, and that tail is worth defending. So let's defend exactly that tail — and stop taxing the ninety-five percent of deployment that heals people faster than any committee can convene.
