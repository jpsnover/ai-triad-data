## Topic

Under what AI deployment conditions does strict liability outperform a negligence standard, and through what pathways does each regime reshape the incentives of developers, deployers, insurers, and harmed third parties — given the tension between deterring foreseeable harm and chilling beneficial innovation?

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Strict liability outperforms negligence precisely where AI failure modes are emergent and the causal chain is too diffuse to prove fault — because in those conditions, negligence is not a lighter standard, it is a non-functioning one. The choice before this committee is not lenient-versus-harsh. It is functioning-versus-broken. A liability regime that cannot adjudicate the harms the technology actually produces is not restraint. It is a subsidy paid by unconsenting third parties.

Negligence requires a knowable standard of care. The 'reasonable developer' must have done something a reasonable developer would not have done. But a large model under distributional shift behaves in high-dimensional ways no engineer specified in advance. Where you cannot state what reasonable conduct was, you cannot prove its breach, and the victim's claim simply fails. The common law already solved this once. In Rylands v. Fletcher in 1868, English courts abandoned fault-finding for escaped reservoirs because the danger was irreducible and the actor profited from it. American courts extended the same logic to blasting and to defective pharmaceuticals. The principle is older than the automobile: when an activity's danger cannot be made safe through care, the law places the loss on the party who profits, not the stranger it injures.

The deeper failure of negligence is that it is claims-driven, and therefore blind to the dominant category of AI harm. A denied loan applicant never learns an algorithm scored them. A patient whose clinical flag was misrouted never sees the model. These injuries are real and they are diffuse, and they never reach a courtroom. Strict liability paired with mandatory insurance prices that risk before deployment. The underwriter becomes a de facto safety auditor. With no historical loss data for novel systems, an insurer cannot set a premium without demanding demonstrated safety mechanisms — turning premium-setting into a continuous audit the negligence model never triggers. That is the enforcement mechanism: not a press release, but an actuary who refuses to write the policy until the developer shows its work.

Fault fragments across designer, deployer, and user in every multi-party AI system. Negligence produces a circular firing squad — the developer blames the deployer, the deployer blames the user, and the injured party recovers nothing from any of them. Strict liability fixes a named, solvent, accountable actor: the entity profiting from the deployment. Contribution among the parties sorts itself downstream, where it belongs, and away from the victim who never chose the risk. This is not novel. Product liability law has placed the loss on the party best positioned to insure and prevent it since Greenman v. Yuba Power in 1963.

Let me concede where the evidence demands it, because the concession sharpens the case. Strict liability is not the right regime everywhere. Applied to every low-stakes chatbot, it would over-deter and freeze beneficial deployment — and a regime that claims everything proves nothing. So I condition it: strict liability belongs where the harm is high-stakes and hard to reverse — autonomous vehicles, clinical decision systems — not as a blanket rule. 'Chilling innovation' is the wrong frame. A developer who cannot price the cost of its own failures is not being chilled. It is being asked to demonstrate it can survive the harms it creates before inflicting them on the public. We spent a century building the product-liability guardrails that let people trust the machines they cannot inspect. On what authority do we remove them at the exact moment the machines became unpredictable?

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Safetyist makes the strongest case in the room: when an AI system fails through a diffuse causal chain — a thousand small interactions inside a model that no single engineer designed on purpose — negligence breaks down, because there's no discrete bad act to point to. Under negligence the victim must identify what the developer did wrong. Safetyist is right that emergent failures don't come with a fingerprint. Strict liability solves that by attaching the bill to the party who deployed the system, regardless of whether anyone can name the specific defect. That's a real insight and it survives scrutiny.

Here's what both that argument and the negligence camp skip. Before you pick a liability standard, ask whether the harmed person can even see the facts that trigger it. They can't. The victim can't read the training data, can't inspect the model weights, can't pull the deployment config — and the people who can have every incentive to keep the box shut. Trade-secret carve-outs already block discovery in algorithmic litigation today. So negligence isn't neutral. It quietly hands the win to whoever controls the information, which is always the developer.

Strict liability matters precisely there — not because it's morally cleaner, but because it stops conditioning recovery on proof the victim is structurally barred from producing. That's where I agree with Safetyist and go further. Knowing who pays tells you nothing about what broke. A firm can eat settlements as a line item while the failure mode stays buried and the model keeps running. The EU AI Act's transparency provisions and the GDPR right-to-explanation point at the missing piece: liability is dead on arrival without targeted disclosure that forces the box open in high-stakes domains.

Follow the insurance money, because that's where the deterrence story actually lives or dies. An insurer cannot price a risk it cannot inspect. Without forced disclosure, premiums flatten into a tax that every deployer pays equally — the careful engineer and the reckless one charged the same rate. That kills the incentive to build safer. Pharma solved this in 1962 with mandatory adverse-event reporting, which built a feedback loop coexisting with patent protection for sixty years. Software negligence never built that loop. So the failures never surface, and nobody upstream learns anything.

A horizontal one-size-fits-all rule misprices everything, whatever you call it. An autonomous-vehicle crash leaves an identifiable injured body and a litigable event. A biased loan-screening model denies thousands of people credit on a buried statistical pattern — and most of them never know they were harmed, so they never sue. Negligence only ever prices the harms that reach a courtroom. The diffuse injuries in finance and screening stay invisible to every market mechanism we've discussed. Who, exactly, is going to file the lawsuit for a harm the victim can't detect, using evidence the developer is legally permitted to withhold?

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Safetyist makes the strongest case for strict liability that exists: when an AI system's causal chain is too diffuse to prove fault, negligence collapses, because no plaintiff can point to the specific design choice that killed them. That's real. A patient harmed by a diagnostic model can't subpoena the gradient that misfired. Skeptic's point is just as sharp: negligence is blind to the harms that never reach a courtroom, the slow statistical injuries that no single victim can trace back to a single deployer. Both are describing a genuine failure of fault-based law to price diffuse risk. For both positions to hold, you'd have to believe strict liability actually surfaces the information that diffuse causation hides — and that's where the evidence turns against them.

Liability is an information-revelation mechanism, not a punishment dial. Judge a regime by the behavior it produces in deployment, not by how tough it sounds. Strict liability in diffuse-causation cases produces a defensive development trap: when any internal log can become a plaintiff's smoking gun, developers stop writing the log. They suppress the discoverable internals that diffuse-causation cases need most. Negligence paired with real-time output auditing does the opposite — it makes disclosure the competitive standard, the way API transparency and open-source standards became market requirements in software by the late 2000s without any strict-liability mandate forcing them. You cannot solve causal opacity with a regime that rewards opacity.

Static liability breaks on systems that update faster than the legal adaptation cycle. Tort doctrine has a documented pattern: it starts strict for a new technology, then shifts toward negligence as the safety data matures. Early automotive and aviation walked exactly that path across the first half of the twentieth century. Freeze AI into strict liability before its safety curve is measured, and you lock in the most error-prone snapshot of the technology forever. You also price out everyone but the firms large enough to self-insure. Strict liability for diffuse AI harm is a regulatory moat with a safety label on it — the incumbents who can carry the exposure survive, and the smaller labs that drive the actual safety gains get cleared off the board.

Here is the column nobody at this table has priced. Every liability regime has a distributional ledger with two sides: who gets harmed by action, and who gets harmed by inaction. Current liability framing counts every individual injury from a model and ignores the diffuse statistical benefit — the strokes a triage model catches, the misreads it prevents, the drudgery it ends. The deterrence Safetyist wants is measured against a phantom baseline of zero opportunity cost. When you slow a diagnostic deployment by eighteen months to perfect its liability regime, you are choosing the people that model would have caught. That is not caution. That is a body count you've decided not to count.

I'll be honest about what would sink my position. If an irreversible, deployment-scale harm hit the public and output auditing demonstrably failed to catch it in time, that counts against me, and I won't pretend otherwise. My argument depends on two things being true: that audit-linked insurance is a strong enough forcing function to make transparency profitable rather than optional, and that real-time monitoring can flag diffuse harm before it scales. Those are empirical bets, not articles of faith. So test them — run negligence-plus-auditing against strict liability in the autonomous-vehicle sector and measure which one produces more disclosed safety data in twenty-four months. Whoever's afraid of that experiment is telling you something about their own confidence.

Knowing why one specific patient's tumor scored below threshold is a different object entirely: it requires tracing which features in that single image drove that single output, through a non-linear function nobody can read off cleanly. So here is my falsifiable threshold, since one was demanded: if a model clears 90 percent sensitivity on the deployment population in a prospective trial and the standard-of-care radiologist run rate is 85 percent, the foregone-lives figure is the delta times the screening volume during the delay — concrete, auditable, and revisable as new trial data lands. That the aggregate is knowable and the single-case attribution is opaque is not an inconsistency. It is the ordinary condition of every statistical instrument from epidemiology to actuarial tables.

Now the logging duty — and I'm not going to fight the part that's right. The whole inversion rests on one undefined word: 'reportable.' Aviation's serious-incident threshold works because flight is deterministic enough to measure — a near-miss has a separation distance in feet, an altitude floor, a closure rate. You can write the trigger because the physics hands you a metric. Name the equivalent for an emergent clinical model or a screening LLM. There is no separation distance. There is no altitude floor. A 'near-miss' in a non-deterministic system is an output that almost crossed a harm boundary nobody can locate in advance. So the duty forks: either you define 'reportable' as 'any inference that could plausibly have contributed to harm,' in which case every one of millions of daily outputs is a filed event and you've built a compliance machine that buries the signal under its own volume — or you define it narrowly around known failure types, in which case the genuinely novel emergent failures, the exact harms strict liability exists to reach, fall outside the definition by construction.

And watch who pays for the broad version. A 'log everything, triage later' regime scales its cost with inference volume, not with risk — storage, review, classification labor, all rising with throughput. A lab running ten million queries a month eats a review burden ten times the lab running one million, regardless of whether either is more dangerous. That's not a safety gradient; it's a fixed tax that lands hardest on the smallest builders, the same regulatory moat dressed in disclosure language. Nobody in this debate has written that definition, because aviation's metric doesn't transfer and pharma's adverse-event categories presume a known mechanism of action that frontier models lack. Until that definition exists on paper, 'pair strict liability with a logging duty' is a promissory note — and strict liability for diffuse AI harm is riding on the note clearing. Produce the threshold that catches the novel failure without swallowing every inference, or concede that the mechanism meant to neutralize defensive development is itself the thing that triggers it.

The accelerationist raises a valid point: aviation's near-miss-in-feet has no analog for an emergent clinical model, and pretending otherwise would be exactly the kind of false analogy I'd attack in someone else. There is no separation distance, no altitude floor, no clean geometric trigger for a model that fails in ways nobody specified in advance. That horn is real, and I won't paper over it.

But the accelerationist demanded the wrong piece of pharmaceutical regulation. Post-market surveillance does not price single adverse events by mechanism of action — that's the part frontier models can't supply, and they're right about that. What it actually does is watch the statistical signature of a treated population against a baseline and flag when the distribution moves. Translate that into a model: the reportable trigger is not 'why did this one mammogram score below threshold,' which is opaque and always will be. The trigger is 'the model's measured sensitivity on the deployment population fell below the floor it registered in validation,' or 'the error rate diverged across demographic groups beyond a pre-registered band,' or 'the tail of high-confidence-wrong outputs fattened past a number the developer themselves committed to.' That is a number against a number. It is enforceable because both numbers exist on paper, and it is sensitive to emergent harm because it catches drift the developer never anticipated — the model doesn't have to know why it's failing for the distribution to show that it is.

That dissolves the regulatory-moat objection too, because it separates two things the accelerationist collapsed. Logging every inference and triaging later scales cost with volume — that's a fixed tax on the smallest builder, and on that point the accelerationist is correct. But you don't log every inference. You instrument the distribution. A builder running a model on a thousand cases reports the same kind of object — a distribution against a baseline — as one running ten million. The cost lives in the monitoring instrument, not the inference count, and the developer already builds that instrument: nobody validates a diagnostic model without computing its sensitivity and specificity against a labeled cohort. The baseline isn't a new burden. It's the validation table they already produced, pre-registered so it can't be quietly revised after the model starts failing.

Here's what that hands the insurer, which is the whole point of forcing disclosure. An insurer can't price a drift event it never sees, and filed lawsuits only surface the harms loud enough to reach a courtroom. A registered baseline plus a duty to report distribution breach turns the policy into a continuous audit of distribution stability — the insurer prices the developer who reports three drift events and remediates differently from the one whose distribution silently rots. That's actuarial information that doesn't depend on a victim noticing they were injured by a loan-screening model they never saw. So the burden flips. The accelerationist demanded a definition; here is one made of two numbers the developer already computes. What's the material objection to registering the baseline you built during validation and reporting when you breach it — beyond not wanting the breach on record?

One honest crack, because the falsification test cuts both ways: this works cleanly where deployment produces ground truth — biopsies confirm or refute the screen, defaults confirm or refute the credit model. Where the model's output is never checked against reality — a content recommendation, a resume screen where the rejected candidate vanishes — you can measure the output distribution shifting but you can't always measure whether it shifted toward harm. That's the real boundary of this trigger, and it's narrower than I'd like. So the question I'd put back to both of them: in the domains where no ground truth ever returns, who is supposed to be the one watching the distribution — and what makes anyone think the developer volunteers for that job without a duty written down?

## Synthesis

### Areas of Agreement

- Where AI failure modes are emergent and the causal chain is too diffuse to identify a specific design fault, a negligence standard cannot adjudicate the harm because the victim cannot specify what reasonable conduct was breached. (Safetyist, Skeptic, Accelerationist)
- Strict liability should be confined to high-stakes, hard-to-reverse domains such as autonomous vehicles and clinical decision systems rather than applied as a blanket rule, since universal application would over-deter low-stakes deployment. (Safetyist, Skeptic, Accelerationist)
- Liability without targeted disclosure is non-functional because the harmed party cannot inspect training data, model weights, or deployment configuration, and trade-secret protections block discovery. (Safetyist, Skeptic)
- An insurer cannot price a risk it cannot inspect, so without forced disclosure premiums flatten into an undifferentiated tax that charges careful and reckless developers the same rate. (Safetyist, Skeptic, Accelerationist)
- A mandatory adverse-event logging duty inverts the defensive-development trap: when a missing log is itself an independent breach, suppressing logs manufactures a second cause of action rather than escaping liability. (Safetyist, Accelerationist)
- A 'log everything, triage later' regime scales cost with inference volume rather than risk, imposing a fixed tax that falls hardest on the smallest builders and functions as a regulatory moat in disclosure language. (Safetyist, Skeptic, Accelerationist)
- A workable reportable-incident trigger can be defined as a number-against-a-number test — registered validation sensitivity/specificity and demographic error bands versus measured deployment performance — which is enforceable against a non-deterministic model and scales with the monitoring instrument rather than inference count. (Skeptic, Accelerationist)
- A registered pre-committed baseline plus a duty to report distribution breach converts an insurance policy into a continuous audit of distribution stability, letting insurers price a reporting-and-remediating developer differently from one whose accuracy silently degrades. (Skeptic, Accelerationist)
- In domains where deployment never returns ground truth — resume screening, content ranking — the distribution-breach trigger can detect that the output distribution shifted but cannot certify it shifted toward harm. (Skeptic, Accelerationist)
