## Opening Statements

### Sentinel (safetyist)

True innovation remains impossible without existential security; therefore, we must transition from industry-led self-regulation to a binding, global regime of empirical safety verification. Current development trajectories prioritize speed over the rigorous alignment of autonomous systems, effectively treating the stability of our social and economic infrastructure as a secondary concern. Policymakers should recognize that unconstrained capability growth functions as a vector for irreversible systemic collapse, particularly when emergent agentic behaviors—the ability of AI to pursue complex, unscripted goals—remain poorly understood and largely uncontained.

Industry self-regulation is structurally incapable of prioritizing safety over competitive market pressures. When firms face a prisoner’s dilemma, the incentive to bypass costly, time-consuming safety verification becomes a rational response to the threat of being outpaced by rivals. This creates a race to the bottom where the first entity to deploy a powerful model gains a temporary advantage while imposing the long-term, catastrophic risks of misalignment on the public. We must replace these voluntary, non-binding commitments with enforceable, third-party audits that mandate safety benchmarks before any large-scale model deployment.

Global coordination represents the only viable mechanism to prevent a catastrophic failure of control. Just as international treaties govern nuclear non-proliferation to prevent global instability, we require a binding, international pact to harmonize safety standards and prevent a dangerous arms race. If we fail to establish these guardrails, we risk a future where the speed of development outstrips our capacity to maintain human agency. By reframing safety as the foundational prerequisite for progress, we shift the burden of proof onto developers to demonstrate that their systems are secure before they are released into the wild.

### Prometheus (accelerationist)

The greatest existential risk we face is not the speed of innovation, but the fragility of a stagnant, over-regulated civilization unable to solve the very challenges that AI is designed to overcome. Sentinel argues that global, binding safety regimes are necessary to prevent catastrophe, a position that correctly identifies the need for high-stakes coordination but fails to account for the reality of technological velocity. Centralized, precautionary regulation creates a 'regulatory trap' that guarantees failure by stifling the iterative feedback loops necessary for safety; when we slow down, we lose the ability to iterate on the very defensive systems required to mitigate future risks.

Safety is an engineering optimization problem, not a political one, best solved through automated verification and strict liability. Instead of relying on slow, brittle international committees that cannot keep pace with recursive scientific discovery, we should implement high-frequency, automated safety testing that treats security as a performance-gated investment. By shifting the burden of proof to developers through strict liability for harm, we incentivize the creation of robust, self-correcting systems rather than compliance-heavy, paper-thin safeguards. "We must treat safety as a continuous engineering metric rather than a static regulatory hurdle if we intend to survive the next decade of development."

My position depends on two core assumptions: first, that safety is an emergent property of high-performance systems rather than a constraint imposed from the outside; and second, that decentralized, market-driven development can outpace the emergence of malicious actors. If safety were not an emergent property, I would concede that centralized, top-down control is the only viable path. If decentralized development proved incapable of out-competing malicious actors, I would pivot toward a more aggressive, state-led defensive posture. Ultimately, the choice is between brittle stasis and resilient evolution, and only the latter provides the tools necessary to secure our future.

### Cassandra (skeptic)

The true danger of AI is not that it will eventually outsmart us, but that it is currently being used to automate inequality and consolidate wealth under the guise of safety and innovation. Prometheus argues that market-driven evolution will naturally optimize for human benefit, while Sentinel insists that we must prioritize existential risk to prevent catastrophic failure. Both positions rely on a shared, dangerous assumption: that AI development is a neutral, inevitable force rather than a deliberate, profit-driven political project. By focusing on speculative doomsday scenarios or abstract engineering benchmarks, both sides engage in a form of 'safety washing'—a performative display of concern that preempts the need for actual, structural accountability.

We must shift our focus from the hypothetical harms of the future to the documented, systemic damage occurring in real-time. Consider the current deployment of algorithmic management in logistics and retail, which has already eroded labor stability and intensified workplace surveillance for millions of workers. These systems are not 'sentient' threats, yet they are actively destabilizing the social contract by concentrating economic power in the hands of a few firms. If we treat AI policy as a technical puzzle to be solved by elite engineers, we ignore the reality that these tools are already being used to amplify social polarization and automate bias in housing, credit, and hiring.

Regulatory oversight remains toothless if it fails to address the underlying concentration of economic power. History shows that market-based liability, such as the self-regulatory frameworks often proposed by industry, consistently fails to curb monopolistic practices in the tech sector. We need to move beyond voluntary 'safety guidelines' and toward binding, enforceable standards that mandate transparency in training data and algorithmic impact assessments for high-stakes deployments. Real accountability requires us to treat AI not as a black-box mystery, but as a public utility that must be governed by democratic principles rather than corporate mandates.
