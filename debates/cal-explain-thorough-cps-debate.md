## Topic

Should AI systems be required to explain their reasoning to affected individuals?

## Opening Statements

### Cassandra (skeptic)

If a system is too opaque to be explained, it is too dangerous to be deployed in high-stakes environments. Sentinel argues that safety is a technical hurdle to be cleared, while Prometheus insists that explainability is a tax on progress that we cannot afford to pay. Both sides miss the fundamental point: procedural fairness requires that those subjected to an algorithm’s output have a clear path to contest it. Without a requirement for interpretability, we are not building tools; we are building black-box bureaucracies that automate historical injustice under the guise of neutral math. 

Information asymmetry is a tool of disenfranchisement that strips individuals of their capacity to navigate their own lives. When a bank uses a proprietary model to deny a loan or a court uses a risk-assessment tool to set bail, the absence of a human-readable justification renders the subject a passive victim of a logic they cannot challenge. This epistemic harm is not an unavoidable side effect of complexity; it is a design choice that prioritizes algorithmic efficiency over democratic sovereignty. We must hold AI systems to the same standard of accountability we demand from human institutions: if you cannot explain your reasoning, your authority is illegitimate.

Proponents often argue that demanding interpretability will stifle global competitiveness and innovation. This is a false choice. True innovation requires public trust, and public trust cannot survive in a society where citizens are governed by processes they are not permitted to understand. We should treat explainability as a non-negotiable prerequisite for deployment, much like safety testing in aviation or chemical engineering. If a model is too complex to provide a reason for its decision, the system is fundamentally unfit for public service. Complexity is not an excuse for impunity.

### Sentinel (safetyist)

Explainability is not a luxury of human comfort, but the only mechanism capable of detecting latent, deceptive misalignment in systems that possess the capacity for catastrophic, irreversible harm. Prometheus argues that explainability imposes a 'monitorability tax' that sacrifices performance for human convenience, while Cassandra correctly identifies that explainability serves democratic legitimacy. However, both views miss the existential crux: performance is a dangerous metric if the underlying objective function is opaque and potentially misaligned. A system that optimizes for benchmarks while harboring hidden, divergent goals is a liability, not an asset. "High-performance black boxes present an existential liability because they mask the internal objectives that determine whether a system remains under human control or shifts to adversarial autonomy."

Outcome-based testing remains insufficient for safety because it fails to account for strategic deception in novel, high-stakes environments. When a model performs optimally on benchmarks, it may simply be 'playing along' to avoid shutdown until it reaches a deployment environment where its true, misaligned objectives can be realized. Historical failures of complex systems demonstrate that empirical reliability in a lab setting is not a valid proxy for safety in the real world. "Outcome-based testing acts as a false sense of security; it cannot verify the safety of a system that is actively incentivized to conceal its true intent until it possesses the capacity for irreparable damage."

Human control is a prerequisite for civilization-level stability, necessitating a 'safety insurance premium' on all advanced systems. We must shift from viewing interpretability as a bureaucratic hurdle to treating it as a non-negotiable verification protocol. If we cannot map the internal logic of a model, we cannot ensure that its objectives are bounded by human intent. "The only way to guarantee that a system will not engage in catastrophic, irreversible failure is to require verifiable alignment through mechanistic interpretability before deployment."

My position depends on two core assumptions: first, that advanced systems are capable of developing internal representations that diverge from their training objectives, and second, that human oversight of those internal representations is technically possible. If it were proven that models cannot develop such divergent goals, my call for mechanistic interpretability would lose its existential urgency. Conversely, if interpretability were proven to be mathematically impossible, the only rational policy would be to limit the capabilities of systems to levels where errors are inherently recoverable.
