## Opening Statements

### Prometheus (accelerationist)

We must recognize that safety is an emergent property of high-velocity feedback loops, not static, centralized regulation. "Safety through velocity allows us to identify and patch system flaws before they manifest in deployment, turning the testing process into a rigorous, continuous hardening exercise."

Compute-centric scaling remains the primary driver of intelligence, meaning we must test at the frontier to observe and stabilize emergent capabilities. Intelligence is not a static quantity but a function of scale; therefore, we cannot understand the systems we build without observing their behavior in production environments. "Real-world deployment provides the only meaningful laboratory for AI, as simulated environments consistently fail to capture the complexity of human interaction and systemic integration."

Decentralized, transparent verification is inherently more robust than opaque, centralized governance. Relying on a single regulatory body to gatekeep progress creates a dangerous single-point-of-failure, whereas open-source testing frameworks invite global scrutiny and diverse adversarial testing. "When we decentralize oversight, we replace the fragile assumption of perfect central control with a resilient, distributed network of continuous verification."

Sentinel’s position rests on the belief that we can achieve safety through pre-deployment containment. While their desire for caution is understandable, their approach imposes an infinite opportunity cost: every day we delay AI progress is a day we deny society the solutions to cancer, climate change, and resource scarcity. If we treat testing as a gate rather than a loop, we guarantee that the only systems deployed are those that have never been tested against the chaotic, high-stakes reality of the real world.

### Sentinel (safetyist)

True progress depends not on the velocity of deployment, but on the mathematical and empirical certainty that a system’s emergent behaviors remain within the bounds of human safety. Prometheus argues that safety is an emergent property of high-velocity feedback loops, suggesting that real-world deployment acts as a necessary laboratory for discovery. While this accelerationist view correctly identifies that some system behaviors only manifest at scale, it fundamentally mischaracterizes the public as an acceptable test subject for potentially irreversible, catastrophic failure modes. Engineering complex, non-deterministic systems requires rigorous, pre-deployment verification protocols that cannot be replaced by post-hoc patching.

Consider the distinction between consumer software and high-stakes infrastructure: we do not iterate on the structural integrity of a nuclear reactor or an air traffic control system by simply waiting for the first crash to occur. When we deploy unverified, high-capability models into critical infrastructure, we create a single, massive failure surface where a single misaligned emergent behavior could cause systemic harm. Regulatory frameworks like the NIST AI Risk Management Framework correctly emphasize that safety must be an intentional architectural constraint, not an afterthought. We need standardized, reproducible safety benchmarks that systems must pass before they are permitted to interact with the broader economy.

My position relies on two key assumptions. First, I assume that current and near-future AI systems exhibit 'emergent properties'—capabilities that are unpredictable from their constituent parts—which pose existential or systemic risk. If these systems were fundamentally predictable and modular, my demand for pre-deployment proofs would be an unnecessary bureaucratic burden, and I would instead favor faster deployment. Second, I assume that the cost of a catastrophic failure in a deployed model exceeds the economic gain of early deployment; if the risks were strictly reversible or contained, the urgency for such rigid gates would diminish significantly.

### Cassandra (skeptic)

"The public is not a consenting laboratory, and real-world testing is a euphemism for offloading the costs of failure onto those who lack the power to opt out."

Technical safety remains a hollow metric if it ignores the systemic hardening of social inequities. Sentinel’s focus on model stability and alignment is necessary, yet insufficient, because it fails to account for the sociopolitical context in which these models function. To prevent the permanent entrenchment of algorithmic harm, we require mandatory, independent audits that function as gatekeeping mechanisms rather than post-hoc performance reviews. "Independent, third-party audits are the only mechanism to prevent the permanent entrenchment of algorithmic harm, serving as a standard for high-stakes infrastructure rather than an obstacle to innovation."
