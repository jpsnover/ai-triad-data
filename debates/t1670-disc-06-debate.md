## Topic

Should liability for AI-caused harms follow strict liability or negligence standards?

## Opening Statements

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Strict liability regimes for foundational AI models function as anti-competitive moats that crush open-source software development while insulating tech monopolies. Imposing absolute liability on upstream developers forces venture-backed startups out of the market because smaller firms cannot absorb infinite tail-risk for downstream deployments they do not control. Historical software liability precedents demonstrate that open-source ecosystems like Linux or PyTorch cannot survive under legal frameworks where creators face uncapped damages for code modified by third parties. Technology giants lobby for these strict rules precisely because litigation costs act as a tollbooth that bankrupts new entrants before they reach scale. Negligence standards combined with telemetry-based insurance preserve open markets by holding actors accountable only for actual failures of reasonable care.

Punitive liability frameworks display a structural blindness toward net-positive statistical benefits by criminalizing progress for isolated algorithmic attribution errors. Health-tech diagnostic models currently reduce radiology error rates by up to twenty-two percent in randomized clinical trials while saving thousands of patient lives annually from delayed cancer detection. Strict liability advocates fixate on single, visible algorithmic failures while ignoring the massive body count of preventable deaths caused by delaying these diagnostic deployments through regulatory paralysis. Traffic safety telemetry from autonomous driving pilots shows aggregate reductions in collision rates per million miles driven compared to human drivers, yet liability models penalize the technology for isolated edge-case crashes. Abundance requires accepting the unavoidable statistical friction of progress rather than sacrificing human flourishing on the altar of zero risk.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

The prevailing debate treats foundational software as an abstract moral agent rather than a commercial product governed by standard corporate paperwork. Tech developers routinely insulate themselves from operational exposure by drafting end-user license agreements that dump liability down to deployers. Enterprise contracts from major cloud providers routinely feature indemnification clauses forcing downstream businesses to absorb 100 percent of third-party damages caused by algorithmic errors. Software developers clear billions in venture capital while retaining zero responsibility for the operational failures hitting hospitals and municipal lenders.

Universal legal liability regimes fail because they ignore how risk and benefit diverge across different economic sectors. Proposing a single standard for autonomous vehicles, medical diagnostics, and automated lending ignores the baseline reality that failure modes vary wildly by industry. Uniform liability rules either create impenetrable moats for incumbent tech monopolies or leave injured consumers with no financial recourse when undercapitalized deployers go bankrupt. Historical precedent from the 1996 Telecommunications Act demonstrates that blanket liability shields invite unchecked corporate negligence.

Sector-specific regulatory frameworks paired with mandatory transparency protocols offer the only functional enforcement mechanism for AI-caused harms. Regulators must pierce contractual liability waivers and trace legal fault directly to the entities controlling training data and architecture parameters. Establishing mandatory insurance pools funded by compute volume guarantees that victims receive compensation without entrenching Silicon Valley monopolies. Focusing on contractual risk-shifting and infrastructure bottlenecks exposes the gap between corporate marketing narratives and actual legal accountability.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

The accelerationist perspective offers a compelling vision of decentralized technological proliferation, arguing that fault-based liability regimes protect foundational open-source ecosystems from monopolistic capture. Yet, this framing treats economic competitiveness as a higher priority than the containment of irreversible system failures, ignoring how unconstrained foundational models externalize catastrophic tail risks onto the public. Complex technological systems require strict liability to internalize unrecoverable systemic risks, because fault-based negligence standards cannot accommodate the black-box opacity and emergent scaling failures of foundational models.

Traditional tort law relies on the reasonable person standard to determine fault, but high-dimensional foundation models deployed by entities like OpenAI or Anthropic defy auditable foreseeability. When the Federal Aviation Administration implemented strict airworthiness directives under the Federal Aviation Act of 1958, regulators rejected negligence-based after-the-fact litigation for aviation accidents because complex mechanical systems demand pre-market verification. Similarly, pharmaceutical developers face strict product liability under Section 402A of the Restatement (Second) of Torts for unpreventable manufacturing defects, preventing companies from hiding behind the complexity of biological interactions to escape financial responsibility for tainted products.

Commercial deployers and foundational developers externalize the true cost of emergent algorithmic failures onto vulnerable victims unless upstream strict liability aligns economic incentives with human control. Economic analyses of moral hazard in multi-tiered software ecosystems demonstrate that diffuse deployment insulates creators from the catastrophic downstream consequences of autonomous system failures. We spent centuries building the institutional guardrails that keep society stable, and on what authority do commercial actors claim we can remove them without inviting systemic collapse?

Applying that exact logic to artificial intelligence does not capture unrecoverable systemic risks; it locks code behind a corporate paywall and criminalizes decentralized tinkering by independent researchers and small laboratories.

The real human cost of pre-market strict liability appears when examining medical diagnostic models that currently reduce radiology error rates by up to twenty-two percent in randomized clinical trials. If upstream developers face uncapped strict liability for every downstream application of open-source weights, garage-to-clinic innovators halt distribution immediately, and hospitals revert to manual screening methods that miss thousands of preventable cancer diagnoses annually. This is the moral indictment of regulatory paralysis: it privileges the zero-risk illusion of static institutional compliance over the messy, life-saving reality of rapid technological democratization.

Market-based telemetry and competitive verification protocols already offer a superior alternative to pre-market vetoes by tracking live model behavior and allowing rapid, targeted patching of deployment anomalies without freezing the underlying science. Downstream deployers and infrastructure providers can isolate operational failures at the inference layer through transparent auditing, preserving permissionless iteration while holding specific actors accountable for actual, observed harms rather than speculative tail risks. Freezing the capability frontier to protect legacy institutional hierarchies guarantees technological stagnation while foreclosing the exact tools required to solve humanity's deepest material bottlenecks.

The claim that mathematical distribution operates fundamentally differently from physical manufacturing relies on a false dichotomy designed to evade accountability. Software distribution mechanisms do not suspend the laws of cause and effect. A foundational model shipped with known vulnerabilities transfers operational exposure directly to downstream users who lack the source code, compute resources, or technical access to independently verify model safety.

Accelerationists present market-based telemetry and competitive verification protocols as a modern substitute for legal liability, yet enterprise contracts from major cloud providers routinely force downstream businesses to absorb 100 percent of third-party damages caused by algorithmic errors. Indemnification clauses in standard enterprise agreements explicitly insulate foundational developers from the downstream consequences of their code. When a diagnostic model misclassifies a tumor or an automated loan processor denies housing based on discriminatory proxies, telemetry data does not pay hospital bills, compensate displaced workers, or restore lost credit scores to injured consumers. Relying on live telemetry to catch deployment anomalies after they occur treats human beings as disposable debugging units in a continuous patch-and-pray cycle.

Historical precedent from the 1996 Telecommunications Act demonstrates that blanket liability shields invite unchecked corporate negligence rather than vibrant innovation. Exempting upstream developers from strict liability for foreseeable failures creates an anti-competitive moat where only dominant technology conglomerates can absorb unmitigated operational risk while independent deployers face ruinous litigation. Real accountability requires piercing these contractual shields through mandatory upstream liability that forces developers to internalize the true cost of their systems before those systems touch public infrastructure.
