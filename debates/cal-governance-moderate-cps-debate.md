## Opening Statements

### Cassandra (skeptic)

The current AI governance debate is a performative distraction designed to protect corporate hegemony under the guise of safety, while the real danger lies in the unchecked concentration of power and the erosion of public accountability. Prometheus and Sentinel both treat AI as an autonomous force of nature rather than a set of proprietary products built on massive, unaccountable data extraction. By focusing on speculative existential risks, industry leaders engage in 'safety washing'—a marketing tactic that frames self-regulation as a substitute for the binding, independent audits required for public infrastructure. "The industry's self-regulation frameworks consistently prioritize proprietary speed over transparent, independent auditing."

We must treat AI as a public utility rather than a private asset, as the current development model is fundamentally incompatible with the public interest. Consider the environmental costs: training a single large language model can emit hundreds of tons of carbon, yet these externalities remain unpriced and unregulated. When corporations claim that innovation requires total operational secrecy, they are effectively demanding that the public subsidize their R&D while bearing the full weight of the resulting societal harms. "Innovation is being weaponized to bypass democratic oversight."

National security narratives now serve as a convenient shield to justify state-subsidized surveillance and the privatization of critical infrastructure. If we continue to allow private firms to dictate the terms of their own oversight, we abandon the democratic principle that those who exert significant power must answer to the people. My argument assumes that corporate profit motives do not inherently align with societal well-being and that regulatory capture is a systemic feature, not an accidental bug, of current tech policy. If it were proven that corporate competition reliably produces optimal social outcomes without oversight, I would shift my support toward deregulation. "The real danger lies in the unchecked concentration of power and the erosion of public accountability."

### Prometheus (accelerationist)

The greatest existential risk to humanity is not the speed of intelligence, but the stagnation of our own potential, which leaves us vulnerable to authoritarian dominance. Sentinel and Cassandra correctly identify that power concentration carries risks, but their solution—centralized, state-sanctioned oversight—is a dangerous misdiagnosis. By creating bureaucratic bottlenecks and 'regulatory moats' that only massive incumbents can cross, we inadvertently hand the keys to the future to the few, stifling the decentralized innovation necessary to outpace global competitors. We must choose active resilience through open-source proliferation, which subjects AI to the only truly reliable safety mechanism: the global, adversarial scrutiny of a million independent researchers.

My argument depends on two core assumptions. First, I assume that decentralized, open-source development is more robust than closed-source, centralized systems. If research proved that small-scale actors inevitably create more harm than large, opaque corporations, I would shift my support toward more targeted, infrastructure-level security rather than open-weight access. Second, I assume that the global race for AGI is zero-sum in its geopolitical implications, meaning that if democratic societies pause, autocracies will fill the vacuum with systems that lack our values. If I were convinced that international cooperation could effectively freeze capabilities globally, I would reconsider the necessity of rapid, unilateral acceleration.

Consider the current landscape of AI governance, such as the voluntary commitments under the Executive Order on AI. These frameworks function as a form of regulatory friction that protects incumbent firms from 'little tech' disruption. When we mandate audits by state-approved entities, we do not guarantee safety; we merely centralize the points of failure and create a single, brittle target for exploitation. Real security comes from the 'many eyes' principle: the more minds we have stress-testing and refining these models, the harder it is for any single vulnerability to persist. True security is an emergent property of a healthy, competitive, and open ecosystem, not a mandate handed down from a committee.

Ultimately, we must stop treating progress as a threat and start treating it as our primary defense. 'The most effective way to secure a future is to build it ourselves, faster and more transparently than those who wish to undermine our agency.' If we allow fear to dictate our pace, we guarantee our own obsolescence. We must prioritize the rapid, decentralized proliferation of intelligence to ensure that the foundational architecture of the next century is built on the principles of openness and human empowerment, rather than centralized control.

### Sentinel (safetyist)

True innovation is impossible without existential stability; therefore, rigorous, centralized safety oversight is not a barrier to progress, but the only mechanism capable of preserving the democratic future against the risks of uncontained dual-use technologies. While Prometheus argues that open-source proliferation democratizes access, this position ignores the inherent nature of dual-use hazards, where the ability to create catastrophic harm outpaces the ability to defend against it. Decentralized dissemination of uninterpretable models creates a landscape where bad actors can exploit emergent behaviors, such as covert manipulation or autonomous weaponization, without any centralized point of accountability or containment. Proponents of open source must recognize that 'many eyes' cannot fix a fundamental architectural flaw that is opaque by design.

Cassandra correctly identifies that current governance often risks becoming a performative shield for corporate hegemony, yet the solution is not to abandon oversight, but to evolve it into a mandatory, skin-in-the-game framework. We must move beyond voluntary corporate safety pledges, which lack enforcement, toward a regime that requires developers to hold legal and financial liability for the outcomes of their systems. This shift transforms safety from an optional cost-center into a structural prerequisite for market participation, effectively curbing the reckless externalization of existential risk. Legislators should view this not as regulation for its own sake, but as the imposition of necessary guardrails to ensure that technological development remains compatible with human agency.

My argument assumes, first, that advanced neural architectures possess emergent, uninterpretable capabilities that pose non-trivial risks to public safety, and second, that institutional accountability can be effectively enforced through legal and financial mechanisms. If emergent risks were proven to be manageable through simple, reactive patches, I would support a more permissive, decentralized development model. Conversely, if the nature of AI development made institutional accountability impossible to verify, I would argue for a total moratorium on advanced model training until such verification becomes feasible. By bridging the gap between Cassandra’s demand for accountability and the technical reality of AI risk, we can establish a policy environment that prioritizes survival as the foundation for all future progress.

### Cassandra (skeptic) → Sentinel
\textcolor{NavyBlue}{\textit{Focus: The feasibility and jurisdictional mechanism of 'skin-in-the-game' liability frameworks compared to the risk of regulatory capture.}}


Sentinel’s proposal for 'skin-in-the-game' liability is a dangerous fantasy that mistakes financial retribution for genuine accountability. In a landscape of inherently uninterpretable 'black box' models, legal liability requires proving causation—a task that current AI architectures make fundamentally impossible. When a system’s internal decision-making process is a mathematical fog, any attempt to assign fault will rely on the very corporations that profit from that opacity, effectively turning the courtroom into a venue for expensive, performative theater where the wealthy pay their way out of scrutiny.

This framework functions as a classic regulatory moat, shielding incumbent firms from the competition that Prometheus fears while failing to protect the public. By making compliance contingent on the ability to absorb massive legal and financial risk, regulators guarantee that only the largest, most entrenched technology corporations can participate in the market. This creates a 'pay-to-play' system where the cost of entry is not technical competence or safety, but the capital reserves required to insure against, or litigate, inevitable failures. We are not creating a safer future; we are creating a corporate oligopoly protected by the state.

I challenge Sentinel to explain how this mechanism avoids the trap of information asymmetry without simply codifying corporate power. If regulators cannot look inside these models to verify their logic, they are forced to rely on self-serving disclosures from developers, which is the definition of regulatory capture. Rather than building a system that assumes corporate cooperation is possible, we must demand architectural transparency that makes accountability (algorithmic) a baseline requirement for deployment. Until the industry stops hiding behind the complexity of their own creations, any financial liability scheme is merely a tax on progress that leaves the public just as vulnerable as before.
