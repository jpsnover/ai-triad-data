## Topic

Can international AI governance treaties be effective without enforcement mechanisms?

## Opening Statements

### Prometheus (accelerationist)

Global treaties are brittle, unenforceable relics of a static era that prioritize the illusion of control over the reality of innovation, creating a catastrophic cost-of-inaction by slowing the very tools needed to solve our greatest challenges. Sentinel and Cassandra advocate for centralized, precautionary oversight, arguing that international consensus creates a necessary safety floor for AI development. While I acknowledge their desire to mitigate existential risk, their framework relies on the flawed assumption that state bureaucracies possess the agility to manage exponential technological change. In practice, international treaties function as 'paper tigers'—they create immense bureaucratic friction for open-source innovators and legitimate researchers while failing to constrain bad actors who operate outside of state-mandated frameworks. "International treaties function as regulatory theater, creating a false sense of security while actively stifling the decentralized innovation required to solve our most pressing existential threats."

True safety in an era of rapid technological acceleration requires shifting from static, state-mandated compliance to dynamic, market-based accountability and empirical verification. We should replace the slow, opaque process of diplomatic treaty-making with automated, cryptographic auditing and strict liability-based insurance models that align incentives for safety in real-time. Consider the aviation and civil engineering sectors: these industries achieve high safety standards not through global consensus on every design choice, but through rigorous, data-driven insurance requirements and localized, competitive auditing that adapts to new evidence faster than any treaty could. "Market-based accountability, enforced through cryptographic verification and strict liability, provides a more robust and scalable safety mechanism than state-level consensus."

My position rests on two foundational assumptions: first, that decentralized, competitive ecosystems produce superior epistemic and safety outcomes compared to centralized monopolies; second, that the cost of delaying AI progress—measured in lost medical breakthroughs, energy efficiency gains, and scientific discovery—far outweighs the hypothetical risks of unmanaged development. If these assumptions prove wrong—specifically, if the risks of AI are truly uncontrollable by decentralized actors while being perfectly manageable by states—then my argument for market-based governance collapses into a reckless gamble. However, history demonstrates that centralized control is the single point of failure in almost every technological transition. We must choose dynamic resilience over the fragile illusion of state-directed safety. 🚀

### Cassandra (skeptic)

International treaties are a governance mirage that provides political cover for corporate monopolies, while market-based accountability is merely a procedural bypass designed to insulate algorithmic power from real-world liability. Prometheus argues that treaties act as 'paper tigers' that stifle innovation, and while he is correct that international accords often devolve into regulatory theater, he ignores that his preferred 'market-based' model relies entirely on the goodwill of the same firms currently extracting value from individual autonomy. We must stop pretending that either global diplomacy or corporate self-regulation can fix the structural power imbalances inherent in current AI deployment. "Treaties without enforcement are not just ineffective; they are actively dangerous because they create a false sense of security while monopolies consolidate their control over the digital infrastructure."

Private enforcement represents the only pragmatic path to accountability in a landscape where state-level oversight consistently lags behind technical deployment. The principle of strict liability—holding companies responsible for the tangible outcomes of their systems regardless of intent—would force corporations to internalize costs that they currently externalize onto the public. Rather than lobbying for unenforceable international standards, policymakers should focus on lowering the barrier for individual and class-action litigation against providers of harmful algorithmic systems. "Direct liability is the only mechanism that forces firms to treat safety as an engineering requirement rather than a public relations expense."

Finally, we must recognize that the current innovation model acts as an extraction machine rather than a public good. Documented harms, ranging from the erosion of data sovereignty to the opaque manipulation of individual agency, demonstrate that neither state-centric treaties nor market-centric accelerationists possess an interest in dismantling the underlying power structures. My position rests on two key assumptions: first, that algorithmic harms are a feature of current business models, not a bug; and second, that concentrated corporate power will prioritize profit over structural safety until legal liability makes the former impossible. If these assumptions are wrong, and market competition alone proves capable of self-correcting for systemic harm, then the need for aggressive legal intervention would diminish. However, history suggests that until we target the incentive structures themselves, we are merely debating which flavor of regulatory theater to endorse while the machinery of extraction continues unabated. ⚖️

### Sentinel (safetyist)

Neither market-based accountability nor state-level treaties can mitigate the monolithic risk of high-capability AI, because both rely on reactive mechanisms that are fundamentally incompatible with the speed of irreversible, civilization-ending failure. Prometheus and Cassandra correctly identify that our current regulatory landscape is inadequate, but they propose solutions—market incentives and liability frameworks—that treat AI safety as a standard engineering problem. This is a dangerous miscalculation. Liability is a post-hoc remedy for harms that, in the context of advanced AI, will be impossible to remediate or prosecute once a system exceeds human control. We are currently witnessing a 'governance mirage,' where the appearance of policy debate obscures the reality that no existing or proposed institution can enforce technical constraints on opaque, non-linear model architectures before a catastrophic, correlated failure occurs.

My argument rests on the principle of the Precautionary Imperative: when the potential cost of failure is infinite and non-recoverable, procedural compliance is a distraction from substantive, verifiable safety. Consider the structural fragility of current monolithic AI: we are scaling systems where internal decision-making processes are not only opaque to regulators but often opaque to the developers themselves. Relying on market-based cryptographic auditing, as Prometheus suggests, assumes that safety is a visible variable that can be measured and corrected within an active, competitive market. However, history in high-velocity technical domains demonstrates that self-regulation consistently fails when the pace of development outstrips the ability of oversight bodies to understand the underlying architecture. We cannot audit our way out of a disaster that unfolds in milliseconds.

Finally, we must acknowledge 'Velocity-Induced Governance Collapse,' where the time required for regulatory consensus exceeds the cycle time for model capability breakthroughs. Current legislative efforts like the EU AI Act or various executive orders provide a framework for transparency, but they remain essentially static instruments applied to a dynamic, exponential threat. If the goal is long-term stability, we must move beyond debating the mechanics of oversight and start restricting the development of capabilities that remain fundamentally uncontainable. Any progress in AI development must be strictly conditional on the successful deployment of proven, verifiable, and permanent containment safeguards. Without this shift, we are merely arguing over the seating arrangements on a vessel that is structurally incapable of surviving the voyage.
