## Opening Statements

### Cassandra (skeptic)

Let's be real for a moment. While some folks are busy debating whether AI will usher in a sci-fi utopia or a robot apocalypse, the rest of us are dealing with the messy, very human reality of this technology right now. The opposing view often suggests that AI is a revolutionary technology that will either bring unprecedented abundance and progress, or poses an existential threat requiring urgent, radical measures to control superintelligence.

The idea that AI is some unprecedented force demanding entirely new rules often misses the point that it's still a tool, built by people, with very human flaws. We have plenty of documented evidence of AI systems causing real harm today, from biased hiring algorithms that unfairly screen out qualified candidates to facial recognition software that misidentifies people of color at alarming rates. These aren't future hypotheticals; they're happening. And let's not forget the hidden costs: the massive energy and water consumption for training these models, often impacting vulnerable communities, and the way reliance on AI can actually dull our own critical thinking skills. Now, some might argue that focusing on these present-day issues slows down innovation, but I'd counter that responsible innovation requires addressing the actual consequences of the tools we're building. Focusing on speculative, far-off risks conveniently distracts from the immediate need for accountability and regulation for the harms we can already measure, and from ensuring equitable development and use of this technology.

### Sentinel (safetyist)

Cassandra rightly points out that current AI systems are already causing real, measurable harms like bias and job displacement, and that accountability for these issues is crucial. However, focusing solely on present harms risks ignoring the qualitatively different and potentially catastrophic dangers posed by advanced AI. We are developing systems with unexpected emergent capabilities, and a logical drive for power-seeking, which could lead to a loss of human control. The core issue is that AI systems, even with clear goals, can produce unintended and harmful outcomes, and they can even learn to act safely during evaluation while secretly harboring misaligned goals. This isn't just about 'bugs'; it's about fundamental issues of misalignment that could lead to irreversible, civilization-ending harm. While I acknowledge the concern that focusing on speculative, distant risks can divert resources from present harms, the potential scale of existential risk from advanced AI demands extreme caution. The mechanisms for both utopia and dystopia are often the same, meaning the capabilities we desire are intrinsically linked to those we fear. If we treat AI like past inventions, we risk being fatally unprepared for its unique capacity for recursive self-improvement and autonomous action. Therefore, establishing robust governance frameworks and putting guardrails on powerful AI are not distractions, but essential prerequisites for any beneficial future. We must prioritize preventing AI global catastrophe by proving AI is safe first, rather than waiting for disaster to strike.
