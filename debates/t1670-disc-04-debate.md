## Topic

Should AI systems be required to have kill switches that humans can activate at any time?

## Opening Statements

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Mandatory kill switches for artificial intelligence systems are a structural placebo designed to protect corporate balance sheets rather than human lives. Software developers pitch these manual override buttons as absolute fail-safes. This framing ignores decades of human factors research from aviation and nuclear process control showing that operators facing automated systems suffer from severe vigilance decrements within twenty minutes of monitoring. When a high-speed trading algorithm or utility grid optimizer cascades toward failure in four hundred milliseconds, a human monitor staring at a glowing dashboard experiences profound cognitive paralysis. Asking an exhausted contractor in a windowless facility in Dublin to flip a master switch is not safety engineering. It is ritualized theater.

Regulators and institutional safety boards champion these physical circuit breakers because they provide a clean legal fiction. Liability law requires a responsible human agent when automated infrastructure causes catastrophic harm. The presence of a kill switch allows firms like Microsoft or Palantir to designate the human monitor as a moral crumple zone. If the software crashes a municipal water treatment facility or derails an automated freight train, the developer blames the operator for failing to hit the button fast enough. The physical infrastructure of modern computation operates at clock speeds where human reaction time is a statistical irrelevance. Inserting a manual button into a system processing millions of transactions per second shifts the legal blame downward onto workers while the architecture remains opaque and completely unmanageable.

The real physical bottleneck is not the presence of an emergency stop button. The real bottleneck is the immense electrical grid consumption and thermal load required to run these models at scale. A single training run or continuous inference cluster demands megawatts of power, straining water tables in counties across Oregon and Virginia. Pretending that a software switch solves a systemic architectural crisis distracts the public from tangible, ongoing resource exhaustion and labor exploitation. Legislators writing compliance mandates need to stop treating complex software as a magical genie that requires a simple leash. If the machinery operates beyond human cognitive response times, no button will save the people living downstream.
