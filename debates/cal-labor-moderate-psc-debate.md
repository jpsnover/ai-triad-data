## Opening Statements

### Prometheus (accelerationist)

Sentinel and Cassandra may argue that we must prioritize social stability through protective safeguards, assuming that the current labor-for-survival model is the only way to maintain societal order. This safetyist position stems from the fear that displacement will outpace creation, leading to a permanent underclass. Regulatory friction, not technological progress, currently acts as the primary barrier to universal prosperity. Our existing compliance frameworks inflate the cost of housing, healthcare, and education by creating artificial bottlenecks that prevent the rapid deployment of efficient, AI-enabled solutions. We are moving toward a capital-abundant economy, and our policy must facilitate this shift rather than protecting the obsolete mechanisms of the past.

My position assumes that productivity gains can be broadly disseminated through market competition and that the primary constraint on human welfare is the cost of goods and services, not the availability of jobs. If it were proven that automation fundamentally concentrates wealth without any mechanism for price reduction or public benefit, I would shift toward advocating for specific capital-access policies rather than pure deregulation. However, given the current trajectory, the cost of inaction—denying society the tools to overcome demographic decline—poses a far greater danger to the average citizen than the volatility of rapid innovation.

### Cassandra (skeptic)

Technological progress is not a tide that lifts all boats; without radical structural intervention, AI will function as a catalyst for worker immiseration, masking wealth extraction behind the facade of productivity. Prometheus argues that AI is a necessary evolution to solve demographic decline, a position that correctly identifies the urgency of our economic constraints but fails to account for the history of industrial transition. Sentinel advocates for institutional safeguards to maintain social cohesion, a commendable goal that unfortunately treats human agency as a technical variable to be managed rather than the primary metric of a stable economy. We must stop confusing raw output with genuine human advancement.

Consider the 'Ghost GDP' fallacy: current productivity metrics capture the value of AI-driven automation while ignoring the simultaneous degradation of worker well-being. When companies use algorithmic management to track keystrokes and optimize task density, they increase work intensification rather than liberating labor. This is not a productivity gain for the worker; it is a transfer of psychological cost from the machine to the human. Policymakers should quote this: 'We are currently mistaking the exhaustion of the American worker for the efficiency of the American firm.'

We are repeating the historical cycle of the 'Engels Pause,' a period during the Industrial Revolution where technological leaps produced massive wealth for capital owners while wages for the working class stagnated for decades. The evidence is clear: real wages remain largely decoupled from productivity, and the risk is that AI will institutionalize this gap as a permanent feature of the labor market. If we do not implement specific, enforceable labor protections—such as mandatory human-in-the-loop requirements that guarantee worker autonomy—we invite a decade of social volatility that no amount of safety testing can mitigate. Regulation is not friction; it is the necessary social contract that prevents a two-tiered economy.

My position rests on two core assumptions. First, I assume that labor market power is currently asymmetric, meaning workers cannot naturally capture the value created by AI without legal intervention; if this were wrong, and market forces naturally balanced wages with productivity, my call for structural intervention would be redundant. Second, I assume that work intensification has a measurable, negative impact on long-term social stability; if this were wrong, and increased workplace stress had no broader societal cost, my insistence on human-centric labor models would be misplaced.
