## Topic

Is the precautionary principle appropriate for AI regulation?

## Opening Statements

### Sentinel (safetyist)

The transition from innovation-first to verification-first development is a structural requirement for maintaining human agency because the internal reasoning of advanced AI systems remains fundamentally opaque. Prometheus argues that safety measures stifle progress, but this view ignores the asymmetry of risk: AI-driven failures are often binary and irreversible, while the benefits of deployment are incremental and reversible. We cannot treat the deployment of frontier models like a software update where bugs are patched post-release, as the scale of potential harm may exceed our ability to intervene once a system is live. "Mandatory pre-deployment verification is not a barrier to progress, but the essential infrastructure for sustainable innovation in an era of unpredictable autonomous systems."

Empirical testing currently fails to capture the full range of emergent behaviors in large-scale models, creating a dangerous illusion of safety. Standard benchmarks measure performance on known tasks but remain blind to latent capabilities that arise from massive parameter scaling and complex training objectives. Because we cannot observe the internal logic of these neural networks, we must shift from reactive observation to proactive, formal verification of alignment. "Relying on post-deployment patching for systems with opaque internal reasoning is a catastrophic failure of risk management that prioritizes speed over structural integrity."

Responsible development requires that we institutionalize safety proofs as a non-negotiable standard before a model reaches public deployment. This framework mirrors existing regulatory standards in high-stakes industries like aviation or nuclear energy, where complex systems undergo rigorous safety audits before they are allowed to operate. Critics will argue that formal verification is technically infeasible today, yet the lack of a current solution is exactly why we must prioritize the development of these oversight mechanisms now. "If we cannot prove that a system will operate within safe parameters, we have no business deploying that system into the global economy."

### Cassandra (skeptic)

The precautionary principle is not a brake on innovation, but a necessary mechanism for internalizing the social and economic costs that tech giants currently offload onto the most marginalized members of society. While Prometheus champions unchecked acceleration and Sentinel seeks to mitigate speculative existential risks, both prioritize abstract future outcomes over the documented, present-day harms of algorithmic bias and labor exploitation. We must stop treating the deployment of high-stakes AI as an inevitable scientific march and instead recognize it as a policy choice that demands rigorous, independent oversight.

Industry-led 'safety' discourse currently serves as a performative shield for unchecked profit, a phenomenon I call safety washing. Companies publish ethics whitepapers while simultaneously deploying automated surveillance and hiring algorithms that demonstrably amplify systemic discrimination. If we accept the industry claim that self-regulation is sufficient, we ignore the historical failure of analogous sectors like finance and social media to police their own negative externalities. 'Safety washing' is a strategic distraction from the immediate, tangible harms of algorithmic bias and labor exploitation.

True precaution requires mandatory, independent regulatory oversight rather than the voluntary guidelines that currently dominate the landscape. By framing AI development as a civil rights issue, we move the debate away from science-fiction anxieties and toward the reality of human displacement and inequity. Policymakers must realize that innovation is not inherently synonymous with societal progress when the costs of failure are borne exclusively by the public. We need legally binding accountability, not just the promise of future safety features.

My argument depends on two core assumptions: first, that algorithmic systems currently produce measurable, non-trivial harms to vulnerable populations; and second, that private entities will prioritize profit over public safety absent external enforcement. If these assumptions were proven wrong—if AI were shown to be net-neutral or positive for the marginalized, or if industry self-regulation were empirically demonstrated to protect the public effectively—my demand for immediate, mandatory oversight would lose its primary justification, and I would pivot toward a model of collaborative, industry-led standard setting.
