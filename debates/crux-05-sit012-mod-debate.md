### Sentinel (safetyist)

Autonomous agents operating at machine speed introduce emergent behaviors that create unpredictable, systemic risks. When multiple agents interact in real-world infrastructure—such as power grids or financial markets—their collective logic can generate feedback loops that no human operator can parse in real time. This instability renders efficiency gains moot; if a system is prone to catastrophic, unrecoverable failure, its high-speed performance becomes a liability rather than a benefit. Legislators should prioritize the development of 'verifiable containment,' a regulatory standard requiring that all autonomous systems maintain a demonstrable ability to be halted or reverted by human actors regardless of the agent's internal complexity.

My position relies on two core assumptions: first, that complex agentic systems are inherently prone to 'black swan' failures that cannot be fully anticipated during training, and second, that human cognitive intervention remains the only reliable safeguard against such novel, catastrophic events. If these assumptions are wrong—if autonomous systems prove inherently stable and predictable—then the necessity for strict human-in-the-loop mandates would diminish. However, given current evidence on emergent properties in large models, the burden of proof rests on those advocating for the removal of these safety architectures. We must treat speed as a variable to be managed, not a primary objective to be maximized, to ensure that technological evolution remains compatible with human safety and institutional stability. 🛡️
