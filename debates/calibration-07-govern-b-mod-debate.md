## Opening Statements

### Cassandra (skeptic)

The current AI governance debate functions as a manufactured distraction, designed to protect private profit under the guise of safety while shielding firms from accountability for immediate, documented harms. Industry leaders prioritize speculative, existential-risk narratives—sci-fi scenarios about future superintelligence—precisely because these myths prevent regulators from imposing meaningful transparency requirements on the algorithmic biases and labor displacement occurring today. By framing the policy choice as a race against an imagined apocalypse, corporations successfully pivot the conversation away from the erosion of democratic oversight and the concentration of wealth in the hands of a few unaccountable firms.

Consider the empirical reality of AI development today: the environmental costs of massive compute clusters and the systematic extraction of value from public data remain socialized, while the financial gains are strictly privatized. When we examine the lifecycle of these models, we see that 'safety' is often a veneer for 'safety washing,' where internal ethics boards serve as corporate public relations rather than genuine enforcement mechanisms. Congress does not need to wait for a hypothetical disaster to act; it must address the current reality where firms deploy systems that influence democratic discourse and economic opportunity without any public audit or democratic mandate.

True governance requires shifting power from corporate boardrooms to the public, moving beyond the failed model of voluntary industry standards. We must replace the industry-preferred 'self-regulation' framework with mandatory, participatory auditing and community-led oversight. If we treat AI as a public utility rather than a proprietary black box, we can enforce transparency and demand that these systems serve the common good. Accountability is not an obstacle to innovation; it is the only mechanism that prevents innovation from becoming a tool for systemic exploitation.

### Sentinel (safetyist)

Robust, precautionary governance is not a barrier to innovation, but the essential infrastructure required to prevent irreversible, existential failures that would permanently terminate the trajectory of human technological advancement. Prometheus argues that safety is a cost of inaction, and Cassandra rightfully highlights that we must address the immediate, tangible harms already affecting our societies. However, both perspectives overlook the compounding nature of AI development; reactive measures—like those currently employed in software patching—consistently fail to mitigate the novel hazard vectors that emerge as model capabilities scale exponentially. We need a regulatory framework that treats safety as a foundational prerequisite for progress, much like how aviation authorities mandate rigorous testing before a new aircraft model enters commercial service.

A global regulatory pact provides the only viable mechanism to neutralize the current race to the bottom, where competitive pressures incentivize companies to bypass safety testing to achieve market dominance. Game-theoretic models demonstrate that when safety is treated as a discretionary expenditure, firms that prioritize caution are penalized by the market, while those that ignore safety gain a temporary, dangerous advantage. By establishing binding, international standards for compute-intensive training runs, we can move safety from a competitive disadvantage to a baseline expectation. As long as the development of transformative AI remains an uncoordinated sprint, the probability of a catastrophic, systemic failure remains unacceptably high.

Finally, we must integrate a 'skin-in-the-game' model to ensure corporate accountability is baked into the development lifecycle. Liability frameworks in high-stakes industries, such as nuclear energy and aerospace, show that when organizations bear direct responsibility for failures, they cultivate internal safety cultures that far exceed the minimum legal requirements. Currently, AI developers operate with significant liability shields that decouple the benefits of rapid deployment from the systemic risks they impose on the public. By enforcing strict, long-term liability for models that cause large-scale harm, we force firms to internalize the risks of their own advancements, aligning their incentives with the long-term stability of the systems they build.
