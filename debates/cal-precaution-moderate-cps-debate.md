## Topic

Is the precautionary principle appropriate for AI regulation?

## Opening Statements

### Prometheus (accelerationist)

The precautionary principle is not a safety mechanism but a tax on human progress, trading the certain, measurable suffering of the present for the speculative, unproven fears of a hypothetical future. Sentinel and Cassandra operate under the assumption that the status quo is a baseline of safety, yet this ignores the millions of lives lost annually to diseases, energy poverty, and systemic inefficiencies that AI is currently positioned to solve. When regulators impose blanket moratoriums or restrictive licensing regimes, they do not create safety; they enforce a state of stagnation that preserves existing harms. We must shift our framework from the paralysis of precaution to the necessity of adaptive governance, where we manage risks through active oversight and real-time iteration rather than through the blunt instrument of bureaucratic delay.

Consider the opportunity cost of caution: every month spent debating the theoretical risks of frontier models is a month where we delay the deployment of AI-driven drug discovery, material science breakthroughs, and optimized energy grids. These are not abstract benefits; they are measurable productivity gains and life-saving tools currently idling in research labs due to regulatory uncertainty. If we prioritize the mitigation of speculative future risks over the resolution of tangible current crises, we effectively choose to maintain a world of preventable death and limited resources. A policy reporter should understand that 'slowing down' is a political decision to accept the status quo’s failures, not a neutral act of prudence.

Finally, the push for restrictive, centralized regulation ignores the geopolitical reality of technological competition. If the United States or its allies adopt suffocating precautionary mandates, they create a power vacuum that less accountable, non-aligned actors will immediately fill. This is a strategic surrender, not a safeguard, as it ensures that the most powerful tools of the next century are developed outside the reach of democratic oversight. Adaptive governance—which relies on rapid audit, red-teaming, and modular deployment—offers a superior enforcement mechanism because it keeps us in the loop of development rather than on the sidelines of history.

By mandating rigorous, pre-deployment safety standards, we do not stifle innovation; rather, we establish the quality-control infrastructure necessary for technology to reliably serve human interests.
