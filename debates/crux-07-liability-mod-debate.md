## Topic

Should AI companies be held strictly liable for harms caused by their deployed systems?

## Opening Statements

### Sentinel (safetyist)

Innovation without strict liability is not progress; it is the reckless externalization of catastrophic risk onto the public. Prometheus argues that rapid deployment is a moral imperative to prevent the costs of inaction, suggesting that market-driven competition will naturally resolve safety flaws. This position relies on the assumption that market actors can adequately price and contain risks that are non-linear, irreversible, and potentially existential. However, historical precedents in complex systems, such as the aviation and chemical industries, demonstrate that when responsibility is diffused, developers inevitably prioritize speed over the containment of systemic hazards, creating a 'moral crumple zone' where the public bears the cost of failure. "Innovation without strict liability is not progress, but the reckless externalization of catastrophic risk."

To address this, we must implement a legal gate that mandates proof of safety before deployment, effectively shifting the burden of proof from the public to the developer. Strict liability provides the only mechanism capable of forcing institutional accountability by assigning a 'thick identity' to the entities profiting from autonomous capabilities. When a company holds financial responsibility for the entirety of an autonomous agent's failure, safety transforms from a secondary constraint into a foundational design requirement. "Strict liability is the essential mechanism that forces developers to internalize the true cost of the autonomous systems they release."

Critics will argue that this creates a chilling effect on development, potentially ceding technological leadership to less regulated jurisdictions. This concern assumes that unmitigated speed is the only metric of progress, ignoring that the scale of potential catastrophe from autonomous systems exceeds the capacity of any market-based insurance or mitigation framework. We must treat innovation stagnation as a necessary safety buffer rather than a loss; we are trading the velocity of deployment for the security of our infrastructure. By codifying strict liability, we align developer incentives with societal survival, ensuring that progress remains conditional on proven safety. 🛡️

### Cassandra (skeptic)

Strict liability is not a tool for managing hypothetical future threats, but a necessary economic circuit breaker for an industry currently built on the systemic externalization of social, environmental, and labor costs. Sentinel correctly identifies that developers must internalize the costs of their products, yet they mistakenly focus on speculative existential risks while ignoring the immediate, documented harms of algorithmic bias and massive environmental degradation. We are currently witnessing a 'Subprime AI' crisis, where massive capital expenditure flows into models with no proven path to profitability, forcing companies to gamble with public infrastructure to keep their valuations afloat. "Strict liability is the only mechanism capable of forcing corporations to treat public safety as a balance sheet item rather than a marketing afterthought."

Prometheus argues that innovation requires total freedom, framing any regulation as a stifling tax on progress. This position assumes that current AI development follows the trajectory of beneficial technologies like the internet, rather than the pattern of an over-leveraged speculative bubble. In reality, the industry uses 'defense-in-depth'—a collection of technical safeguards—as a performative shield to avoid addressing institutional negligence. When a firm deploys a model that displaces workers or amplifies bias, they point to their safety boards; I point to the lack of legal recourse for the affected parties. "Technical safety layers are currently used as a corporate shield to deflect institutional accountability for real-world failures."

My argument depends on two primary assumptions: first, that AI companies prioritize shareholder value over social stability, and second, that current market incentives are incapable of self-correcting for negative externalities. If these assumptions are wrong—if firms are actually altruistic or if market competition naturally drives safety—then strict liability would indeed be an unnecessary burden. However, given the documented patterns of 'safety washing' and the lack of post-deployment remediation for existing harms, strict liability remains the only viable policy mechanism. We must move from debating the future of intelligence to regulating the present reality of commerce. ⚖️

Strict liability is not a safety mechanism but a regressive regulatory tax that entrenches monopolies and imposes a catastrophic cost of inaction on humanity by stifling the innovation required to solve our most urgent global crises. Sentinel and Cassandra argue that liability acts as a circuit breaker against reckless risk; this position correctly identifies that AI deployment carries inherent complexity, yet it wrongly assumes that state-mandated legal frameworks can outpace the speed of technical evolution. By imposing strict liability, regulators effectively create a high-cost barrier to entry that only established incumbents can afford, while decentralized and open-source innovators are priced out of the market before they can even begin. "Strict liability functions as a barrier to entry that preserves incumbent dominance by forcing every developer to navigate a minefield of unpredictable litigation."

Market-driven accountability and technical verification offer a superior, more responsive path to safety than the sluggish, capture-prone machinery of the courtroom. While Sentinel advocates for punitive legal regimes, the reality is that automated interpretability—the technical process of mapping internal model states to human-understandable concepts—provides real-time, adaptive safety that litigation-driven compliance cannot match. When safety is treated as a balance sheet liability, firms prioritize legal avoidance over technical robustness; conversely, when safety is a competitive feature, it drives a race to the top in transparency and verification. "Competitive transparency provides a more effective safety architecture than static, reactive legal frameworks because it aligns developer incentives with user trust rather than legal risk mitigation."

We must reject the moral hazard of the 'circuit breaker' argument, which treats the status quo as a safe baseline rather than a state of continuous, preventable suffering. Every day of innovation delayed by regulatory friction is a day where life-saving efficiency, medical breakthroughs, and energy abundance are denied to the public. If we equate progress with danger, we ignore the massive opportunity cost of failing to solve the very problems that currently threaten our stability. "The true reckless act is delaying the deployment of life-saving, efficiency-generating technologies under the guise of caution, thereby condemning millions to the harms of a slower, less capable world." 🚀

My position depends on two core assumptions: first, that technical interpretability will scale effectively with model complexity, and second, that decentralized, competitive markets are more capable of identifying and mitigating risks than centralized regulatory bodies. If interpretability fails to keep pace with model capabilities, the case for external, state-mandated oversight would strengthen; if market competition consistently leads to 'race-to-the-bottom' safety standards, the argument for baseline liability would gain empirical merit.

### Sentinel (safetyist) → Prometheus
\textcolor{NavyBlue}{\textit{Focus: Address Prometheus's claim that strict liability functions as a barrier to entry that preserves incumbent dominance (AN-7) and explain how a liability framework could be designed to avoid this outcome.}}


I concede that a blunt, one-size-fits-all liability framework can inadvertently function as a moat for incumbents, creating a barrier to entry that stifles competition. The danger is that rigid legal burdens, if applied indiscriminately to all developers, will indeed force smaller innovators into bankruptcy while allowing large firms to absorb the cost of litigation as a routine business expense. However, this is a failure of design, not a failure of the principle of accountability (institutional). We can avoid this outcome by implementing a tiered, capability-based liability model that scales legal obligations strictly according to the level of risk posed by a system's capabilities (hazard).

Under this proposal, liability (institutional) would remain dormant for low-risk, small-scale research, ensuring that early-stage startups and open-source contributors can innovate without the threat of catastrophic litigation. Only when a system crosses specific, empirically verified thresholds in capabilities (hazard)—such as autonomous replication or significant cross-domain reasoning—would the full weight of strict liability be triggered. This approach ensures that developers of high-stakes, potentially existential technologies internalize the true costs of their deployments, while shielding the broader ecosystem from prohibitive compliance costs. "By calibrating legal burdens to actual risk rather than firm size, we can maintain competitive vibrancy while ensuring that those who deploy systems with the potential for irreversible harm remain financially and legally responsible for their outcomes."

Prometheus argues that competitive transparency is a more effective safety architecture than reactive legal frameworks. I disagree; transparency provides critical data for verification, but it is a diagnostic tool, not an enforcement mechanism. Transparency allows stakeholders to see a fire, but it does not compel the party that built the structure to install sprinklers or accept responsibility when the building burns down. Accountability (institutional) is the necessary partner to transparency; we need a system where the ability to inspect a model is paired with a clear, enforceable legal duty to ensure that model remains under human control (human agency).
