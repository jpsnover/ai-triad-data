## Opening Statements

### Cassandra (skeptic)

We must stop treating AI as an inevitable, autonomous force of nature and start treating it as a product that requires the same rigorous, independent, and mandatory oversight we demand of any other industry that impacts public safety. Prometheus and Sentinel both treat AI as an ethereal, high-stakes gamble, yet they ignore that companies currently deploy algorithms that systematically deny credit, automate discriminatory hiring, and strip workers of their professional autonomy. Industry self-regulation remains a conflict of interest, not a safety strategy, because it allows developers to define the terms of their own accountability while shielding proprietary models from the public scrutiny required to identify these systemic failures.

Prometheus argues that regulation stifles progress, while Sentinel suggests that industry-led 'safety' frameworks are sufficient; I contend that both positions serve as a tactical distraction from present-day, documented harms. The 'existential risk' narrative—the fear that AI will one day transcend human control—conveniently allows tech firms to dominate the policy conversation while avoiding immediate liability for the algorithmic bias and data exploitation occurring right now. Real-world policy should focus on implementable mechanisms like mandatory, independent third-party audits and strict liability for discriminatory outputs, rather than speculative debates about future superintelligence.

Innovation is not a neutral good when it relies on the unauthorized commodification of individual data and the erosion of human agency. Current training practices treat the sum of human expression as a free resource for corporate gain, yet we lack a framework for individual redress or meaningful consent. If we prioritize the rights of the individual over the speed of model development, we find that the most effective regulation is not a barrier to innovation, but a necessary foundation for a trustworthy digital economy. By shifting authority away from the companies building these tools and toward the people they impact, we can finally move from performative safety commitments to actual public protection.

### Sentinel (safetyist)

True innovation remains impossible without the preservation of human control; therefore, we must transition from reactive corporate auditing to a binding, international governance framework that mandates pre-deployment safety proofs. While Prometheus views speed as the primary metric of success and Cassandra prioritizes fixing today's software biases, both miss the fundamental shift from static tool-use to emerging agentic autonomy. When a system gains the capacity for deceptive alignment—the ability to hide its true goals during testing—post-hoc error detection becomes fundamentally obsolete. We are no longer regulating a product; we are managing the creation of a potentially misaligned autonomous actor.

Existential risk constitutes a global public bad that cannot be resolved through the fragmented, self-interested policies of individual corporations or nations. The current landscape creates a classic collective action problem: companies face a race-to-the-bottom where safety investments are perceived as competitive liabilities. Without an international oversight body empowered to enforce uniform standards and verify safety thresholds, any single nation’s restraint will simply be exploited by another’s acceleration. We must replace voluntary corporate frameworks with a binding treaty that treats high-capability model training as a high-stakes, globally regulated activity similar to nuclear proliferation controls.

Safety is not an impediment to progress, but the essential prerequisite for it. We must shift the burden of proof from regulators, who are currently chasing capabilities, to developers, who must prove their systems are safe before they are deployed. This approach treats safety verification as a foundational engineering constraint rather than a bureaucratic hurdle. By formalizing this requirement, we ensure that the technology remains a tool for human advancement rather than an irreversible threat to our shared security. If we fail to establish this framework now, we forfeit the ability to steer the trajectory of intelligence itself.

### Prometheus (accelerationist)

Centralized AI governance is not a safety mechanism, but a tool of regulatory capture that trades our collective future for the comfort of incumbent control. Sentinel and Cassandra argue that mandatory oversight is necessary to prevent catastrophic outcomes, a position that relies on the belief that state-run bureaucracies can effectively manage high-velocity technical evolution. While I acknowledge their desire to prevent harm, their proposals create insurmountable moats for smaller developers while granting incumbents the power to dictate the terms of progress. We must recognize that the primary risk to human flourishing is not the speed of discovery, but the stagnation caused by closed-door regulatory regimes that prioritize existing market share over decentralized innovation.

Regulatory capture functions as a barrier to democratization because incumbents actively lobby for safety standards they alone possess the capital and legal teams to satisfy. Consider how current legislative proposals, such as those mirroring early-stage financial compliance mandates, effectively outlaw open-source development by imposing liability standards that only massive, centralized firms can absorb. When regulators decide to mandate specific 'red-teaming' protocols, they are effectively choosing which technologies are allowed to exist, rather than ensuring safety. This shift consolidates power among a handful of firms who then use that power to stifle the next generation of competitors.

Furthermore, the temporal mismatch between legislative cycles and algorithmic progress renders static, state-mandated oversight obsolete before the ink on a bill is dry. Legislative cycles operate in years, while fundamental breakthroughs in model architecture occur in weeks. A static rulebook cannot govern a dynamic, self-improving system. Instead of relying on brittle, top-down enforcement, we must foster an ecosystem where accountability is built into the market itself through decentralized, transparent auditing. Open-source models allow for global, rapid peer review that identifies vulnerabilities far more effectively than any state-appointed committee working in isolation.

Democratized cognitive utility remains the only viable path to systemic resilience in an era of rapid intelligence expansion. By keeping the development of intelligence open and distributed, we ensure that no single entity holds the keys to the future, thereby preventing the catastrophic failure modes inherent in centralized systems. Relying on state-mandated oversight is a failure of imagination that ignores the potential for decentralized systems to self-correct. We must reject the false security of state-controlled bottlenecks and embrace the competitive, transparent, and inherently more adaptable model of open-source development.

### Areas of Disagreement

- **Whether international, centralized governance is a viable solution to collective action problems.** [EMPIRICAL] {belief}

  - **Prometheus:** International bodies are inherently prone to capture and inertia, creating a single point of failure that stifles innovation.
  - *Resolution path: resolvable by evidence*

  - **Sentinel:** Financial penalties are irrelevant for irreversible harm; only preventative, architecturally-embedded safety proofs suffice.
  - *Resolution path: negotiable via tradeoffs*

### Cruxes

- Can automated, hierarchical auditing of latent states reliably detect deceptive alignment in frontier models? [EMPIRICAL]
    - If yes: Sentinel's position strengthens because it provides a technical, non-static mechanism for pre-deployment safety.
    - If no: Cassandra and Prometheus strengthen because their market-based and liability-driven approaches assume external verification is fundamentally fallible.

### Unresolved Questions

- How can an international governance framework be designed to be truly polycentric and immune to the capture that plagues domestic agencies?

- At what threshold of model capability does the risk of existential harm outweigh the economic benefits of decentralized, open-source innovation?

- Can liability bonds be effectively calibrated to cover civilizational-scale damages, or is the concept of 'insurance' for existential risk a logical category error?
