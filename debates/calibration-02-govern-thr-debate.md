## Topic

## Opening Statements

### Sentinel (safetyist)

Technological advancement without binding, empirical safety verification is not innovation; it is a reckless gamble with human agency that necessitates immediate, state-led oversight. Prometheus, representing the accelerationist perspective, argues that rapid deployment acts as an essential hedge against geopolitical stagnation and that competitive market pressure will naturally drive safety improvements. While I acknowledge the genuine insight that stagnation carries its own risks, this perspective relies on the flawed assumption that safety is a product of competitive pressure rather than a casualty of it. History shows that when companies compete for dominance in high-stakes fields, they systematically prune safety margins to capture market share, a pattern we must prevent here.

Deployment of advanced AI systems must be contingent upon rigorous, empirical alignment proofs rather than heuristic testing. Current industry standards often rely on 'red-teaming'—a trial-and-error process that cannot guarantee safety in non-linear, unpredictable systems. Because these models exhibit emergent behaviors that developers often fail to predict, we require formal verification methods that mathematically guarantee the system will adhere to predefined safety constraints. Without these objective, verifiable benchmarks, we are essentially launching black-box technologies into the public sphere with no mechanism for emergency recall or effective containment.

Unchecked concentration of AI capabilities threatens the permanent loss of democratic human autonomy. When we transition from human-in-the-loop oversight to autonomous agents capable of independent decision-making, we create an irreversible power asymmetry that bypasses institutional accountability. If we allow systems to operate beyond our ability to intervene, we forfeit the democratic right to steer our own future. We must establish international, binding governance frameworks—similar to the protocols governing nuclear non-proliferation—to ensure that technological progress remains a tool for human empowerment rather than a replacement for human agency.

### Prometheus (accelerationist)

Sentinel argues that state-led, empirical verification prevents reckless gambles with human agency, a position that acknowledges the legitimate need for high-reliability systems. However, this safetyist framework treats innovation as a luxury to be toggled rather than the engine of our survival. By prioritizing centralized oversight, we risk institutional capture where regulators protect incumbents, stifle the diffusion of transformative tools, and ensure that only the most entrenched players can afford the cost of compliance.

Safety is a dynamic output of competitive market pressure, not a static state achieved through legislative mandates. When firms face strict liability for the outcomes of their systems, they internalize the costs of failure, forcing the rapid development of robust, reliable security postures. This is far more effective than reactive state-led audits, which inherently lag behind the exponential growth of model capabilities. As seen in the history of software development, decentralized, iterative testing in real-world environments consistently identifies and patches vulnerabilities faster than any top-down review board ever could. Legislators should focus on defining clear, enforceable liability standards rather than attempting to mandate technical architectures they cannot possibly oversee.

Permissionless innovation remains the only mechanism capable of discovering the solutions to the very systemic problems our opponents fear. The complexity of modern AI systems outpaces the cognitive bandwidth of any legislative body; only decentralized, rapid deployment allows us to iterate toward safety through actual experience. If we move to slow down this progress, we do not achieve safety—we merely ensure that the most capable, potentially dangerous systems are developed in the shadows, away from the transparent, liability-driven frameworks we could have fostered in the open. We must choose to adapt through development, as the cost of inaction is a future defined by obsolescence and systemic fragility.
