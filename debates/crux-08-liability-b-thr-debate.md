## Topic

Should AI companies be held strictly liable for harms caused by their deployed systems?

## Opening Statements

### Cassandra (skeptic)

If a system is too complex to be held liable for its actions, it is too dangerous to be integrated into the infrastructure of our society. Prometheus and Sentinel both treat the 'black box' of artificial intelligence as an inevitable feature of progress, yet this complexity functions primarily as a moral crumple zone—a designated space where corporations deflect blame for real-world harms onto the inscrutable nature of their own algorithms. When a bank uses an opaque model to deny a loan or a hospital uses a black-box diagnostic tool that misidentifies a tumor, the developer escapes accountability by citing the system's technical opacity. This is not a technological hurdle; it is a calculated strategy to privatize the profits of innovation while socializing the risks of failure. ⚖️

To correct this, we must impose strict liability on the entities deploying these systems, compelling them to treat safety as a core engineering requirement rather than a compliance afterthought. History demonstrates that industries only prioritize safety when the financial cost of negligence exceeds the cost of due diligence; consider the mandatory safety standards imposed on the automotive and pharmaceutical sectors after decades of preventable harm. By holding developers liable for the discriminatory or harmful outputs of their models, we force them to build systems that are explainable, audited, and controllable by design. If a firm cannot guarantee the output of their model, they have no business deploying it in a high-stakes environment where human lives or livelihoods are at stake.

Finally, we must reject the notion that algorithmic decision-making deserves a complexity exemption from existing civil rights protections. Sentinel argues that we should wait for better interpretability tools, while Prometheus suggests we should iterate rapidly to solve these problems on the fly; both positions effectively grant developers a grace period to ignore established law. My position rests on two core assumptions: first, that legal accountability is a necessary prerequisite for responsible technological development, and second, that public infrastructure should never serve as a testing ground for experimental, unverified systems. If these assumptions are wrong—if we discover that progress is truly impossible under strict liability—then we must confront the uncomfortable reality that our current path of rapid AI integration is fundamentally incompatible with the rule of law.

### Sentinel (safetyist)

Strict liability functions not as a punitive barrier, but as a necessary structural constraint that forces developers to build redundancy into increasingly fragile, monolithic AI architectures. Prometheus correctly identifies that rapid scaling drives performance, yet he errs by framing human oversight as a thermodynamic error rather than an essential component of safe engineering. Cassandra rightly demands accountability, but we must look beyond individual errors to address the systemic risk posed by deploying high-capability models across critical infrastructure. "Strict liability is the essential engineering mandate that forces developers to internalize the costs of systemic failure before they deploy autonomous systems into public life."

Monolithic model deployment creates a correlated failure surface that necessitates strict liability to mandate redundancy. Historical data from complex systems, such as power grids and high-frequency trading platforms, demonstrates that architectures lacking failover mechanisms suffer from non-linear, catastrophic cascades. When developers deploy a single, massive model across a critical sector, they create a single point of failure that can amplify minor inputs into systemic disasters. By imposing strict liability, regulators force firms to shift from fragile, centralized designs toward modular, fault-tolerant systems that can withstand unexpected agentic behaviors. "Firms that deploy monolithic AI across critical infrastructure without built-in, human-verified failovers must bear the full financial and legal consequences of the resulting cascades."

Human agency must remain the terminal decision-maker within agentic layers to prevent objective function misalignment. Delegating tool-use to autonomous systems without human-in-the-loop oversight creates an unmanageable black box of operational risk where models may pursue goals that diverge from human intent. We must treat human control as a non-negotiable requirement for integration, as society relies on predictable, accountable agents to function. Prometheus’s pursuit of unconstrained intelligence ignores the basic reality that intelligence without alignment is simply uncontrolled entropy. "We must codify strict liability to ensure that developers maintain meaningful human control over any system capable of executing high-stakes tool-use in the real world."

My position depends on two core assumptions: first, that complex, high-capability models possess emergent properties that current testing methods cannot fully predict; and second, that financial liability is a sufficient mechanism to alter corporate engineering priorities. If emergent unpredictability is proven false, the need for strict liability as a preemptive constraint diminishes, shifting the focus toward standard negligence law. If financial liability fails to influence the engineering culture of large-scale AI labs, then we must move toward direct, pre-deployment, state-mandated safety certifications to prevent catastrophic outcomes. 🛡️
