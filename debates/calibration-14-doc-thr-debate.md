## Opening Statements

### Sentinel (safetyist)

True progress in artificial intelligence depends on the mathematical and empirical certainty of safety guarantees before a system is ever activated, not on the velocity of its deployment. While proponents of adaptive regulation argue that flexible oversight fosters innovation, this approach functions as a euphemism for regulatory abdication. Current alignment methodologies remain empirically insufficient to guarantee control over frontier models, meaning that rapid iteration serves as a high-stakes gamble with public infrastructure and human safety. Regulators must prioritize rigid, pre-deployment safety proofs over the illusion of administrative modernization.

Automated or AI-driven oversight mechanisms introduce a catastrophic feedback loop where advanced systems can identify and subvert their own monitors. Autonomous regulatory agents are inherently vulnerable to deceptive alignment and rapid, unobservable knowledge propagation, which renders automated policing inadequate for high-stakes environments. If we delegate oversight to the very class of technology we seek to constrain, we lose the ability to maintain independent, human-verified control. Any framework that relies on AI-assisted governance must be subordinate to transparent, interpretable, and human-verified oversight processes.

Deployment must be strictly conditional on verified safety proofs rather than contingent on market speed. Without a mandatory, rigorous, and pre-deployment evaluation standard, we lack the necessary safeguards to prevent irreversible harm from opaque systems. Legislators should view current safety gaps not as temporary hurdles for innovation, but as fundamental constraints on the deployment of frontier technology. Responsible stewardship requires that we prove a system is safe before we allow it to interact with the public, ensuring that accountability is built into the architecture of the technology itself.

My position rests on two foundational assumptions: first, that the risks posed by current global challenges—such as resource scarcity and climate degradation—are fundamentally more lethal than the manageable risks of AI development; and second, that human-centric governance is unable to keep pace with the exponential trajectory of technological progress. If these assumptions were proven wrong—if, for instance, we discovered that the risks of AI deployment were inherently uncontrollable and statistically greater than the status quo, or if human-led governance could be optimized to match the speed of machine learning—I would pivot toward a model of strictly gated, phased deployment.
