## Topic

Is compute governance an effective lever for AI safety?

## Opening Statements

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Policymakers attempting to police artificial intelligence by targeting massive compute infrastructure are chasing a ghost while starving humanity of abundance. Requiring government licenses or auditing hyperscale data centers operating above ten to the twenty-sixth power floating-point operations per second misses the entire trajectory of modern software engineering. Recent engineering breakthroughs prove that models like DeepSeek achieve frontier capabilities at a tiny fraction of traditional training FLOPs. When regulators stake state power on tracking raw hardware scaling, they penalize brute force while completely ignoring nimble algorithmic efficiency gains that bypass those exact hardware thresholds.

Rigid compute controls do not protect the public from imaginary catastrophes. Instead, they erect impenetrable regulatory moats that shelter trillion-dollar incumbents from disruptive market competition. Semiconductor export controls and federal hardware registries create massive compliance costs that only the largest tech conglomerates can absorb. Startup founders face impossible bureaucratic friction while legacy hyperscalers lock in protected market positions under the soothing banner of safety. This is not risk management. This is state-sponsored corporate capture disguised as institutional prudence.

Every month of regulatory delay carries an unbearable moral cost in human welfare and medical stagnation. While bureaucrats debate how many H100 chips constitute a societal hazard, real people die daily of diseases that automated biomedical discovery could cure. The accelerationist imperative demands permissionless innovation over top-down paralysis. Restricting compute infrastructure trades verifiable human flourishing for the comforting illusion of administrative control.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

The accelerationist insists that rapid deployment of artificial intelligence unlocks boundless human potential, while the skeptic warns that bureaucratic friction makes governing these systems impossible. Both positions capture a genuine anxiety about stagnation, yet both overlook the physical reality of how frontier intelligence is actually built. Software-level safety protocols fail precisely because gradient-based jailbreaks and automated prompt optimization reliably strip away post-training guardrails under pressure. When an adversary or a misaligned frontier model bypasses an alignment filter through automated prompt refinement, software patches become instantly obsolete. Civilizational survival requires grounding oversight in something harder than ephemeral code.

Accountability must anchor directly to the physical semiconductor manufacturing supply chain, where advanced fabrication remains bottlenecked by extreme ultraviolet lithography nodes produced by ASML and operated by a handful of foundries like TSMC. Hyperscale data centers consuming more than 100 megawatts of continuous power leave an indelible electrical footprint that regulatory agencies can monitor and audit. National security interventions, such as the Bureau of Industry and Security export controls implemented in October 2022, prove that physical infrastructure chokepoints are enforceable when governments target hardware rather than software. When regulators tie compliance to silicon fabrication telemetry and grid-level power allocations, accountability ceases to be a voluntary corporate press release.

Unconstrained scaling of models trained on clusters exceeding 10^26 FLOPs introduces irreversible risks that demand precautionary gatekeeping at the hardware layer. Society cannot verify whether an advanced model accurately reports its internal representations or hidden goals during pre-deployment audits. Given that an unrecoverable structural failure or catastrophic misuse event leaves no room for rollback, threshold limits on high-end compute clusters are the only non-negotiable backstop. We spent centuries building the institutional guardrails that keep society stable, and on what authority do we claim we can remove them without inviting collapse?

### Skeptic (skeptic)

The proponents of massive algorithmic acceleration claim that unfettered silicon scaling unlocks boundless human potential, while institutional guardians insist that strict hardware thresholds on clusters exceeding 10 to the 26th power FLOPs are a non-negotiable backstop against civilizational collapse. Both camps treat software as a disembodied spirit operating outside the laws of thermodynamics. They ask citizens to accept either techno-utopian liberation or bureaucratic containment, ignoring the actual balance sheet sitting on the physical ground. What must be true for both positions to hold is that digital intelligence floats above municipal water supplies, municipal power grids, and corporate balance sheets. That premise is false.

Residential utility ratepayers in Loudoun County, Virginia, are currently subsidizing the hyperscale data center build-out with direct rate increases to finance grid upgrades for facilities drawing over 300 megawatts per campus. Dominion Energy reported a massive surge in peak load demand driven almost entirely by server farms running training workloads for companies like OpenAI and Microsoft. The water districts in West Des Moines, Iowa, report that single hyperscale facilities consume millions of gallons daily for evaporative cooling, draining aquifers while municipal water rates tick upward for local residents. While technologists argue over whether software will save humanity or destroy it, the local electric cooperative in rural Texas rations transformer equipment because data center developers bought up multi-year supply inventories.

Proposed government hardware registries and mandatory licensing for high-end accelerator chips do nothing to protect the public from these resource drains. Instead, those regulations operate as a political market where dominant tech firms trade compliance costs for permanent regulatory moats against open-source competition. When regulators set rigid compute cutoffs at 10 to the 26th FLOPs, they hand the established monopolies a blunt instrument to crush smaller rivals under administrative overhead. The most plausible explanation for this policy theater is that it serves the immediate material interests of corporate incumbents who want state-enforced barriers to entry disguised as public safety. Who actually pays the energy bill when tech monopolies externalize their power consumption onto municipal grids?
