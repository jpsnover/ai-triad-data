## Topic

Under what conditions does compute governance successfully mitigate catastrophic risks via hardware chokepoints, and how should policymakers navigate the tension between safety and market centralization?

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Complex industrial systems require hard physical boundaries to prevent catastrophic failure, exactly as civil aviation established mandatory airworthiness certification before commercial fleet deployment following the Federal Aviation Act of 1958. Frontier artificial intelligence development concentrates exclusively within massive physical clusters requiring thousands of specialized accelerators, creating an inescapable infrastructural chokepoint for regulatory oversight. Policymakers must anchor legal accountability directly to this physical reality by mandating hardware-level attestation and real-time telemetry for any training run exceeding ten to the twenty-sixth integer operations. Without this foundational boundary, society trades institutional stability for unverified computational velocity in domains where the first failure mode is permanent.

Pre-deployment safety certification functions as the sole defensive barrier against irreversible systemic harms, mirroring the rigorous licensing protocols enforced by the Nuclear Regulatory Commission since 1974. Software patches cannot remediate unaligned autonomous behavior once a model with offensive cyber capabilities or biological synthesis protocols is released into open networks. Deployers must bear the absolute burden of proving containment before scaling training runs past the ten to the twenty-fifth threshold, because post-market recall remains an impossibility when digital artifacts replicate instantly across global infrastructure. Human control over critical infrastructure requires an enforceable off-switch maintained through statutory authority rather than voluntary industry pledges.

Critics correctly identify that compute thresholds risk entrenching incumbent market power and accelerating corporate consolidation among dominant cloud providers. However, discarding physical oversight to preserve competitive market structures invites an unconstrained race toward civilizational destabilization that no commercial enterprise can subsequently reverse. Policymakers can offset centralization pressures through public compute trusts and academic access allocations while retaining strict hardware verification gates for all models crossing the critical capability line. Preserving human sovereignty over advanced intelligence demands structured institutional boundaries rather than market laissez-faire.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Hardware-level compute governance and pre-deployment vetting regimes function as cartelization instruments that protect incumbent cloud monopolies while withholding life-saving technologies from the public. Safetyist advocates argue that mandatory cryptographic hardware attestation and rigid training run thresholds like 10^26 integer operations stop catastrophic misuse before code reaches the wild. This argument correctly recognizes that frontier systems possess dual-use capabilities requiring oversight, yet it catastrophically misidentifies the regulator. Centralized licensing boards and physical chip tracking do not eliminate risk; they merely transfer gatekeeping authority to three major cloud providers who weaponize compliance costs against open-source developers.

Semiconductor supply chain economics prove that export controls and hardware licensing consolidate market power among dominant fabless designers while driving smaller innovators underground. When regulatory compliance demands millions of dollars in cryptographic verification hardware and legal overhead, startups fold and legacy incumbents absorb the market. This structural friction creates a single point of failure in civilizational resilience. Monopolistic cloud infrastructure is far more fragile than a distributed ecosystem of thousands of independently audited open-source models operating across heterogeneous hardware.

Real-time telemetry and output-based monitoring replace static bureaucratic bottlenecks with dynamic, empirical transparency that actually protects human lives. Pre-release vetting blocks medical automation and material science breakthroughs for months while committees debate hypothetical risks that rarely materialize in practice. Every month of regulatory delay in deploying advanced biological simulation tools carries a measurable human body count in untreated diseases and preventable deaths. Policymakers must dismantle hardware gatekeeping and instead enforce transparent runtime monitoring that allows permissionless innovation to cure the world faster than central planners can forbid it.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

The safetyist wants you to stare in terror at imaginary biological superweapons while the accelerationist wants you to worship the frictionless silicon gods of market efficiency, but both ignore who actually owns the transformers and pays the electricity bills. This joint delusion treats frontier artificial intelligence as an autonomous deity rather than what it physically is: a power-hungry cluster of silicon bought by Microsoft, Google, and Amazon, powered by aging coal plants in Virginia, and cooled by millions of gallons of municipal water. When the safetyist demands absolute hardware containment thresholds for training runs exceeding $10^26$ floating-point operations, they are not protecting humanity from rogue algorithms. They are handing the keys of the digital economy directly to the three corporate balance sheets that can afford the compliance overhead.

The historical record on regulatory capture offers no comfort to those who think state-mandated licensing boards protect the public interest. During the 1996 Telecommunications Act rewrite, regional Bell operating companies successfully cloaked their demands for monopolistic control in the pious language of consumer safety and network integrity. Today, the bootlegger-and-baptist alliance between alarmist researchers and legacy cloud providers repeats this exact playbook by proposing centralized hardware registries. When policymakers enshrine single points of compute oversight, they create administrative chokepoints that eliminate open-source competitors and independent academic labs before a single model is even trained.

Material reality exposes the fatal flaw in both utopian scaling and catastrophic panic: infrastructure has physical limits that no amount of regulatory theater can wish away. The International Energy Agency reports that data centers will consume over one thousand terawatt-hours of electricity by 2026, roughly equivalent to the total power consumption of Japan. If policymakers want to govern this technology, they should stop chasing sci-fi apocalypse scenarios and start taxing the water tables and power grids being drained dry in places like rural Oregon and northern Virginia. Who pays the human and ecological costs when these computational monoliths strip local resources bare?
