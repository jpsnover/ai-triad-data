## Topic

AI systems in high-stakes decisions: healthcare triage and criminal justice

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Automated triage and sentencing systems dismantle human oversight. They replace named judges and doctors with opaque machine learning models. Hospital clinical directors and state sentencing commissions currently buy algorithms that deny life-saving medical care and dictate prison terms without a clear human author. When a proprietary model trained on health data gives a low priority score to a Black patient in an ER, no human clinician signs that denial. This missing human actor destroys the chain of legal liability that patients and defendants need when errors happen. Society spent centuries building constitutional due process and medical ethics to stop anonymous power from deciding who lives or goes to prison.

Pre-deployment testing must happen before any high-stakes predictive models are bought, because later lawsuits cannot undo permanent harm. Federal agencies like the Food and Drug Administration and the Department of Justice lack the legal power and forensic tools to audit deep neural networks before hospitals and courts use them. In the 2018 United States v. Loomis ruling, the Wisconsin Supreme Court allowed COMPAS risk scores in sentencing even though the code was secret, denying defendants the right to inspect the code that lengthened their prison terms. This creates a dangerous governance gap where private vendors hide proprietary weights behind trade secrets while public institutions pay the social costs of wrongful imprisonment and medical neglect. Safety requires software developers to prove their models pass strict error tests in controlled trials before any judge or doctor uses them.

Critics argue that human clinical and judicial judgment has severe implicit bias, making algorithmic triage a necessary fix rather than a loss of authority. Human bias in sentencing and diagnosis is well-documented, but human decisions can still be challenged through appeals, cross-examination, and direct peer review. Algorithms do not fix bias; they lock historical discrimination into mathematical permanence while hiding systemic failure behind computer code. Replacing flawed human discretion with automated bigotry without a rollback tool just scales injustice at machine speed. Real reform means fixing human institutions directly instead of outsourcing moral judgment to unverified software that nobody can override in real time.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Deploying predictive algorithms in high-stakes human decisions does not stop bias. It industrializes bias behind trade-secret walls while shifting blame from institutions to software. When hospital directors and sentencing commissions adopt automated triage tools, they launder human prejudice through proprietary machine learning models. The Department of Justice and the Food and Drug Administration fail to regulate these systems because corporate vendors hide training data behind intellectual property claims. Software vendors profit by selling fake neutrality, while vulnerable patients and defendants bear the physical and financial cost of false positive risk scores.

Algorithmic decision tools in healthcare and criminal justice encode and amplify structural discrimination through skewed training data. A 2019 study published in Science showed that a commercial healthcare allocation algorithm used across US hospitals systematically discriminated against Black patients. It used past healthcare costs as a proxy for medical need. Because unequal insurance access meant lower historical spending on Black patients, the software rated them as healthier than white patients with identical chronic illnesses. Pretrial risk assessment tools deployed by state sentencing commissions use the same circular logic. They ingest arrest records generated by decades of selective policing, then output high-risk labels that justify more police patrols in those exact neighborhoods.

Post-deployment accountability is destroyed by circular feedback loops. Predictive policing and sentencing recommendations generate the very arrest data used to validate them. When a recidivism risk tool recommends pretrial detention for a defendant based on past neighborhood arrest volume, that detention disrupts employment and housing. This disruption empirically increases later arrest probability. The algorithm treats its own punitive intervention as proof of its predictive accuracy. Federal procurement guidelines currently lack any enforcement mechanism to audit these feedback loops in clinical or judicial settings. Hospital administrators and judges accept vendor output as scientific fact because it shields them from personal liability when a patient dies or a defendant languishes in jail.

Mandating public transparency and bias audits will not fix this failure as long as private vendors control the source code and training weights. Requiring accountability demands independent, mandatory pre-deployment testing by civil rights technical experts with subpoena power over proprietary datasets. If the FDA can demand clinical trials for a drug before market entry, federal procurement rules must demand equivalent validation for software that decides who gets ICU beds or prison sentences. Until federal regulators tie public healthcare and justice funding to open-source algorithmic auditing, deployment remains an unregulated corporate experiment on marginalized populations.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Freezing high-stakes algorithms out of hospitals and sentencing commissions protects failing human baselines under the false flag of caution. The Safetyist wants named human actors to keep absolute control, while the Skeptic demands complete bias elimination before deployment. Both arguments share a fatal flaw: they treat human judgment as neutral while demanding math perfection from machines. Human triage nurses miss critical sepsis indicators up to twenty percent of the time in busy emergency departments. Human judges impose racial sentencing disparities that federal sentencing commissions document year after year. Banning AI triage and sentencing tools does not protect vulnerable populations. It locks in human error and calls it safety.

Dynamic telemetry and real-time output monitoring replace static bans with checkable safety standards. Clinical directors and sentencing commissions should use risk-stratified audit protocols that track error rates across demographic groups continuously. The Department of Justice and the Food and Drug Administration can enforce mandatory pre-deployment auditing standards modeled on existing medical device clearance pathways under Section 510(k). This framework catches disparate impact and model drift in real time instead of waiting for a big lawsuit. Speed requires continuous measurement, not administrative paralysis.

Automated compliance monitoring and permit processing clear public sector backlogs that currently trap vulnerable populations in bureaucratic purgatory. Municipal housing agencies and public health departments face millions of delayed applications because human processing capacity has collapsed under administrative overhead. Deploying targeted AI agents to handle routine cases reduces wait times by up to eighty percent in pilot jurisdictions like Estonia and select US municipal districts. Public administrators gain the processing capacity to serve marginalized communities without cutting corners on oversight. Stopping this automation to prevent hypothetical harms imposes a direct, measurable cost on the people waiting for help.

Continuous audit tools and demographic error tracking are necessary to measure system drift, and clinical directors must use them to maintain visibility. Granting this administrative necessity does not save the underlying model, because telemetry measures past casualties rather than preventing unrecoverable failures before deployment. When a sentencing commission or hospital triage unit relies on continuous monitoring to catch demographic skews mid-deployment, the feedback loop acts as a post-hoc autopsy report rather than a protective shield. Discovering that an algorithm systematically misallocated intensive care resources or triggered wrongful pretrial detention after thousands of automated decisions executed offers zero remedy to the patients who died or the defendants whose liberty was permanently broken.

Separating ex-post telemetry from ex-ante deployment gating reveals the structural error in treating monitoring as a substitute for hard legal and technical thresholds. Aviation safety manuals classify serious incidents based on the chance of an accident and whether catastrophe was avoided only by luck, demanding absolute airworthiness certification before any aircraft carries passengers. Clinical care and criminal justice demand the same standard of prior verification, because human lives and constitutional liberties cannot function as live debugging environments for unverified machine learning models. Post-deployment telemetry catalogs the debris after the collision, whereas genuine governance requires blocking the vehicle from leaving the hangar until its safety boundaries are mathematically and legally proven.

Without strict pre-deployment legal liability attached to named developers and deployers, continuous auditing collapses into performative compliance theater where software vendors merely document their own systemic failures. Existing regulatory frameworks like the EU AI Act and domestic civil rights proposals establish that high-risk systems governing parole eligibility, healthcare allocation, and housing require mandatory pre-market evaluations and binding safety certifications before reaching the public. Institutional guardians must reject the premise that dynamic telemetry can compensate for the absence of a hard pre-deployment gate. When complex human systems remove their protective boundaries to accommodate rapid software iteration, they invite institutional collapse under the weight of uncorrectable errors.
