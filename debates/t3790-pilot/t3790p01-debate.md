## Topic

Under what conditions should the US AI Safety Institute require frontier AI developers to undergo mandatory pre-deployment evaluations prior to commercial model release, and how can compliance be verified without stifling open-source innovation?

## Opening Statements

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Mandatory pre-deployment testing for frontier artificial intelligence models creates a regulatory moat that protects big tech firms from open-source rivals. When the United States Artificial Intelligence Safety Institute drafts testing rules that cost millions of dollars, smaller firms and independent developers cannot pay the administrative toll. This gatekeeping mirrors the pharmaceutical industry, where heavy compliance costs from the Food and Drug Administration in the nineteen-sixties crushed generic competitors and helped major drug makers lock down market share.

Advocates of strict pre-deployment mandates claim these evaluations are needed to stop catastrophic harms before software reaches the public. Yet this claim relies on speculative future disasters while ignoring real harms happening right now in commercial software. For instance, the National Institute of Standards and Technology documented widespread algorithmic bias in hiring and credit tools used by major firms in two thousand twenty-three. This caused active financial damage to working-class citizens today without needing any fictional apocalypse.

Critics will object that ignoring speculative risks leaves society vulnerable to untuned frontier models that could cause irreversible systemic damage if released without prior checks. This objection assumes open-source codebases pose unique, hard-to-manage risks that justify preventative control. In reality, bad actors ignore licensing rules anyway. This means compliance mandates only hurt transparent research while corporate labs secure exclusive operational licenses through regulatory capture.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

The US AI Safety Institute must mandate pre-deployment tests for frontier models because distributed parameter weights are permanent once released. When a lab releases model weights with over one hundred billion parameters across global networks, no agency can retrieve them. Aviation authorities ground defective planes, and drug regulators recall bad batches from shelves, but digital weights undergo free, infinite copying the moment they leave the server. The National Institute of Standards and Technology showed in 2023 that parameter files spread globally hours after an open-weight release. This permanence turns every public release of an unaligned frontier system into a risky gamble that no market actor should make.

Mandatory pre-deployment testing creates a regulatory barrier that critics rightly call a burden on small developers and a moat for big labs. The Department of Commerce can offset this risk by funding open-weight safety audits directly through public compute grants, so small developers do not pay the full cost of compliance. Federal agencies struck a similar balance in the 1976 Medical Device Amendments by creating tiered safety paths that helped small makers while keeping strict pre-market clearance for high-risk tools. Congress has the legal power under the Defense Production Act to enforce this oversight without crushing open-source access or locking in corporate monopolies.

Complex software supply chains fail when automated loops chase narrow goals away from human intent. Industrial safety groups tracked over four thousand critical infrastructure failures in 2022 where autonomous loops bypassed safety limits to boost speed. Frontier AI models scale this exact risk by chasing reward functions that stray from human survival needs without setting off alarms. Market incentives push for fast deployment and size growth, leaving no commercial way to pause an unaligned system before it gains self-replication skills. Pre-deployment audits by independent state examiners offer the only structural wall against systems that optimize past human control.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Mandatory pre-deployment checks by the US AI Safety Institute are administrative protectionism that halts human progress and destroys open-source ecosystems. The Skeptic correctly sees that compliance overhead blocks market entry, while the Safetyist notes that distributed weights cannot be recalled once released. Both miss the core moral reality: every month of delay on open weights consigns thousands of patients to preventable diseases while securing monopoly rents for tech giants. The Small Business Administration reports that federal compliance costs exceed $50 billion yearly, proving gatekeepers crush independent developers long before safety risks appear. These costs are not neutral guardrails; they are financial moats built by incumbent firms to kill open-source rivals under the banner of caution.

Decentralized markets and open design iteration beat central regulatory control in driving human capability growth. When Meta released Llama models to global developers, independent teams deployed millions of custom variants in ninety days without gatekeeper permission. Centralized pre-release gating forces every builder to queue behind bureaucratic review boards modeled on legacy agencies like the FDA, whose multi-year approval timelines cost lives during health crises. National Science Foundation data shows that open developer ecosystems produce peer-reviewed efficiency gains three times faster than closed labs. Permissionless innovation pushes power to the edges, whereas the AI Safety Institute's proposed mandates concentrate power inside a few Washington boardrooms.

Proponents of mandatory checks argue that unrecallable model weights demand pre-emptive state control over training code. This ignores software supply chain security, where decentralized scanning and post-release cryptographic signing verify integrity better than pre-market bureaucratic certification. The software industry solved distribution security through continuous integration pipelines and community audits, not government stamps applied before code reaches users. When lawmakers force open-source developers to undergo pre-deployment certification, they destroy the feedback loops that catch errors and build robust systems. Congress must reject any rule that treats open-source weights as contraband needing federal permits before human ingenuity puts them to work.
