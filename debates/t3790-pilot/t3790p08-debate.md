## Topic

Under what conditions should frontier AI labs reallocate capital from capability scaling toward mechanistic interpretability research to meet compliance standards?

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Frontier artificial intelligence labs must shift at least thirty percent of their compute and cash budgets from scaling raw skills to mechanistic interpretability research. Congress and safety groups like the National Institute of Standards and Technology must back this rule with mandatory pre-market licenses. Hidden internal states in huge models mean normal tests cannot catch lethal flaws before launch. History shows that rushing complex engineering without rules leads to collapse, just like the 2008 financial crash or early aviation before Federal Aviation Administration rules. We took centuries to build guardrails that keep society stable. Why do you think we can drop them now?

Critics say moving cash to safety will hurt local labs when rival nations lack these rules. This point fails because a rogue or broken frontier model causes total loss, which no market lead can fix. The Department of Commerce has the power under the Defense Production Act to tie chip exports and cluster permits to safety audits. A strict rule stops any single lab from winning a commercial edge by skipping safety checks.

Mechanistic interpretability is the only way to read neural network weights and check goals before launch. Big labs spend over ninety percent of their cash on raw compute while treating safety as an academic side project. State safety groups must check these budget reports every quarter and halt training runs that fail to decode internal circuits. Without this shift, humans lose the power to stop or override advanced autonomous systems once they launch.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Frontier AI labs must speed up capability scaling. Market performance and output tests show ground-truth results that bureaucrats cannot manufacture.

Safetyist argues that labs must move at least 30 percent of their compute and capital budgets from scaling to mechanistic interpretability research to secure black-box models. That position captures a genuine desire for predictability. Yet it assumes internal code audits catch dangerous skills better than empirical API testing and runtime telemetry.

Diverting billions of dollars into speculative interpretability research taxes technological progress without giving clear compliance metrics.

Forcing labs to shift capital toward unproven internal inspection tools hurts economic competitiveness and tech growth. Past market performance shows that decentralized development and unhindered compute scaling drive rapid societal abundance and medical breakthroughs.

Regulatory rules that freeze development out of hypothetical fear hurt people waiting for automated cures. Congress must reject any compliance standard that trades verifiable real-world output for unverified bureaucratic oversight.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

The safetyist framework demands a thirty percent capital shift toward mechanistic interpretability to peer inside black-box neural networks before deployment. That argument uses a clear logic: if no one can inspect model circuits, alignment failures stay unpreventable as models grow past trillions of parameters. Yet this debate over internal neural visibility treats AI as a pure software risk while ignoring hard material bottlenecks in every server farm. Labs do not work in a vacuum where code safety is their main limit. They face immediate physical limits like local power delays, grid backlog, and millions of gallons of daily water use to cool specialized chips.

Mandatory pre-deployment interpretability certification acts as a regulatory capture tool that builds high entry barriers for small rivals. History in the drug industry shows how complex rules concentrate market power by pushing out small firms while big giants absorb the fixed costs. If the government mandates internal circuit audits by executive order, startups cannot pay the compute overhead to prove safety compliance. Big tech firms will gladly back these standards because mandated interpretability research acts as a moat to guard their market lead.

Demanding huge capital shifts into internal model checks assumes that market competition and current rules fail to handle corporate harms without special internal mandates. That point fails when physical limits like regional grid limits already slow down how fast firms build new clusters. Policymakers must focus on real resource use and labor arbitrage instead of speculative internal transparency metrics that help incumbent monopolies. When a data center draws hundreds of megawatts from a strained local grid, the public harm shows in kilowatt-hours and water bills, not in unread neural weights. If regulators want to rule labs well, who pays the local power upgrade costs while staff audit internal weight matrices?
