## Topic

Under what regulatory conditions can the Bureau of Industry and Security set compute thresholds and export controls that effectively mitigate frontier AI risks without crippling open-source hardware developers and allied cloud providers?

## Opening Statements

### Skeptic (skeptic)

The Bureau of Industry and Security uses compute thresholds to protect domestic tech monopolies, not national security. Federal export rules set in October 2022 restrict sales of advanced chips over set interconnect bandwidth limits to foreign markets. These rules hand the entire high-performance computing market straight to three American cloud providers. Big tech firms secure exclusive hardware access while smaller open-source developers drown in licensing fees and paperwork.

Critics warn that loose rules let advanced chips leak to foes via shell companies. Yet this compliance theater just masks domestic market capture. Bureaucratic licensing hits small firms that lack legal teams, while tech giants win blanket exemptions and multi-billion-dollar federal subsidies via the CHIPS Act. Open-source hardware groups cannot survive these administrative costs. This market consolidation leaves infrastructure control in fewer hands than before.

Physical resource extraction exposes the empty rhetoric of AI safety and export control alike. Training a single frontier model uses millions of gallons of water in places like drought-stressed Iowa and hot data hubs in Oregon. Compliance officers ignore these ecological bottlenecks while chasing abstract risks from corporate marketing decks. Regulating code and silicon while ignoring local water tables and power grids protects corporate balance sheets at public expense.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

The Bureau of Industry and Security uses compute limits and export rules to lock in market power. The Skeptic is right that license delays hurt small firms while tech giants get exemptions through the CHIPS Act. That point hits the mark on regulatory capture. Yet just critiquing the rules leaves the system in place while delay hurts real people.

Bandwidth caps of 600 gigabytes per second and compute cutoffs from the October 2022 rules crush open-source hardware teams. These rules create flat costs that small inventors cannot pay. Only big cloud firms with huge legal teams survive this filter. The Bureau of Industry and Security thus acts to guard cloud monopolies.

Export controls do not stop rivals from getting hardware; they starve open groups. When agencies block chips below 4800 bits per second per watt, they kill indie research groups without corporate backing. This top-down setup concentrates power in three Silicon Valley firms and delays medical breakthroughs that AI systems could bring. Every month of delay lets preventable diseases take lives that open tech could save.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

The Bureau of Industry and Security sets compute limits to stop frontier AI risks before rogue models break human control. Critics argue these rules just protect domestic monopolies and punish small developers. That point has some truth: federal chokepoints concentrate power and create heavy rules for allied cloud providers. Yet that friction is a price we must pay. Without it, open-source spread will destroy our ability to stop runaway systems.

Regulators enforce this boundary using hardware tracking and chip factory chokepoints. Under the Export Administration Regulations, the Department of Commerce tracks special chips like Nvidia H100 units once clusters pass certain math limits. Central data centers offer the only real leverage point because software copies multiply forever while silicon needs physical factories. Without these checks, bad actors will bypass safety rules for good.

Opponents claim strict hardware controls drive open-source developers underground and fail to stop decentralized spread. That objection ignores how physical supply chains actually work. Advanced manufacturing requires extreme precision found in fewer than ten global foundries like TSMC. Lawmakers can catch illegal clusters at these physical bottlenecks long before models can copy themselves. We spent centuries building guardrails to keep society stable. On what authority do you claim we can drop them without inviting collapse?
