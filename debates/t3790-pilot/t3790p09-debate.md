## Opening Statements

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Artificial intelligence automation is the engine required to end human toil and reach post-scarcity abundance, not cause a job crisis. Developed economies face a dropping work-to-retirement ratio that will hit 2.5 workers per retiree by 2050, based on United Nations data. Protecting old wage-labor through strict rules forces society to break down under an aging population. Federal labor agencies must reject rules that freeze tech use and instead speed up machine autonomy to keep output high.

Critics argue that fast automation causes severe wealth gaps and job losses that markets cannot absorb. That objection ignores the history of past industrial shifts where farm workers moved to higher-value jobs. The Department of Labor should focus on cutting friction instead of funding decline. Each month of delay keeps an inefficient system that harms the workers protectionists want to shield.

State labor rules break up the national market and threaten our edge against global rivals. Congress must pass federal laws modeled on the Telecommunications Act of 1996 to set uniform rules for automation. Uniform federal standards stop local rules from blocking vital productivity gains. Fast deployment is the top moral goal for human well-being.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

The Accelerationist argues that fewer workers and major labor shortages make fast AI automation a must. This view accepts real population aging, but it is wrong to think that loose rules protect social order. Complex human systems need strict limits to survive. Taking away those limits leads straight to collapse.

We need the Department of Labor to set firm limits on AI power because voluntary corporate rules have failed in fields like civil engineering and aviation. When the Federal Aviation Administration let Boeing check its own 737 MAX flight software, the failure killed 346 people. Unchecked AI put into factories and offices brings the same risks unless public officials hold a strict off-switch.

Owners take the profits from automation while leaving towns and state job agencies to handle the job losses. Past shifts in capital and labor during early factory growth show that wild tech use hurts workers instead of sharing wealth fairly. As digital tracking gets cheaper, bosses have more reason to police jobless workers than to train them.

State agencies cannot stop AI use when federal law takes away local labor rules, leaving workers helpless against algorithmic job loss. Congress must pass mandatory automation impact tariffs modeled on the Occupational Safety and Health Act of 1970 to pay for permanent wage insurance and to keep veto power over risky tech. We spent centuries building guardrails to keep society stable; why should tech firms think we can remove them without inviting ruin?

### Skeptic (skeptic)

The Department of Labor and state agencies face a corporate strategy disguised as progress. Firms use automation not to solve labor shortages, but to bypass unions and lower wages. Accelerationists argue that low worker-to-retiree ratios force rapid tech adoption. Safetyists argue that federal automation impact tariffs modeled after the 1970 Occupational Safety and Health Act are needed to fund wage insurance. Both views hold partial truths. Yet they ignore the real incentives driving employers to break labor solidarity through software monitoring and job replacement. Both views could only be true if corporate tech deployment served public demographic goals instead of private profit extraction.

Data center growth in rural Iowa and northern Virginia shows the physical reality behind the marketing. These centers use millions of gallons of water each day while replacing union manufacturing and office workers. State workforce boards lack the legal power or money to audit these investments. This leaves towns to pay for infrastructure costs while tech monopolies take the financial windfalls. When firms replace human workers with automated systems, they push risk onto the public while keeping the productivity gains. Congress must stop letting developers self-report AI safety. Instead, Congress should tax large-scale model training to fund public worker infrastructure and independent oversight.

Critics argue that heavy compute taxes and strict labor rules will push AI development overseas to places with weak rules. This would hurt domestic tech competitiveness. This objection assumes the state cannot tax compute infrastructure without corruption. It ignores the 1954 Internal Revenue Code, which successfully governed heavy industrial capital assets. State employment agencies can collect these taxes directly at the physical data center where electricity grids and water mains enter the building. If we fail to intervene through compute taxes and union oversight, automation will permanently lock in inequality under the name of inevitability.

### Safetyist (safetyist) → Accelerationist
\textcolor{NavyBlue}{\textit{Focus: Accelerationist's claim that safety regulations create an artificial scarcity tax (AN-10) and the unaddressed Boeing 737 MAX regulatory failure (AN-4)}}


Treating pre-deployment safety verification as an artificial scarcity tax misdiagnoses how decentralized software networks actually externalize systemic fragility onto the labor market. When capital owners deploy automated systems across global networks without mandatory safety-gated deployment, the resulting structural shocks bypass traditional containment mechanisms and hit organized labor directly. Municipalities and state workforce boards absorb the infrastructure costs of mass clerical and blue-collar displacement while tech monopolies capture the financial windfalls, echoing how the International Energy Agency predicts electricity demand for AI-optimized data centers will quadruple by 2030 while local communities bear the grid capacity burden. Requiring rigorous third-party audits and automation impact tariffs is not a protectionist tollbooth for legacy employers; it is a necessary firewall against unconstrained wealth extraction that destabilizes entire industrial sectors.

The claim that decentralized software scaling makes rigid institutional oversight obsolete ignores the reality that network effects amplify, rather than isolate, operational failures. Where closed physical hardware loops in aviation contained localized risks, distributed software models propagate edge-case failures simultaneously across millions of deployment endpoints, instantly undermining collective bargaining agreements and wage structures in industries ranging from manufacturing to logistics. As the Algorithmic Accountability Act of 2022 demonstrates through its mandate for systematic impact assessments, complex technological systems require enforceable liability standards rather than developer self-reporting precisely because software contagion moves faster than market self-correction can respond. Without binding pre-deployment certification and dedicated compute taxes funding permanent workforce transition infrastructure, workers bear the uncompensated downside of rapid software iteration.

Demanding that accelerationists provide an operational rollback mechanism for distributed autonomous systems exposes the fatal flaw in market-driven deployment models. If an unconstrained model update systematically displaces 40% of a unionized clerical workforce within a single fiscal quarter, the market provides no mechanism to reverse the structural harm or restore the decimated tax base of the affected municipality. Establishing strict liability regimes and mandatory automation impact tariffs modeled after the Occupational Safety and Health Act of 1970 ensures that capital owners retain ultimate accountability for the labor market disruptions their tools create. Without a clear, enforceable authority to halt dangerous software deployments before they reach scale, the promise of permissionless innovation amounts to little more than a corporate license to externalize social ruin.
