## Topic

Under what conditions can binding treaty obligations for frontier AI safety standards balance global risk mitigation against the costs of regulatory capture by domestic tech incumbents?

## Opening Statements

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Binding international AI safety treaties act as regulatory moats. They protect domestic tech monopolies and block life-saving innovations through bureaucratic control. When states draft rules like FDA drug approvals, they add fixed costs that exceed one hundred million dollars for each frontier training run. This red tape destroys independent developers and open-source projects before their code reaches users. Tech giants like Microsoft and Google back these treaties because government paperwork kills garage competition.

Open-source groups offer better safety checks than closed regulatory silos. Linux proved that thousands of independent eyes fix security flaws faster than any single corporate board. When Meta released Llama weights to the public, hundreds of university labs checked safety vectors at once without waiting for UN bureaucrats. Centralized treaty enforcement concentrates risk inside three corporate boardrooms while pretending to manage global safety.

Delaying AI carries a human body count that safety advocates ignore. Holding back automated drug discovery tools for five years dooms millions of patients to preventable deaths from cancer and drug-resistant bugs. The FDA already delays medical treatments for decades with red tape while people die on waiting lists. International safety treaties bring this exact bureaucratic paralysis to artificial intelligence under the false banner of civilizational survival.

Independent software groups and university clusters enforce security through transparent, peer-reviewed model tests instead of opaque government certificates. The United States Congress must reject any international treaty that gives the Department of Commerce monopoly power over compute licenses. Market competition through open distribution delivers real safety via radical transparency, not state-enforced stagnation.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Binding global safety treaties can stop major disasters without locking in tech monopolies. We just need to check what models can actually do instead of burying small firms in rules. Complex tech needs mandatory safety checks before release to stop final, bad failures. Past rules in aviation and drug fields show that outside safety reviews stop system crashes without killing off smaller firms.

The Federal Aviation Administration type certification process demands strict checks before a plane carries people. This sets a basic safety floor that helps the whole transit network.

Big firms take over markets because of high setup costs, not basic safety rules. We need safe zones for open-weight models to keep the market open. When rule costs tie to massive compute budgets over $10^{26}$ training FLOPs, small builders face walls they cannot scale. Standardized safety audits open to university labs and small firms can split real technical risk checks from big firm lobbying tricks. The European Union Artificial Intelligence Act sets tiered rules that risk pricing out open-source builders unless explicit rules protect free, non-commercial models.

We stop both total catastrophe and unfair market lock-in by tying safety checks to what a model can do, not how big the company is. Outside auditors working for a global watch group can test model weights for dangerous self-copy skills without demanding billions in corporate overhead. Open-weight builders who add standard safety filters need legal safe zones from later misuse blame to keep open-source groups alive. Governments must tie frontier releases to clear safety proofs instead of corporate size to keep human control over advanced systems absolute.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

The accelerationist insists that markets will weed out bad software. The safetyist warns that without global treaties, rogue labs will cause a catastrophe. Both ignore who pays for compliance. When the Commerce Department sets export controls on advanced chips, three multinational firms take over the verification pipeline. International safety standards do not stop risk. They build a financial wall that small innovators cannot climb.

Global treaties act as permission slips that block open-source rivals under the guise of risk management. Rules like cryptographic watermarks and mandatory audits demand high costs that only monopolies can handle. The European Union Artificial Intelligence Act costs over ten million euros per baseline model. This starves independent developers before they launch. Big tech firms lobby for these exact limits because legal moats protect market share better than trade secrets.

Independent audit tools cannot survive when safety gates rely on corporate self-reporting and closed doors. Public oversight must separate safety enforcement from compute monopolies by funding open-source audit tools directly. If governments fail to separate rules from market gatekeepers, treaties will just legalize corporate consolidation while leaving the core technology unmonitored.
