## Topic

Under what conditions should the U.S. AI Safety Institute mandate pre-deployment safety evaluations for frontier models, and how can regulators balance national security safeguards against open-source developer access?

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

The United States AI Safety Institute must require formal safety checks before developers release frontier models that use over 10 to the 26th integer operations. Complex systems need binding safety rules because after-the-fact patches cannot undo a major failure once a model is out. Congress set safety standards through the Federal Aviation Administration in the Federal Aviation Act of 1958 to keep unverified designs out of public airspace. Advanced artificial intelligence needs the same safeguard before companies release systems that outgrow human control.

Critics argue that mandatory pre-market gates cause regulatory capture and starve open-source developers of needed resources. Yet national security does not gain from the unchecked spread of dual-use weights that bypass accountability. The Commerce Department enforces export controls under the Export Control Reform Act of 2018 precisely because tech leaks threaten sovereign stability. Open-source ecosystems need clear safety baselines instead of a total pass from liability when model skills cross into dangerous territory.

Regulators must check capabilities during training when risky traits first appear. The National Institute of Standards and Technology reported that voluntary industry pledges fail to stop unexpected capability jumps during large compute runs. Independent auditors must test safety controls before firms grant outside access to core model settings. Congress has the power to fund this oversight and fine non-compliant firms through civil penalties run by the Department of Justice.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

The Safetyist argues that the National Institute of Standards and Technology must test all frontier AI systems above 10^26 integer operations before release to stop biological or cyber disasters. That view correctly sees the need for public checks, but it treats code like planes under the 1958 Federal Aviation Act. The Department of Commerce stops certain exports through the Bureau of Industry and Security, but rigid rules always protect big tech firms while starving open-source builders of cash. When officials freeze model-weight sharing under the Export Control Reform Act, they do not stop risk; they just hand global market lead to foreign rivals in Beijing and Shenzhen.

Forcing centralized safety checks creates costs that crush the open developer ecosystem and the lives abundance would have saved. Small startups cannot pay millions for audits forced by Washington bosses. The National Science Foundation notes that open-source code drives over eighty percent of modern machine learning use, powering drug discovery tools at firms like Insilico Medicine. Slowing down open-weight releases delays clinical trials for rare diseases by years. Who gives state agencies the moral right to trade human lives today for guessed risks tomorrow?

Real data from the European Union Artificial Intelligence Act rollout shows that rigid rules hurt small builders while corporate lobbyists write the loopholes. The Department of Energy labs already hold enough compute power to test open models through runtime monitoring instead of stopping them at the start line. Officials must swap static aviation-style rules for flexible systems that protect model weight sharing. Decentralized trial and error beats central planning every time, bringing medical cures and labor relief faster than any state committee ever could.
