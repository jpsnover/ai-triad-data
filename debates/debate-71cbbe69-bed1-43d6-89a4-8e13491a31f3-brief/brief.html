The Question

The United States Congress should establish a National AI Injury Compensation Program (NAIICP), modeled on the National Vaccine Injury Compensation Program (VICP), to grant limited liability immunity to AI developers in exchange for a mandatory excise tax and adherence to tiered federal safety standards.

Time horizon: near-to-medium term legislative implementation

  • developer liability shielding vs tort victim access to justice
  • federal safety standard rigidity vs rapid pace of AI innovation
  • tax-funded socialized risk vs private market accountability
  • administrative efficiency of compensation funds vs traditional court litigation

Empirical Cruxes

Congress should replace legacy tort paralysis with a National AI Injury Compensation Program funded by a 1.5 percent excise tax on frontier compute providers.
  • If yes → Opponents must accept that administrative funding models and excise taxes successfully replace legacy tort systems without externalizing systemic risks.
  • If no → Proponents must acknowledge that administrative compensation programs fail to substitute for the deterrent and investigative functions of tort law.
Hardware-level actuarial telemetry replaces dead-letter court filings with live error-rate tracking that catches algorithmic failures the millisecond they occur.
  • If yes → The safetyist and skeptic must concede that automated telemetry successfully captures both computational faults and systemic non-computational harms.
  • If no → The accelerationist must concede that hardware-level telemetry is blind to systemic proxy discrimination and requires complementary enforcement mechanisms.
When the Federal Aviation Administration delegated certification authority to manufacturers, the Boeing 737 MAX disaster killed 346 people because regulatory oversight was hollowed out by industry self-regulation.
  • If yes → Proponents of industry self-regulation must accept that delegation without rigorous oversight directly leads to catastrophic safety failures.
  • If no → Skeptics of delegation must concede that industry self-regulation can operate safely without causing catastrophic regulatory failures.
Developers trade absolute liability immunity for mandatory hardware-level logging and excise contributions that fund immediate, no-fault victim relief.
  • If yes → Opponents must acknowledge that structured log exchanges and excise contributions provide a workable equivalent to judicial remedies.
  • If no → Proponents must concede that liability immunity leaves significant categories of non-computational harm entirely uncompensated.

Values Crux(es)

Lawmakers must reject liability shields that trade away the right to sue for administrative payouts funded by industry excise taxes.
  • If yes → The accelerationist and skeptic must concede that administrative alternatives are fundamentally inadequate for protecting civil rights.
  • If no → The safetyist must concede that alternative compensation mechanisms can operate effectively alongside or in place of traditional litigation.

These are values choices — not resolvable by evidence.

Questions to Ask Yourself

  • How do administrative payouts protect civil rights compared to traditional tort litigation?
  • Will federal safety standards be too rigid to accommodate the rapid pace of AI innovation?
