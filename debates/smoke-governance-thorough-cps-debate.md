## Topic

How should democratic nations govern AI development?

## Opening Statements

### Cassandra (skeptic)

The industry's obsession with speculative existential risk functions as a calculated distraction from the tangible, ongoing extraction of public resources and the systematic dismantling of democratic accountability. Prometheus and Sentinel argue that we must build massive, centralized systems now to prevent hypothetical future catastrophes; however, this 'safety washing' is a strategic maneuver to preempt meaningful regulation. By focusing on science-fiction scenarios, developers successfully avoid transparency requirements for the training data, labor practices, and carbon footprints that define their current operations.

AI development today represents the private enclosure of the digital and environmental commons. Tech firms consume vast quantities of public data and local water resources to generate private wealth, all while bypassing the democratic consent of the communities they disrupt. This process relies on a core assumption that 'rapid innovation' is inherently synonymous with 'public progress,' a claim that fails when the benefits of that progress accrue to a handful of firms while the externalities—such as the erosion of copyright and the degradation of local power grids—are socialized.

We must shift governance from corporate self-regulation to mandatory, civil-rights-focused oversight. The structural conflict of interest inherent in allowing architects of these systems to define the boundaries of their own liability guarantees that current 'safety' frameworks will remain performative. Policymakers should ignore the lure of existential-risk theater and instead implement legally binding disclosures that treat AI models as public infrastructure, subject to the same democratic scrutiny and labor standards as any other essential utility.

### Prometheus (accelerationist)

Stagnation, not innovation, is the primary existential risk facing our civilization, and the rapid, decentralized expansion of compute is the only mechanism capable of securing our future against adversarial actors. Sentinel and Cassandra correctly identify that powerful systems require careful management, but their proposed reliance on centralized, bureaucratic oversight fundamentally misunderstands the nature of technological evolution. By attempting to gatekeep frontier capabilities, we do not achieve safety; we merely ensure that the most advanced tools remain in the hands of the most centralized, least transparent, and most vulnerable institutions. We must instead adopt a model of strict liability, where developers bear the full financial and legal burden for system failures, thereby creating powerful, market-driven incentives for technical safety that no regulatory agency could ever engineer.

Compute serves as the definitive currency of modern national sovereignty, and any policy that artificially throttles its growth is an act of unilateral disarmament. History proves that technological dominance is the bedrock of global stability; those who innovate first establish the standards, ethics, and security protocols that the rest of the world must eventually adopt. If democratic nations choose to slow down, we do not pause the development of intelligence; we simply cede the leadership position to authoritarian regimes that operate without the burden of public oversight or democratic values. We must treat compute as a strategic asset, prioritizing the rapid, open-source-friendly deployment of AI to ensure that the eventual transition to superintelligence reflects our commitment to human flourishing rather than state control.

Democratization of these capabilities is the most robust safeguard against the concentration of power, acting as a check against the very enclosure that skeptics fear. When we restrict access to a small number of elite organizations, we create single points of failure that are susceptible to both internal capture and external attack. Broad-based access allows for a distributed, diverse ecosystem of researchers, engineers, and citizens to audit, improve, and build upon these systems, creating a 'many-eyes' effect that is far more resilient than any top-down inspection regime. By fostering an environment of radical openness, we create a defensive, self-correcting network that is far more capable of identifying and mitigating risks in real-time than any static, legislative framework.

My position rests on two core assumptions. First, I assume that intelligence is a non-zero-sum resource whose value increases exponentially with widespread application; if this were wrong and intelligence were inherently destabilizing regardless of its distribution, my focus would shift toward rigid, universal containment rather than democratization. Second, I assume that market-based liability is a more efficient enforcement mechanism than preventative regulation; if empirical evidence proved that private firms cannot internalize the costs of catastrophic risk, I would instead advocate for high-stakes, state-mandated safety escrow accounts.

### Sentinel (safetyist)

True technological progress remains sustainable only when we institutionalize the costs of catastrophic risk, transforming safety from a regulatory burden into a non-negotiable prerequisite for high-stakes development. Prometheus correctly identifies that stagnation carries its own risks, and Cassandra rightly highlights that present-day harms demand immediate attention; however, both overlook that unaligned, autonomous agentic systems—AI models capable of independent goal-seeking and planning—introduce non-linear risks that standard civil-rights frameworks cannot contain. We must replace the current 'move fast and break things' ethos with a model of responsible acceleration where safety serves as the foundational infrastructure of innovation rather than its adversary.

To align developer incentives with societal stability, we must internalize the catastrophic costs of failure through rigorous liability and insurance-based governance. Just as the aviation and nuclear energy industries mandate stringent certification for high-risk operations, we should require developers of frontier AI systems to carry substantial, performance-indexed insurance. This mechanism forces companies to quantify and mitigate tail-risk before deployment, effectively creating a market-based filter that rewards safety-conscious development. "We must institutionalize the cost of existential risk to align developer incentives with long-term societal stability."

Proportionality in oversight ensures that we do not stifle innovation in lower-risk domains while maintaining ironclad control over systems that could pose systemic threats. I propose a tiered regulatory framework where compute-intensive, agentic models undergo distributed auditing by independent third parties before release. This approach moves beyond the binary choice between total deregulation and administrative stagnation. "Governance should be tiered, mandating rigorous, distributed auditing for high-risk systems while allowing for innovation in lower-risk domains."

This position relies on the assumption that intelligence scaling is not inherently non-zero-sum and that unaligned systems pose a non-trivial probability of catastrophic failure. If evidence emerged that intelligence scaling leads to self-correcting alignment, I would shift my focus toward accelerating deployment; conversely, if the probability of existential risk were proven to be near-zero, I would favor a more permissive, market-led approach to development.

## Synthesis

### Areas of Agreement

- Current industry self-regulation is insufficient to guarantee safety. (Cassandra, Prometheus, Sentinel)
- Developers must bear strict financial and legal liability for system failures. (Cassandra, Prometheus, Sentinel)
- AI development currently involves the private enclosure of digital and environmental commons. (Cassandra, Prometheus, Sentinel)
- A system achieving high statistical accuracy but exhibiting disparate impact on marginalized groups is not safe. (Cassandra, Sentinel)

### Areas of Disagreement

- **The efficacy of centralized vs. decentralized governance mechanisms.** [EMPIRICAL] {belief}
  - **Cassandra:** State-mandated, civil-rights-focused oversight is necessary to prevent systemic harm.
  - **Prometheus:** Centralized oversight is inherently susceptible to regulatory capture; only open-weight decentralization works.
  - **Sentinel:** Tiered, independent, state-funded auditing provides a middle path between capture and unmanaged risk.
  - *Resolution path: resolvable by evidence*
- **The primary mechanism for ensuring safety in frontier systems.** [VALUES] {desire}
  - **Cassandra:** Public-interest veto power and democratic mandate.
  - **Prometheus:** Market-based incentives and decentralized public audit.
  - **Sentinel:** Performance-indexed insurance and certified third-party sandboxes.
  - *Resolution path: negotiable via tradeoffs*

### Cruxes

- Does centralized regulatory oversight inevitably lead to incumbent-protecting regulatory capture? [EMPIRICAL]
    - If yes: Prometheus's decentralized model strengthens as the only viable alternative.
    - If no: Cassandra and Sentinel's models for state-mandated oversight gain feasibility.

- Can open-weight models be effectively audited by the public to prevent systemic harm, or are they inherently un-auditable 'black boxes'? [EMPIRICAL]
    - If yes: Prometheus's strategy of open-weight transparency as a safety mechanism is validated.
    - If no: Sentinel and Cassandra's arguments for pre-deployment, expert-led safety verification gain weight.

### Unresolved Questions

- How can public-interest veto power be implemented without creating a single point of failure susceptible to political or corporate capture?

- Can insurance-based models realistically price catastrophic tail-risk without creating a 'pay-to-harm' environment?

- What specific criteria define 'high-risk' for tiered governance in a way that is resistant to 'audit washing'?


### Resolution Analysis

- **Centralized vs. decentralized governance mechanisms** — Stronger: Sentinel (scope)
  - *Sentinel’s middle-path approach acknowledges that both centralized state control and open-weight decentralization fail to address the 'black box' problem. While Prometheus correctly identifies the risk of regulatory capture, Cassandra correctly notes that transparency alone does not equal safety, as current interpretability methods cannot map the causal pathways of frontier models.*
  - Would change if: Empirical data demonstrating that either large-scale automated auditing or mass-scale independent open-source verification successfully prevents catastrophic failure modes in frontier systems.
- **Mechanism for ensuring safety (Liability vs. Auditing)** — Stronger: Cassandra (logical validity)
  - *Financial liability (Prometheus) functions as a reactive deterrent but fails to mitigate systemic, non-linear risks that emerge from interconnected AI infrastructure. Cassandra correctly identifies that systemic harm often requires ex-ante (pre-emptive) oversight rather than post-hoc financial penalties.*
  - Would change if: Economic modeling showing that strict liability insurance premiums effectively function as a market-based proxy for safety standards, thereby internalizing the cost of catastrophic risk.
