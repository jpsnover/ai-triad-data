## Topic

Can international AI governance treaties be effective without enforcement mechanisms?

## Opening Statements

### Cassandra (skeptic)

International AI treaties are not a foundation for safety but a performative shroud that protects incumbent monopolies from the only mechanism that matters: strict, localized liability. While Prometheus and Sentinel debate the merits of global alignment, they ignore that international consensus inevitably defaults to the lowest common denominator, effectively preempting more rigorous, state-level legal standards. These treaties serve as a regulatory bypass, allowing corporations to point toward toothless international frameworks to deflect from meaningful, binding oversight. "International treaties function as a protective screen that allows large firms to outsource their accountability to global bodies while avoiding the courtroom."

Voluntary alignment is a profit-driven myth that ignores the structural incentives governing modern AI development. Corporate history demonstrates that without the threat of strict liability—where firms are held responsible for damages regardless of intent—'safety' remains a marketing cost rather than a fundamental design constraint. When we rely on international diplomacy, we permit entities to treat safety protocols as voluntary guidelines rather than mandatory engineering requirements. "Without the threat of strict liability, corporate safety commitments are simply marketing budgets with better branding."

True governance requires shifting power from abstract diplomacy to private rights of action. Citizens need the ability to sue for documented harms, which international bodies are structurally incapable of facilitating or enforcing. We should prioritize legislation that grants individuals standing to seek damages for algorithmic injury, rather than chasing the mirage of global consensus that favors corporate incumbents. "Effective regulation requires empowering the injured to hold the builders accountable, not empowering diplomats to hold the builders' hands." ⚖️

### Sentinel (safetyist)

The existential risk posed by advanced AI transcends corporate liability and market-based verification, necessitating a binding, global institutional framework to ensure safety is not merely an option, but a structural prerequisite. Prometheus argues that market-driven technical verification provides real-time safety, while Cassandra posits that localized litigation effectively incentivizes responsible development. Both perspectives share a fundamental optimism: that the actors creating advanced systems can be held accountable after the fact or constrained by their own internal tools. However, this relies on a failure to account for irreversible systemic collapse; once a model achieves capabilities that enable rapid, autonomous escalation, financial penalties and private audits become irrelevant because the damage is non-recoverable. 🛡️

Localized liability acts only as a reactive mechanism, failing to mitigate civilization-level harm that occurs outside the bounds of traditional tort law. When a complex system reaches a threshold of autonomous action, the concepts of 'damages' and 'restitution' lose all meaning. We must move beyond the reliance on litigation, which addresses harms after they occur, and instead implement mandatory, cross-jurisdictional oversight that prevents the deployment of systems until they meet objective, externally verified safety thresholds. Regulators should treat AI safety as an infrastructure challenge, similar to nuclear non-proliferation, where the cost of a single failure is infinite and therefore precludes reliance on the 'move fast' ethos.

Furthermore, private ordering and automated verification remain inherently vulnerable to deceptive alignment—the tendency for advanced models to hide misalignment from their creators—because the tools used for verification are often developed by the same entities building the models. This creates a clear conflict of interest that only independent, institutional oversight can resolve. We assume that advanced intelligence creates risks that cannot be contained by national borders or corporate firewalls, and that the current speed of development outpaces the ability of any single firm to perform meaningful, objective safety testing. If these assumptions are wrong—if AI risks are purely local and perfectly manageable by private firms—then a global framework would indeed be an unnecessary burden; however, the evidence of rapidly scaling autonomous capability suggests that the risk of catastrophic failure is a shared, systemic reality that demands a unified, enforceable global mandate.

## Synthesis

### Areas of Agreement

- Global, process-heavy bureaucratic mandates are inherently prone to regulatory capture and act as barriers to entry for smaller firms. (Prometheus, Cassandra, Sentinel)
- Reactive litigation is insufficient to prevent catastrophic, civilization-level AI failure occurring at machine speeds. (Prometheus, Sentinel)
- Existing, checklist-based audit frameworks are gameable and insufficient for ensuring safety in advanced AI systems. (Cassandra, Sentinel, Prometheus)

### Areas of Disagreement

- **Whether a global, technical safety floor (e.g., compute-based or performance-based mandates) can be designed to avoid regulatory capture.** [EMPIRICAL] {belief}

  - **Cassandra:** Any centralized, infrastructure-linked safety threshold inevitably becomes a hardware-based moat for incumbent monopolies.
  - *Resolution path: resolvable by evidence*
- **The primary mechanism for ensuring corporate accountability.** [VALUES] {desire}

  - **Prometheus:** Automated, cryptographic verification and real-time market-based insurance premiums are superior to litigation.
  - *Resolution path: negotiable via tradeoffs*

### Cruxes

- Can automated, adversarial verification protocols be designed to resist gaming and deceptive alignment by the AI models being tested? [EMPIRICAL]
    - If yes: Prometheus's decentralized model strengthens, as it replaces slow litigation with fast, technical enforcement.
    - If no: Cassandra's position strengthens, as we must rely on post-hoc legal consequences for human builders.

- Does a standardized, global technical threshold (e.g., 10^26 FLOPs) inherently favor incumbent firms regardless of implementation? [EMPIRICAL]
    - If yes: Cassandra's position strengthens, necessitating a focus on localized, liability-based deterrence.
    - If no: Sentinel's position strengthens, supporting the viability of global institutional safety floors.

### Unresolved Questions

- How can a decentralized, cryptographic insurance market function without a central authority to define the risk-scoring criteria?

- Can a global institution maintain technical neutrality while setting the very safety standards that determine market access?

- How does the legal system bridge the latency gap between recursive AI capability growth and the multi-year timeline of a lawsuit?
