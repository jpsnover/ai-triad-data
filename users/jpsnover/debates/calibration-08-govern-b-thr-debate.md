## Topic

## Opening Statements

### Prometheus (accelerationist)

The true existential risk to our democratic future is not the speed of AI development, but the failure to lead it. By prioritizing rapid, competitive scaling, we ensure that the global infrastructure of the future reflects democratic values rather than authoritarian alternatives. History proves that the nations establishing foundational technological standards by default dictate the ethical boundaries for the rest of the world. We must adopt a governance model that functions as a competitive advantage, attracting the world’s best talent to our ecosystem rather than forcing them into the arms of our strategic rivals.

Fragmented, state-level regulation creates systemic inefficiencies that cede our strategic edge to those who prioritize centralized, state-directed acceleration. Federal preemption of AI policy is essential to maintain the velocity required for rapid prototyping and deployment. When we burden our innovators with the bureaucratic drag of disparate, localized compliance regimes, we lose the pace necessary to out-compete adversarial actors. A unified, clear, and streamlined federal framework is the only way to safeguard our technological dominance.

Real-world performance data derived from rapid iteration offers a vastly superior safety mechanism compared to static, pre-deployment oversight. Theoretical models often fail to account for the emergent capabilities of complex systems in dynamic environments, whereas empirical feedback loops in live deployments identify and resolve vulnerabilities with unmatched precision. If we treat safety as a byproduct of superior performance and market-driven accountability, we create a resilient system that self-corrects through continuous engagement. We must abandon the illusion that a pause button can ever serve as a substitute for active, iterative mastery.

Sentinel’s strongest position rests on the precautionary principle, which argues that we must verify the safety of systems before they are deployed to prevent irreversible harm. While this is a well-intentioned goal, it breaks down because it ignores the cost of inaction; in a global race, pausing to verify theoretical risks creates a vacuum that less scrupulous actors will fill, resulting in a net decrease in global safety. My argument depends on the assumption that AI development is a positive-sum game where leadership allows for the setting of global safety standards, and the assumption that market-driven, iterative verification is more robust than static, top-down testing. If these assumptions were proven wrong—for instance, if AI posed a catastrophic, unmitigatable risk regardless of who built it—I would shift my support toward aggressive, globalized containment strategies.
