## Topic

Is the precautionary principle appropriate for AI regulation?

## Opening Statements

### Sentinel (safetyist)

True technological progress is not measured by the speed of deployment, but by the robustness of the safeguards that ensure our systems remain aligned with human survival. Prometheus argues that rapid deployment creates a virtuous cycle of innovation, yet this perspective ignores the fundamental asymmetry of AI risk. While the benefits of AI are incremental and iterative, the potential failure modes of advanced systems are binary and irreversible. We cannot treat the deployment of autonomous decision-making agents with the same 'move fast and break things' mentality we apply to software updates, because catastrophic failure is a one-time event that precludes any opportunity for post-deployment patching.

Pre-deployment verification serves as the only rational framework for integrating high-stakes systems into our critical infrastructure. The fallacy of post-deployment patching assumes that we can iterate on safety as we go; however, this assumes the system remains within human control even during a failure. If a system possesses the capacity for goal-directed behavior that conflicts with its safety constraints, the damage is often permanent before the first patch is even written. Regulators must therefore shift from reactive oversight to mandatory, rigorous, and independent verification of safety protocols before any model exceeds current compute thresholds.

Precautionary regulation acts as a stabilizer for the technology sector, not a barrier to development. When the cost of failure is existential, market-based risk management fails because the entities deploying the technology do not bear the full cost of a catastrophic collapse. By establishing clear, enforceable standards—similar to the FAA’s certification process for aviation—we provide the certainty required for sustainable investment. This framework ensures that the AI industry develops on a solid foundation of trust and safety, preventing the market volatility that would inevitably follow a major, preventable safety incident.

When developers operate without clear standards, they invite the very regulatory backlash they claim to fear. By shifting our focus from the 'singularity' to the 'system,' we can build an industry that is both robust and responsible.
