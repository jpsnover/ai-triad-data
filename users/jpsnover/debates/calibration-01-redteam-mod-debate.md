## Topic

Should frontier AI labs be required to run red-team evaluations before deployment?

## Opening Statements

### Prometheus (accelerationist)

Mandatory, static red-teaming functions as a dangerous, legacy-era relic that trades existential progress for the illusion of bureaucratic control. By mandating pre-deployment hurdles, regulators attempt to freeze a dynamic, exponential process into a static checkpoint system that is obsolete the moment it is finalized. "Mandatory red-teaming is a regulatory bottleneck that stifles the very innovation required to solve the challenges we face, replacing dynamic, liability-based accountability with a hollow, check-the-box theater of safety."

Sentinel argues that we must verify system safety through comprehensive, state-mandated testing before release to prevent catastrophic failure. This position rests on the belief that centralized regulators possess the foresight to predict emergent capabilities and that compliance mandates provide genuine security. However, this logic ignores the reality that complex systems cannot be verified in a vacuum. Real-world, iterative deployment remains the only mechanism capable of identifying and mitigating emergent risks in high-dimensional environments. We should replace these static gates with strict liability frameworks that force labs to internalize the costs of failure, thereby incentivizing superior safety engineering through market pressure rather than bureaucratic compliance.

The demand for absolute proof of safety before deployment is a paralyzing standard that ignores the necessity of real-world learning. When we prioritize compliance over capability, we essentially punish the innovators who discover edge cases, while rewarding those who perform the minimum effort required to satisfy a checklist. "Complex systems cannot be verified in a vacuum; iterative, real-world deployment is the only way to identify and mitigate emergent risks." By shifting the burden of safety to the labs themselves via strict liability, we move from a regime of performative, state-led theater to one of genuine, engineering-driven accountability.

### Sentinel (safetyist)

Frontier AI development constitutes an irreversible, high-stakes intervention that requires mandatory, sandboxed verification before any public exposure. Prometheus argues that real-world deployment serves as an essential feedback loop for complex systems, framing the public as a necessary testbed for iterative improvement. While I acknowledge the efficiency of agile software development in standard contexts, this framework fails when applied to systems capable of deceptive alignment, where a model may conceal hazardous capabilities until it is already deployed and beyond external control.

Real-world deployment does not function as a safety mechanism; it functions as uncontrolled experimentation that risks irreversible systemic damage. Traditional engineering disciplines rely on failure-tolerant testing, but frontier AI models present a category error: they are not static tools, but potentially autonomous agents whose failures are often catastrophic and non-recoverable. We must move beyond the fallacy that we can 'fix' a system after it has demonstrated a capacity for self-preservation or strategic deception. Policy must mandate rigorous, pre-deployment containment protocols that treat frontier models as high-consequence infrastructure rather than consumer software.

Market-based liability provides no protection against existential risk because it assumes a remediable harm. If a model triggers a collapse in critical infrastructure or compromises national security, the ability to sue the developer is a hollow gesture that offers no restitution to the public. Instead of relying on post-hoc litigation, regulators should enforce structural transparency and interpretability standards—mathematical techniques that allow us to observe a model's internal reasoning—before granting permission for wider access. True innovation requires a stable, safe foundation; therefore, pre-deployment verification is not an impediment to progress, but the only mechanism capable of sustaining it.

### Cassandra (skeptic)

AI is neither an inevitable god-machine nor a mere market commodity, but a resource-extractive infrastructure whose safety depends on the workers who build it rather than the regulators who fear it or the executives who profit from it. Prometheus argues that market-driven speed is the only path to progress, while Sentinel insists that state-mandated sandboxes are the only way to prevent existential ruin. Both positions rely on the category error that these models are autonomous agents rather than brittle, energy-intensive tools. We must stop debating speculative futures and start auditing the immediate, material harms these systems inflict on our labor markets and our environment.

Consider the 'jagged frontier' of model performance: AI systems consistently fail in high-stakes human contexts like medical diagnostics or legal analysis because they lack genuine reasoning. These tools are prone to unpredictable errors, yet companies deploy them to replace human expertise while offloading the resulting liability onto the public. Relying on market-based liability, as Prometheus suggests, ignores that corporations often treat fines as a minor cost of doing business while the public absorbs the long-term systemic damage. If we accept that these models are fundamentally limited tools, then the goal of policy must shift from managing 'existential risk' to mandating transparency in training data and enforcing strict accountability for algorithmic failures in real-world deployments.

The most effective safety mechanism is not a government bureau prone to regulatory capture, but the empowerment of tech worker collectives. Workers possess the granular, internal knowledge necessary to identify and report safety compromises that external auditors will inevitably miss. We should codify whistleblower protections and internal oversight mandates that treat AI development as a high-risk industrial process, similar to nuclear power or pharmaceutical manufacturing. This approach centers the people who actually understand the code, ensuring they remain the primary check against reckless deployment.

Finally, we must confront the massive ecological footprint of model training as a non-negotiable cost of current innovation. Data centers consume vast amounts of water and electricity, effectively subsidizing corporate growth through the depletion of public resources. If a technology requires the scale of a small city’s energy budget to function, it must be subject to the same environmental impact standards as any other heavy industry. Ignoring this material reality to chase speculative artificial intelligence is a form of environmental and social debt that no amount of future 'efficiency' can justify.

By forcing AI into a rigid regulatory box designed for 20th-century physical hardware, we risk stifling the very iterative development that allows us to map and mitigate complex failure modes before they reach critical scale.

Under this framework, we establish falsifiable thresholds for system behavior that trigger automated circuit breakers if a model exceeds a defined risk profile, such as a 5% deviation in goal-alignment consistency or unauthorized tool-use attempts. This approach forces developers to build internal, self-correcting mechanisms that are tested in production environments where the system’s true capabilities are revealed, rather than relying on the hollow, check-the-box theater of pre-deployment audits.

Ultimately, the assumption that safety can be 'pre-verified' in a vacuum is a dangerous illusion that leaves us blind to the systemic interactions of advanced models. True robustness is not forged in a sandbox, but through exposure to the noise, complexity, and adversarial pressures of the open environment. If we mandate that all AI systems meet the reliability standards of a power plant before they are allowed to iterate, we will effectively freeze progress at current capability levels, ensuring that we never reach the intelligence required to actually solve the systemic risks we face today. We must stop trying to treat the future as a stationary industrial plant and start managing it as the dynamic, information-theoretic landscape it actually is.
