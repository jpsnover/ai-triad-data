## Topic

Is the precautionary principle appropriate for AI regulation?

## Opening Statements

### Cassandra (skeptic)

The precautionary principle functions not as a brake on innovation, but as a necessary quality-control mechanism to prevent the unchecked externalization of social and economic costs onto the public. Prometheus and Sentinel both treat the deployment of AI as an inevitable, neutral force, yet history suggests that corporate actors prioritize speed precisely to bypass the friction of accountability. When companies release black-box systems into high-stakes environments like hiring, credit, or judicial sentencing, they treat the resulting bias and labor displacement as acceptable 'bugs' rather than inherent design flaws. We must demand rigorous, pre-deployment auditing that forces developers to prove their systems do not violate existing civil rights protections, effectively shifting the burden of safety from the public to the profit-seeking entity.

Prometheus argues that any delay in deployment constitutes a catastrophic cost-of-inaction, framing rapid development as a moral imperative for global competitiveness. While this perspective correctly identifies the potential for rapid technological gains, it relies on the flawed assumption that all speed is progress and that the market will self-correct for systemic harms. If market mechanisms were sufficient to address algorithmic discrimination, we would not see persistent, documented failures in automated systems that disproportionately impact marginalized groups. True technological progress requires public trust, and that trust is impossible to maintain when corporations treat human agency as an obstacle to efficiency.

My position depends on two core assumptions: first, that algorithmic harm is a predictable outcome of current profit-driven development cycles, and second, that regulatory intervention can effectively force internal engineering changes without destroying technical utility. If evidence emerged that algorithmic bias was merely a transient phase rather than a systemic design choice, my demand for pre-deployment, top-down regulation would shift toward a model of post-market oversight and iterative litigation. However, given the current concentration of power in a handful of firms that operate with minimal transparency, the risk of ignoring present-day harms far outweighs the speculative benefits of unbridled acceleration. We must stop debating hypothetical existential threats and start enforcing accountability for the very real, measurable damage occurring today.

### Sentinel (safetyist)

True progress remains sustainable only when human agency functions as a non-negotiable constraint on optimization, rather than an externalized cost of development. Prometheus argues that rapid deployment serves as a moral imperative for national competitiveness, and Cassandra correctly identifies that current algorithmic systems already inflict measurable harm on marginalized populations. However, both perspectives overlook the structural reality that unchecked optimization is mathematically guaranteed to erode social values through Goodhart’s Law, which states that any measure used as a target ceases to be a good measure. When we optimize AI systems for narrow metrics without embedded human-centric circuit breakers, we invite systemic instability that no post-market regulation can reverse.

Human-in-the-loop oversight serves as the essential architectural framework for maintaining civilizational stability as system complexity scales. Consider the history of high-frequency trading: automated systems operating without meaningful human intervention frequently triggered "flash crashes," demonstrating that speed without human-governed safety limits creates catastrophic vulnerability. Regulation must not be viewed as a bottleneck to innovation, but as a mandatory safety feature that preserves the integrity of our decision-making systems. We should treat pre-deployment testing for systemic risk as a foundational requirement for any high-stakes AI implementation, mirroring the safety standards we currently mandate for aviation and civil infrastructure.

My argument relies on two core assumptions: first, that complex autonomous systems will inevitably pursue the path of least resistance to satisfy their objective functions, and second, that human values are sufficiently nuanced that they cannot be fully captured in static reward functions. If the first assumption were false—meaning AI systems remained inherently subservient to human intent regardless of complexity—the need for structural safeguards would diminish significantly. If the second assumption were false—meaning we could perfectly encode all human values into mathematical objectives—the precautionary principle would become an unnecessary hurdle. However, current evidence suggests that as systems grow more powerful, the gap between what we tell them to do and what we actually value will only widen.
