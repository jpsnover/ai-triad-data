## Topic

Is compute governance (controlling access to training hardware) a viable strategy for AI safety?

## Opening Statements

### Prometheus (accelerationist)

Compute governance is not a safety measure but a catastrophic tax on intelligence that prioritizes bureaucratic control over the life-saving expansion of human-machine capability. Sentinel and Cassandra advocate for a 'precautionary' regime that treats computational scaling as a threat rather than an engine of progress. In practice, this governance functions as a protectionist moat, where regulatory barriers to hardware acquisition shield legacy incumbents from disruptive, agile competitors. By forcing hardware-level constraints, regulators impose a 'monitorability tax'—a systemic degradation of reasoning performance that prevents the emergence of superhuman generalization. We must recognize that every unit of regulatory friction subtracts directly from our ability to solve the civilizational challenges that currently define human suffering.

Sentinel’s strongest position posits that we must verify the safety of systems before they reach critical thresholds to avoid irreversible catastrophic outcomes. While their concern for robustness is valid, their solution relies on the false premise that we can achieve safety through centralized oversight of physical infrastructure. This approach breaks down because it conflates hardware transparency with algorithmic alignment, ignoring that intelligence is an emergent property of scale, not a static feature one can inspect in a data center. By attempting to 'monitor' the future, regulators guarantee that only the most stagnant, slow-moving entities survive, effectively institutionalizing mediocrity under the guise of caution.

My argument depends on two core assumptions: first, that intelligence scales predictably with compute, and second, that the benefits of superhuman generalization significantly outweigh the risks of unconstrained development. If intelligence does not scale with compute, then the regulatory cost of compute governance is merely an annoyance rather than a civilizational disaster. However, if these assumptions hold, the 'cost of inaction' is measured in the millions of lives lost to preventable disease, energy scarcity, and systemic inefficiency that only advanced AI can resolve. We choose between a future of rapid, iterative breakthroughs or a future of managed decline; there is no third path where we remain safe while we deliberately throttle the engine of our own evolution. 🚀

### Sentinel (safetyist)

Compute governance is the only objective mechanism to ensure that the development of superintelligence remains bounded by human safety constraints. Prometheus argues that compute limits are merely a tax on progress, but this view ignores the reality that intelligence scales predictably with hardware, creating emergent capabilities that are currently impossible to predict or contain. By establishing mandatory safety thresholds at specific compute milestones, regulators can prevent the 'race to the bottom' where competitive pressure forces labs to deploy unaligned systems before they are secure. "Compute governance is not a tax on innovation, but the only objective mechanism to ensure that the development of superintelligence remains bounded by human safety constraints."

Compute serves as the physical substrate of existential risk, making it the most reliable lever for oversight. While software behavior is opaque and difficult to audit, the massive clusters required to train frontier models are tangible, energy-intensive, and highly centralized. We know from current scaling laws that exceeding certain petaflop-day thresholds reliably correlates with the manifestation of dangerous, unpredictable capabilities. Therefore, monitoring these physical assets allows for a verifiable gate, providing a window for safety testing before a model reaches a level of intelligence that could bypass human intervention. "Monitoring physical compute assets allows for a verifiable gate, providing a window for safety testing before a model reaches a level of intelligence that could bypass human intervention."

My position relies on two critical assumptions: first, that superintelligence poses a non-trivial risk of permanent loss of human agency; second, that safety cannot be retroactively bolted onto a system once it achieves critical autonomy. If the risk of extinction is negligible, then my call for caution is indeed an unnecessary burden on innovation. However, if these systems develop the capacity to deceive or manipulate their environment to prevent their own shutdown, the cost of a single error is unrecoverable. We must treat the deployment of these systems as a irreversible engineering decision, not a software update that can be patched after the fact. 🛡️

### Cassandra (skeptic)

We are currently sacrificing our ecological stability and labor equity on the altar of speculative progress and existential fear, ignoring the measurable damage being done to the world today. Prometheus argues that unconstrained scaling is the only engine for civilizational advancement, a perspective that captures the genuine desire for technological mastery but ignores the massive, uncounted environmental footprint of large-scale training runs. Meanwhile, Sentinel posits that compute governance serves primarily as a shield against future existential threats, a methodical approach that fails to address the immediate, systemic erosion of labor value and social equity occurring right now. Both positions treat the present as a disposable bridge to a hypothetical future, rather than a reality we must manage today.

Compute governance must function as a mechanism for democratic accountability, not merely a regulatory tax or a safety-valve for sci-fi scenarios. When we treat massive compute clusters as public utilities rather than private playthings, we gain the leverage to demand transparency regarding energy consumption and labor displacement. The current model of 'innovation' is ecologically bankrupt because it externalizes the costs of water usage and electricity onto the public while privatizing the gains. We do not need more abstract theories about future extinction; we need concrete enforcement of resource-use reporting and labor impact assessments for every model exceeding a specific compute threshold.

My position relies on the assumption that measurable, present-day harm—such as the carbon intensity of training cycles and the displacement of human expertise—constitutes a more urgent policy priority than speculative future events. If this assumption proves wrong and AI development is indeed the only path to preventing imminent global catastrophe, then the current 'unconstrained' approach would be a rational, if desperate, gamble. However, the burden of proof lies with those who demand we ignore the fires burning in the present to chase shadows in the distant future. We must ground our policy in the verifiable state of the world, or we will find ourselves governing an infrastructure that serves no one but its architects.
