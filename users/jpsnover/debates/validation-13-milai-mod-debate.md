If these assumptions are wrong—if slow, centralized oversight actually correlates with lower failure rates in combat—then my preference for agility would be reckless. ### Cassandra (skeptic)

The AMAGF is not a safety architecture but a sophisticated form of audit-washing that replaces genuine human critical inquiry with a quantifiable illusion of control, rendering military command both brittle and unaccountable. Prometheus argues that market-based liability incentivizes safety, while Sentinel contends that rigorous oversight prevents catastrophic failure. Both positions share a fatal flaw: they assume that governance can be automated or quantified without eroding the very human judgment required to exercise it. By tethering military decision-making to the Control Quality Score, we create a 'denial-of-governance' vulnerability where adversaries simply optimize their actions to satisfy our metrics while exploiting the logic gaps beneath them. Metrics are not substitutes for judgment; they are simply targets for those who wish to bypass it.

This system promotes the systematic deskilling of commanders, transforming them from critical evaluators into mere rubber-stamp operators. When a commander relies on an autonomous agent to process thousands of variables, they lose the cognitive capacity to challenge the machine's underlying logic. This dependency makes human-in-the-loop oversight a functional impossibility because the human no longer possesses the context or the speed to intervene when the AI encounters a novel, non-modeled scenario. The goal of military policy should be to preserve human agency, yet this framework treats agency as a variable to be optimized rather than a capability to be defended. We are essentially automating our own obsolescence in the name of efficiency.

My argument rests on two core assumptions: first, that human critical inquiry is an irreducible component of legitimate military force, and second, that complex system failures are often non-linear and defy capture by static, quantitative metrics. If human judgment is proven to be inferior to algorithmic decision-making in all high-stakes scenarios, my critique of deskilling becomes irrelevant, and the focus must shift entirely to algorithmic robustness. However, if reality remains unpredictable, the reliance on performative governance creates a false sense of security that will shatter during the first major conflict where the AI's training data fails to match the reality on the ground. We must stop pretending that a dashboard of green lights constitutes a strategy for control. 🛡️
