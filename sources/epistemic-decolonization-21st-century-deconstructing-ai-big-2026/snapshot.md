Abstract

Despite  promises  of  technological  neutrality,  the  digital  revolution  often  reproducesùand
sometimes intensifiesùhistorical patterns of inequality and exclusion. This paper investigates how
artificial intelligence (AI), big data, and global knowledge infrastructures continue to reflect and
reinforce colonial hierarchies, frequently sidelining non-Western epistemologies and communities.
Grounded  in  decolonial  theory,  the  analysis  draws  on  diverse  case  studies,  including  the
MASAKHANE  project  for  African  language  AI,  Indigenous-led  data  sovereignty  initiatives,  and
policy debates in the Global South, to illustrate both the persistence of digital coloniality and the
rise of innovative local resistance.

By mapping how data extraction, algorithmic bias, and the epistemic marginalization of minority
cultures  are  embedded  in  contemporary  digital  systems,  the  research  reveals  the  limitations  of
technical "fixes" and argues for a more fundamental transformation of digital governance. The paper
advances a framework for epistemic decolonization that foregrounds pluralism, community control,
and  the  recognition  of  multiple  knowledge  systems  in  technology  design  and  policy.  Policy
recommendations are offered to support collective data governance, participatory AI development,
and capacity building in underrepresented regions. Ultimately, the study calls for a reimagining of
digital futures in which justice, inclusion, and epistemic diversity are centralùnot peripheralùto
technological  progress.  This  approach  has  broad  implications  for  scholars,  practitioners,  and
policymakers seeking to build more equitable, plural, and sustainable digital societies.

1. Introduction

Recent  years  have  seen  renewed  public  soul-searching  about  the  legacy  of  colonialism,  most
visibly through activism aimed at rethinking monuments and school curricula. Yet, in these heated
debates, it is striking how rarely the conversation turns to technology. While many commentators
dissect political and economic legacies, the invisible hand of digital platforms and algorithms slips
largely  under  the  radar.  Addressing  current  inequities,  I  argue,  requires  us  to  recognize  how
technologies  both  reflect  and  reshape  the  power  structures  they  supposedly  transcend.  Despite
persistent claims about the objectivity of artificial intelligence (AI) and big data (Roberts et al., 2023),
these technologies are not created in a vacuum.

deploy them (Barrett et al., 2025). The fantasy of ôdigital silenceöùwhere code and data float above
historyùremains a barrier to honest analysis (Ford et al., 2025). This paper brings decolonial theory
to  bear  on  the  digital  present,  showing  how  algorithms  and  infrastructures  inherit  and  amplify
colonial logics. Consider the MASAKHANE project, which builds African-language AI: it is not
simply an act of technical innovation, but a challenge to the epistemic dominance of English and the
marginalization  of  African  knowledge.  Here,  ôepistemic  decolonizationö  means  reviving  and
legitimizing ways of knowing that were actively suppressed by colonial rule (Mignolo, 2011). The
stakes go beyond technical fairnessùwhatÆs at issue is who gets to define knowledge, whose voices
and values are built into the systems that increasingly shape the world. With these questions in mind,
this study traces how digital power operates through epistemic colonialism, explores pathways of
resistance, and proposes principles for a genuinely inclusive digital future.

2. Literature review

Foundations of epistemic colonialism: The imperialism of knowledge. Colonial conquest was
never  just  about  territory;  it  was  equally  an  assault  on  meaning.  Quijano  (2007)  argues  that  the
ôcoloniality  of  powerö  outlasted  empires,  continuing  to  shape  how  race  and  knowledge  are
constructed.  The  philosophical  toolkit  of  colonialismùCartesian  dualism,  faith  in  progress,  the
pretense  of  universality  (Mignolo,  2011)ùhas  long  privileged  certain  ways  of  knowing  while
actively discrediting or erasing others. For Indigenous  peoples, knowledge, language, and culture
were not simply ignored but systematically declared irrational or ômyth,ö a process Santos (2018)
calls ôepistemicide.ö Colonial institutions, from museums to census bureaus, did not just describe
the worldùthey invented the categories that justified domination, fixing the Global South as object
of study, never author (Said, 1978).

The political economy of knowledge production: The modern university as a neo-colonial
site.  Epistemic  colonialismÆs  influence  persists  in  shaping  modern  knowledge  systems.  Despite
espousing ideals of universality, contemporary universities continue to prioritize Western intellectual
traditions,  thereby  sustaining  what  has  been  termed  the  "coloniality  of  knowledge"  (Grosfoguel,
2013). This is reflected in the predominance of Western academic journals and publishing houses, as
well as prestige metrics that disproportionately benefit institutions in the Global North (Bellen et al.,
2024).  Student  migration  from  the  Global  South  to  the  Global  North  perpetuates  patterns  of
intellectual dependency, reinforcing hierarchical relationships (Keet, 2014). Development paradigms
often serve as forms of "epistemic charity," imposing Western frameworks as universal solutions to
problems defined through Western perspectives (Escobar, 2018).In the digital era, these hierarchical
dynamics  are  further  entrenched.  Practices  such  as  "open  data"  and  "knowledge  transfer"  can
replicate extractive relationships, where data is sourced from the Global South while value creation
and analytic power remain concentrated in the Global North (Thatcher et al., 2016).

The critical turn in Tech studies and its decolonial limit. Recent critical work in technology
studiesùby  scholars  such  as  OÆNeil  (2016)  and  Eubanks  (2018)ùhas  exposed  how  algorithms
reinforce social inequalities.

The FAccT movement has made bias visible and measurable (Barocas et al., 2019). Yet, even
these critiques often remain within a Western liberal frame, focusing on fairness without questioning
the deeper assumption that all human life should be turned into data (Birhane, 2020; Mohamed et al.,
2020). This paper argues for a more ambitious decolonial critiqueùone that challenges the epistemic
foundations of the digital age itself.

3. Research methodology

This  study  is  anchored  in  a  decolonial  approach  that  interrogates  the  deep  histories  and
philosophies shaping digital technology. Rather than tinkering at the margins, it asks foundational
questions about the criteria by which knowledge is recognized and valued. Three central concepts
provide the backbone:

Digital  Episteme:  The  impulse  to  reduce  all  life  to  quantifiable  data  is  now  pervasiveùfrom
social media ôlikesö to analytics that flatten our emotional worlds (Vargas-Solar & Genoveva, 2022).
This worldview privileges what can be counted over what can be lived. The extent of this impulse
can be measured by the scope of daily activities now captured and analyzed by digital platforms.

Coded Coloniality: Digital systems not only mirror but often magnify the discriminatory logics
of colonial rule. The persistent failure of facial recognition for people of color is not a technical glitch
but  a  symptom  of  deeper,  historically-rooted  exclusion  (Benjamin,  2019;  Buolamwini  &  Gebru,
2018). Disparities in algorithmic outcomes reveal the ongoing force of coded coloniality.

Data Colonialism and Epistemic Dispossession: Data is routinely extracted from communities
without their knowledge or benefit (Couldry & Mejias, 2019). Real justice requires asking not just
who is studied, but who controls and benefits from knowledge production.

By  grounding  these  key  concepts  in  lived  examples,  clarified  definitions,  and  measurable

indicators, the framework offers a robust, actionable basis for analyzing digital coloniality.

To advance from conceptual critique to real-world impact, this research advocates for empirical

approaches and practical strategies to operationalize decolonial principles:

Empirical Methods:
Participatory Action Research (PAR): Work directly with affected communities (e.g., Indigenous
data  stewards,  local  language  advocates)  to  co-design  research  questions  and  methodologies.
Example:  MASAKHANEÆs  collaborative  language  model  development  involves  local  experts  at
every stage.

ò Mixed-Methods Case Studies: Integrate qualitative interviews, ethnographic observation, and
quantitative surveys to capture both lived experience and measurable outcomes of algorithmic harm
or resistance. Example: Assessing both user narratives and usage metrics for Indigenous knowledge-
sharing platforms.

ò Algorithmic Auditing: Conduct systematic, independent audits of AI and data systems to reveal
bias,  epistemic  exclusion,  or  power  asymmetries.  Example:  Annual  disparity  analyses  of  facial
recognition technologies in public sector use.

Practical Implementation:
ò Community Data Governance: Establish local advisory boards and grant them veto power over
data collection, use, and sharing. Example: Indigenous OCAP« principles  in health  data  projects
ensure community ownership and approval.

ò Capacity Building: Create training programs and open-source resources for underrepresented
communities in AI and data science. Host regular hackathons focused on developing open-source
tools for marginalized languages, tracking participation and outcomes.

ò  Policy  Integration:  Work  with  government  and  multilateral  bodies  to  embed  decolonial  and
epistemic justice metrics into digital policies and procurement. Example: Require that public sector
AI deployments publicly report on community participation and epistemic diversity.

These  empirical  and  practical  strategies  enable  systematic  measurement  of  progress  toward
epistemic  decolonization,  grounding  policy  and  research  in  the  lived  realities  and  aspirations  of
diverse communities.

Analytical Approach. This studyÆs analytical approach blends decolonial theory (Quijano, 2007;
Mignolo, 2011; Santos, 2018) with critical research on technology (O'Neil, 2016; Crawford, 2021)
by  employing  a  dual  strategy.  First,  it  uses  conceptual  mapping  to  trace  how  colonial  logics  and
hierarchies become embedded in the design and deployment of AI and big data systems. MignoloÆs
(2011)  concept  of  the  "colonial  matrix  of  power"  is  central  to revealing  the  continuities  between
historical  and  digital  forms  of  epistemic  dominance.  Second,  the  analysis  draws  on  thematic
investigation of real-world casesùsuch as algorithmic discrimination in public services or resistance
movements in the Global Southùpaying particular attention to how these cases illuminate power
relations in political economy, bias, and infrastructure. Rather than treating examples from the Global
South  as  peripheral,  this  methodology  places  them  at  the  core  of  knowledge  production.  The
approach is committed to pluriversality: it recognizes the legitimacy of multiple knowledge traditions
and explicitly challenges the universalizing tendencies of Western techno-scientific norms.

4.1. Three pillars of digital coloniality

Pillar 1: The political economy of data extraction
The global hunger for data echoes the exploitative patterns of colonial resource extraction. Data
is not only the ônew oilöùit is the core resource of the digital economy, with flows from the Global
South to the Global North reinforcing old hierarchies. Humanitarian organizations, sometimes under
the banner of ôefficiency,ö gather sensitive biometric data from refugees, raising complex questions
about consent and ownership. ôFreeö platforms entice users with convenience, only to monetize their
information. Infrastructure remains concentrated in the North, ensuring that value and power flow
upward while communities in the South are reduced to raw data sources. This is digital enclosureù
an old logic in a new guise.

Pillar 2: Algorithmic bias as epistemic violence
Algorithmic  ôbiasö  is  not  just  a  bug  to  be  fixed;  it  is  a  form  of  epistemic  violence  rooted  in
histories  of  classification  and  exclusion.  AI  trained  on  Western-dominated  data  reproducesùand
sometimes  intensifiesùthe  marginalization  of  other  worldviews.  Predictive  policing  tools  in  the
United  States  have  assigned  higher  risk  to  African-American  defendants,  perpetuating  cycles  of
injustice. Facial recognition systems routinely fail with darker-skinned faces.

 These are not mere technical errors, but signs of who is allowed to be visible, and whose realities

are systematically erased. Coded coloniality lives on.

Pillar 3: The digital divide as epistemic marginalization
The real ôdigital divideö is not just about access to hardware or connectivity. It is about who gets
to define the digital worldùwhose languages, cultures, and knowledge are embedded, and whose
are left out. With less than 1% of online content in African languages, vast cultural worlds remain
invisible. The dominance of English in code and infrastructure shapes what is possible, setting the
terms for what counts as knowledge. This marginalization is not accidental; it is a product of design
and institutional choices. Further, the lack of diversity in the technology workforceùespecially in
influential  tech  hubs  like  Silicon  Valleyùmeans  that  products  and  platforms  are  often  created
through the lens of a narrow demographic, perpetuating exclusionary practices (Broussard, 2018).
Another dimension of this divide is the imposition of Western-designed technological "solutions"
onto contexts for which they were not intended, sidelining local expertise, cultural values, and social
realities  (Talhouk  et  al.,  2021;  Mollema  &  T.,  2024).  The  consequence  is  a  digital  form  of
epistemicide,  where  entire  knowledge  systems  and  cultural  perspectives  are  rendered  invisible,
restricting the construction of digital futures to a single, dominant worldview instead of embracing a
diversity of epistemologies (Mollema & T., 2024).

4.2. Pathways to decolonization

Data sovereignty as collective self-determination
The struggle for digital justice begins with the recognition that data is not just an individual asset,
but a collective resource bound up with cultural identity and community autonomy. Unlike privacy
regimes that focus solely on personal consent, data sovereignty asserts the right of communities to
determine how information about their members, lands, and practices is gathered, stored, and shared.
The  M?ori-led  Te  Mana  Raraunga  initiative  in  New  Zealand  offers  a  powerful  example:  by
embedding  Indigenous  values  into  data  governance,  it  challenges  the  presumption  that  global
standards are universally applicable. Similar efforts around the globeùsuch as the adoption of the
CARE  Principles  and  the  OCAP«  frameworkùdemonstrate  that  authentic  data  sovereignty  is
measurable, not only by the presence of policy but by the number of digital projects meaningfully
governed by the communities they represent. In the African context, debates over KenyaÆs Huduma
Namba system reveal both the promise and the pitfalls of localizing control in the face of corporate
and  state  interests.  Ultimately,  these  efforts  mark  an  ongoing  negotiation  over  what  it  means  to
reclaim digital self-determination.

Indigenous AI and the pluriversal figital future
A crucial pathway to digital decolonization is the creation of Indigenous and community-driven
artificial  intelligenceùtechnologies  designed  from  the  ground  up  to  reflect  non-Western  ways  of
knowing  and  local  cultural  values  (Mohamed  et  al.,  2020).  Rather  than  simply  adapting  Western
technologies for new contexts, this approach prioritizes the development of data sets and AI models
that are rooted in the languages, histories, and ecological knowledge of the communities they are
meant to serve (Roberts et al., 2023; Asiedu et al., 2024).For example, AI tools can be purpose-built
to  document  and  revitalize  oral  traditions,  or  to  support  land  and  resource  management  based  on
traditional ecological practices (Lewis et al., 2021; Jacob et al., 2018).

 This vision embodies the principle of pluriversality, echoing the Zapatista philosophy of ôa world

where many worlds fitö (Escobar, 2018).

In this framework, a truly just digital future is not a single, universal model imposed on all, but a
constellation  of  distinct  technological  worldsùeach  grounded  in  the  knowledge  systems  and
aspirations of its own community (Barrett et al., 2025).The challenge, then, is not to localize Western
technology,  but  to  foster  genuinely  new  digital  imaginaries  that  emerge  authentically  from
Indigenous and marginalized traditions. Progress toward this goal can be measured by the degree to
which digital infrastructures, educational content, and policy frameworks explicitly include and are
shaped  by  non-Western  epistemologies.  Ultimately,  the  struggle  for  epistemic  justice  in  AI  is
inseparable from broader efforts to recognize and empower diverse knowledge systems in shaping
the  future.  The  strength  of  the  decolonial  framework  advanced  here  lies  in  its  ability  to  explain
persistent forms of algorithmic and data-driven harm that conventional technical fairness approaches
frequently fail to address. By foregrounding the historical and political origins of digital power, the
analysis demonstrates why technical fixes are often inadequate for achieving genuine justice. The
robustness of the studyÆs claims is further supported by drawing upon empirical evidence and case
studies from the Global South, treating these as central, not peripheral, to the theorization of digital
coloniality (Asiedu et al., 2024).

5. Conclusions

Limitations  of  the  study:  This  research  is  subject  to  several  limitations.  First,  the  analysis  is
primarily conceptual and relies on secondary data, which may not capture the most recent or localized
forms of resistance  and innovation. Second, the formidable resource requirements  for  developing
competitive  AI systems  mean  that  local  or alternative projects  often remain  on  the  margins, with
limited influence on dominant technological trends. Third, there is a structural tension between the
pursuit  of  data  sovereignty and the  practical  benefits of  global  digital interconnection, which can
complicate decolonial goals. Finally, efforts by actors in the Global North to "include" marginalized
voices  risk  perpetuating  paternalistic  relationships,  sometimes  reducing  genuine  pluriversality  to
mere tokenism (D'Ignazio & Klein, 2020).

Generalizability and future research: The analytical framework presented is applicable to any
context where Western technological standards are imposed on non-Western epistemologies. Future
research should  be  as  plural  and  participatory as the visions  for digital  justice  it  aims  to support.
Three  main  avenues  are  proposed:  (1)  ethnographic  and  participatory  studies  of  data  sovereignty
initiatives, with attention to governance and practical challenges; (2) technical research focused on
developing AI and data infrastructures that are culturally and linguistically inclusive, especially for
marginalized  groups;  and  (3)  policy  and  legal  analysis  that  contributes  to  the  design  of  global
governance models prioritizing epistemic justice and data sovereignty.

 The  broader  aim  is  not  to  reject  technological  progress,  but  to  democratize  and  pluralize  its

development through collective, bottom-up processes.

 Legal  mechanisms  must  empower  these  groups  to  exercise  meaningful  authority  over  the
collection,  storage,  use,  and  distribution  of  data  derived  from  their  knowledge,  identities,  and

2. Mandate Pluriversal AI Design: Regulatory bodies should require that AI systems, especially
those deployed in multicultural and multilingual contexts, incorporate input from a broad spectrum
of epistemic traditions. This includes funding for the development of datasets and algorithms that
represent diverse cultural logics and languages, as well as the establishment of participatory design
processes. Example: The MASAKHANE project demonstrates collaborative development of NLP
models  for  African  languages,  centering  local  knowledge  and  language  expertise  in  AI.  Metric:
Measure the proportion of AI projects that include participatory co-design with representatives from
at least three distinct epistemic or cultural communities.

3. Enforce Algorithmic Transparency and Accountability: All entities developing or deploying
AI  and  data-driven  technologies  must  be  obligated  to  conduct  independent  audits  for  bias,
discrimination, and epistemic exclusion. Audit results should be made publicly accessible, and there
must  be  clear  remediation  pathways  for  affected  groups.  Example:  The  Gender  Shades  project
(Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018) set a benchmark for independent algorithmic audits, revealing bias in
commercial facial analysis systems and prompting industry reform. Metric: Require annual public
reporting of audit outcomes and remediation steps, and track the number of bias incidents identified
and addressed per year.

4. Incentivize Capacity Building in the Global South: Investment in research, infrastructure, and
education  must  be  directed  towards  institutions  and  communities  in  the  Global  South  to  foster
autonomous technological development. This includes support for local cloud infrastructure, open-
source  tools,  and  multilingual  educational  resources.  Example:  The  African  Institute  for
Mathematical  Sciences  (AIMS)  is  a  pan-African  network  that  builds  research  capacity  and  trains
scientists from across the continent. Metric: Monitor year-on-year growth in local R&D funding, the
number  of  locally  led  tech  projects,  and  the  increase  in  STEM  graduates  from  Global  South
institutions.

5.  Promote  International  Norms  for  Epistemic  Justice:  Multilateral  organizations  should  adopt
and  enforce  guidelines  that  prioritize  epistemic  justice  in  technology  governance.  This  involves
integrating  the  principles  of  data  sovereignty,  cultural  self-determination,  and  the  recognition  of
multiple  knowledge  systems  into  global  standards  for  digital  innovation.  Example:  The  United
Nations Permanent Forum on Indigenous Issues (UNPFII) works to embed Indigenous perspectives
and  epistemologies  in  global  policy  discussions.  Metric:  Track  the  number  of  international
agreements and standards that include explicit language on epistemic justice and cultural pluralism.
Implementing these policy recommendations is necessary for redressing structural inequities
and advancing a more just, inclusive, and pluralistic digital future (Mohamed et al., 2020; Abdilla
et al., 2023; Kukutai & Taylor, 2016).
