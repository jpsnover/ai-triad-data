Can AI behave ethically during military crises?

Preserving human moral agency

JAMES JOHNSON*

The  integration  of  artificial  intelligence-powered  decision-support  systems
(AI-DSS)  into  strategic  decision-making  is  fundamentally  reshaping  how
commanders assess threats, weigh the use of force and respond under pressure,
thereby  accelerating  the  tempo  of  information  processing  and  decision-
making,  shaping  perceptions  and  influencing  judgements  about  escalation,
proportionality and the use of force.1 However, as AI-DSS becomes increas-
ingly autonomous, concerns grow over its impact on human moral judgement,
ethical responsibility and the ability to maintain oversight and accountability in
humanûmachine interactions.2 As AI systems become more autonomous and
their use in military operations widens, a critical question emerges: can human
commanders retain true authorship over life-and-death decisions, or do they
risk ceding ethical responsibility to AI-assisted systems?

While  extensive  research  has  examined  the  implications  of  lethal  autono-
mous weapons systems, far less attention has been paid to how AI-DSS shape
human moral agency and ethical decision-making in military operations.3 This

For  the  foreseeable  future,  until  the  emergence  of  hypothetical  artificial
general  intelligence  (AGI),5  AIÆs  ethical  behaviour  will  depend  entirely  on
human  ethics,  whether  programmed  explicitly  or  learned  from  data.  Yet,
designing AI systems to behave ethically is inherently complex due to the lack
of consensus on fundamental ethical questions that have persisted despite centu-
ries of philosophical inquiry. How do we determine right from wrong? What
constitutes sound ethical reasoning? What defines a justifiable decision? Despite
these  unresolved  debates,  the  growing  autonomy  of  AI  systems  in  military
decision-making makes it imperative to explore ethical control architecturesù
that is, system design frameworks that embed ethical principles into AI-DSS
interfaces,  workflows  and  humanûmachine  interactionsùto  preserve  human
oversight and moral responsibility. A key contribution is the development of
a  hybrid  exemplar  framework  that  integrates  virtue  ethics,  deontology  and
consequentialism into AI-DSS design while preserving human moral agency.

Regardless of how well these architectures align with human morality, an
ethical  framework  is  essential  for  shaping  AI  systems  and  guiding  humanû
machine  interactions  to  prevent  ethical  blindness  in  high-stakes  decision-
making. But which ethical theory should serve as the foundation for humanûAI
collaboration? This article examines the feasibility of embedding deontological,
consequentialist and virtue ethics principles into AI-DSS to help prevent ethical
blindness and lapses in human judgement during high-stakes decision-making.
The  article  does  not  propose  that  moral  responsibility  should  be  offloaded
onto machines. Instead, it argues that carefully embedding structured ethical

Programming ethical reasoning into AI is not an attempt to make AI a moral
agent,  but  to  better  equip  human  agents  with  ethically  salient  insights  in
complex, time-pressured environments.

This article is divided into three sections. The first examines the expanding
role of AI in military decision-making, the ethical challenges posed by AI-DSS
and the imperative of preserving human moral agency. The second develops a
hybrid ethical framework for guiding humanûmachine interaction, addressing
the  epistemological  and  design  challenges  of  embedding  context-sensitive
ethical  reasoning  into  AI  systems.  The  third  translates  this  framework  into
practice,  outlining  concrete  design  principles  and  institutional  safeguardsù
including normative criteria, ethical overrides, trust calibration and specialized
trainingùto  ensure  that  AI-DSS  function  as  instruments  of  ethical  support
rather than of displacement in crisis decision-making.

The ethical imperative of humans interacting with AI-DSS in military
crises

In theory, AI-DSS is designed to augment rather than replace human decision-
makers in determining the use of force. These systems assist commanders in
selecting  or  rejecting  suggested  targets  and,  ultimately,  authorizing  military
actions.  According  to  Peter  Todd  and  Izak  Benbasat,  the  goal  of  AI-DSS  is
æto  guide  and  direct  the  decision-maker  towards  a  better  solutionÆ.6  AI-DSS
facilitates decision-making by parsing, filtering and summarizing vast amounts
of data, integrating information from diverse sources (for example social media,
geospatial intelligence and open-source intelligence), revealing novel patterns
and  clarifying  human  decision-makersÆ  preferences  and  judgements.7  Recent
conflicts, including the IsraelûHamas and RussiaûUkraine wars, demonstrate
the  increasing  use  of  AI  technologies  (including  computer  vision,  machine
learning, speech recognition and large language modelsùLLMs) to augment
human decision-making in military operations.8

While decision-support systems have existed in computational and automated
forms for decades, the advent of AI-powered toolsùespecially generative AI
LLMs  such  as  ChatGPTùhas  introduced  new  and  significant  complexities.9

LLMs  are  increasingly  integrated  into  military  AI-DSS  to  enhance  decision
speed, scale, efficiency and autonomy in complex, dynamic battlefield environ-
ments.10  These  models  offer  advanced  text  processing  and  pattern  recogni-

simulate scenarios, generate wargaming models and generate tailored recom-

However, concerns remain over their limitations. Some AI experts suggest
that LLMs may develop æemergentÆ capabilities beyond their explicitly trained
functions, leading militaries to explore their potential for applications outside
current known capacities.11 Despite these advantages, LLMs introduce signifi-
cant  risks  in  military  decision-making,  including  bias,  factual  distortions
(æhallucinationsÆ) and user over-relianceùa phenomenon known as automation
bias, which is discussed below.12 Due to these limitations, former United States
Deputy Secretary of Defense Kathleen Hicks has stated that æmost commer-
cially available systems enabled by [LLMs] arenÆt yet technically mature enough
to comply with our DoD ethical AI principlesÆ.13

Types and uses of AI-DSS in military decision-making

Militaries worldwide are incorporating AI-DSS at all levels of command, from
tactical  to  operational  to  strategic.  While  AI-DSS  can  support  a  wide  range
of military tasks, such as planning the deployment of personnel, transporting
weapons and equipment, and making logistical decisions that indirectly affect
how  military  force  is  applied,  this  article  focuses  on  the  types  of  command
decision-making that directly inform the time, place and target of an attack.
Military  organizations  are  exploring  the  integration  of  John  BoydÆs  OODA
loop (observe, orient, decide, act) into AI-DSS to expedite decision-making
in  fast-paced  conflicts,  improving  adaptability  while  managing  uncertainty.14
Faster  processing  offers  a  tactical  advantage  while  raising  ethical  concerns
regarding decreased decision-making deliberation in high-stakes situations.15

AI-DSS supports decision-making across strategic, operational and tactical
levels. At the strategic level, AI enhances wargaming, black-box systems and the
prediction of adversary behaviour.18 Although militaries are exploring strategic
applications,  AI-DSS  has  primarily  been  used  at  the  operational  and  tactical
levels.19  At  the  operational  level,  AI-DSS  aids  in  target  selection,  collateral
damage assessments and intelligence synthesis.20 AI-DSS can also use computer
vision and data fusion to distinguish between enemy and friendly forces, antici-
pate adversary movements, uncover links within and across militant networks,
and map leadership hierarchies in decentralized organizations such as Hamas
or Hezbollah.

Since the application of AI-DSS across the three levels of command decision-

based  taxonomy  helps  categorize  its  potential  uses.  AI-DSS  supports  three

involves  gathering  and  parsing  data;  2)á prediction  and  extrapolation,  which
identifies patterns and probabilistically forecasts outcomes; and 3)áprescription,
which  recommends  courses  of  action  and  optimal  strategies.  These  AI-DSS
functions serve as æepistemic toolsÆ, designed to process and analyse informa-
tion  to  support  human  decision-making.24  This  article  examines  the  ethical
implications of integrating AI-DSS into military operations, particularly with
respect to its role in augmenting descriptive and prescriptive decision-making
across the multi-layered use-of-force kill chain.

The risk of diminishing human moral agency

The  increasing  reliance  on  AI-DSS  for  recognizing  patterns  in  vast  datasets,
predicting  conflicts,  generating  targeting  lists  and  recommending  courses
of  action  is  fundamentally  reshaping  how  humans  and  machines  interact
in  warfare.25  However,  the  diffusion  and  redistribution  of  moral  agencyù
intentional  or  unintentionalùraise  profound  ethical,  legal  and  humanitarian
concerns. The central question is how and whether AI can be integrated into
military decision-making without eroding human ethical deliberation, which
is essential for assessing the consequences of actions, ensuring proportionality
and preserving moral responsibility in the use of force. From a legal perspec-
tive, international humanitarian law (IHL) holds humans solely responsible for
ensuring compliance with legal and ethical obligations in sanctioned violence.26
Value  judgements  and  assessments  that  inform  these  decisions  cannot  be
delegated to machines without creating an æaccountability gapÆ where legal and
moral responsibility becomes obscured.27 In practice, as demonstrated below,
shifting agency in humanûAI interactions may lower the threshold for violence
and complicate accountability in military operations.28

Humans  in  the  machine:  an  ethical  framework  for  humanûmachine
interactions

As outlined earlier, the key challenge is to harness AIÆs computational capabili-

undermining fundamental moral responsibility. This design challenge carries a
second-order responsibility: ensuring accountability for individual commanders

ensuring military commanders retain meaningful moral agency in future warfare.

Top-down vs bottom-up

Wendell Wallach and Colin Allen distinguish between two primary approaches
to programming ethics into AI systems: top-down (rule-based) and bottom-
up (learning-based) models.58 Top-down approaches apply predefined ethical
rules from deontology, consequentialism or virtue ethics to guide AI decision-
making.59  These  models  function  as  heuristics  that  translate  abstract  ethical
principles  into  operational  guidelines.60  However,  researchers  continue  to
debate whether ethical codes, norms and standards can be effectively encoded
into AI in ways that ensure consistency, flexibility and context-sensitivity.61

Bottom-up  approaches,  by  contrast,  use  machine  learning  and  reinforce-
ment  learning  to  develop  ethical  behaviour  through  experience  rather  than
relying  on  fixed  principles.  Like  human  learning,  these  models  refine  their
decision-making  processes  through  trial  and  error,  receiving  feedback  based
on  the  moral  consequences  of  their  actions.62  While  this  allows  for  adapt-
ability, it also introduces unpredictability and raises concerns about alignment
with  human  values.63  In  practice,  most  AI-DSS  employ  a  hybrid  approach,
combining  structured  ethical  guidelines  with  adaptive  learning  mechanisms.
This  integration  aims  to  strike  a  balance  between  the  predictability  of  rule-
based ethics and the flexibility of machine learning. LLMs, such as ChatGPT,
primarily  use  a  top-down  structure  but  incorporate  bottom-up  elements  by
identifying  patterns  and  refining  responses  based  on  human  interaction  and
training data.

Technical bottlenecks

Implementing ethical decision-making in AI-DSS presents significant technical
challenges. One of the most pressing issues is the ability to predict and assess the
consequences of actions in real-time combat scenarios.64 Unlike humans, AI
systems lack embodiment in the natural world, raising fundamental questions:
how can an AI evaluate right from wrong? Who determines the ethical rules it
must follow? These concerns are particularly relevant in high-pressure military
situations,  where  moral  considerations  must  be  balanced  against  operational
effectiveness.

A classic ethical dilemma that illustrates these challenges is a military variant
of the trolley problem, in which an AI-DSS must decide whether to autho-
rize  a  missile  strike  on  a  high-value  target,  knowing  that  civilian  casualties
are  likely  to  occur.65  The  system  would  need  to  navigate  competing  ethical
frameworks, including deontological principles that emphasize the inviolability
of  civilian  life,  consequentialist  calculations  aimed  at  preventing  future  harm
and virtue-based considerations regarding military honour and the principle of
proportionality. Unlike human decision-makers, who can intuitively integrate
multiple ethical considerations, AI must rely on predefined weightings that may
not fully capture the moral complexity of the situation.

To address these concerns, some scholars propose hybrid ethical models that
blend deontological constraints with consequentialist evaluations, an approach
sometimes referred to as ælimited consequentialismÆ within just war theory.66
However,  a  fundamental  challenge  remains:  how  should  AI-DSS  determine
when consequentialist calculations override deontological imperatives? Beyond
computational  difficulties,  AI-based  decision  support  must  also  account  for
human  psychological  and  social  factors,  ensuring  ethical  decision-making
aligns with military norms and broader social values. Moreover, for AI systems
to  earn  trust  and  confidence,  their  guidance  must  align  with  social  accept-
ability. As Wallach and Allen note, æpeople donÆt want artificial moral agents to
replicate the abstractions of moral philosophers. Instead, they want machines
to respond flexibly and sensitively in real and virtual environments.Æ67

Virtue ethics, in contrast to consequentialism and deontology, emphasizes
moral  character  and  the  development  of  adaptive  decision-making.  Rather
than  prescribing  fixed  rules  or  maximizing  predefined  outcomes,  virtue
ethics  focuses  on  cultivating  ethical  judgement  sensitive  to  the  complexities
of specific situations. For instance, an AI-DSS guided by virtue ethics might
weigh restraint, courage or prudence when advising a commander facing an
ambiguous threat in a populated area. This approach is particularly well suited
to military AI applications, where ethical dilemmas are often context-depen-
dent and cannot be resolved through strict adherence to rules or algorithmic
optimization.  In  practice,  however,  no  single  ethical  framework  is  sufficient
for  AI-DSS.  Instead,  these  systems  require  a  hybrid  approach  that  integrates
deontological  constraints,  consequentialist  risk  assessments  and  virtue-based
adaptability to balance military necessity with ethical oversight.

A hybrid approach

Virtue  ethics  bridges  the  gap  between  rule-based  and  outcome-driven
approaches,  providing  a  more  flexible  and  context-sensitive  framework  for
ethical  decision-making.  Unlike  deontology,  which  prioritizes  adherence  to
strict moral duties, or consequentialism, which focuses on maximizing positive
outcomes,  virtue  ethics  emphasizes  cultivating  moral  character  and  practical
wisdom.  AristotleÆs  concept  of  the  ægolden  meanÆ  illustrates  this  approach,
advocating  for  ethical  decision-making  that  avoids  rigid  absolutes  while
maintaining ethical integrity.80 While deontology, consequentialism and virtue
ethics represent distinct philosophical traditions, this article follows a pluralist,
pragmatic approach, recognizing that no single theory can address the full range
of ethical challenges in crisis decision-making. The proposed hybrid exemplar
framework leverages the strengths of each tradition to inform the design of an
ethical AI-DSS, without compromising their theoretical distinctions.

Michael Slote characterizes a virtuous person not as someone who follows
ethical rules, but as someone who demonstrates intelligence and moral sensi-
tivity in determining the right course of action in different circumstances.81 This
adaptability makes virtue ethics particularly relevant to humanûAI interactions
in military decision-making. While rule-based and utilitarian models provide
structured  ethical  guidance,  they  struggle  to  accommodate  the  complexities
of  dynamic  military  environments,  moral  ambiguity  and  shifting  contextual
factors.82

decision logs, justification templates and real-time tracking tools. Automatically
recording when and why human commanders accept or override AI recom-
mendations creates an audit trail that reinforces human authorship and enables
meaningful post hoc review. Such mechanisms help ensure that responsibility
for  decisions  remains  with  the  human  operator  rather  than  being  implicitly
transferred to the system.

Trust calibration is equally critical. Confidence scores, uncertainty indicators
and justification fields help balance trust and oversight, reducing automation
bias and indiscriminate rejection. For instance, when an AI-DSS flags a high-
risk recommendation with only moderate confidence, it should prompt human
deliberation or further corroboration. To support transparency and understand-
ability, interactive explanation tools and layered detail displays can help users
grasp not only what the system recommends but why. Integrated workflows that
prioritize  ethically  salient  information  enable  operators  to  engage  in  ethical
reflection, even under pressure of time.

Effective  ethical  override  mechanisms  must  extend  beyond  formal  avail-
ability  to  enable  practical  and  routine  use.  AI-DSS  should  incorporate  clear
feedback  loops  that  allow  users  to  understand  how  their  input  influences
subsequent system behaviour. Features designed to mitigate cognitive biasesù
such as counterfactual prompts or alternative course generationùcan further
help  challenge  automation  bias  and  anthropomorphism,  as  discussed  earlier.
Moreover, collaborative feedback loops, including team dashboards and itera-
tive updates, promote shared ethical judgement, ensuring that responsibility is
distributed across humanûAI teams rather than being siloed in either domain.
Recognizing the psychological impact on usersùincluding moral stress, cogni-
tive  burden  and  emotional  fatigueùcan  inform  the  development  of  stress-
reducing interfaces and operator support tools.

Finally, in multi-agent scenarios, where multiple human and AI actors inter-
act, ethical adaptability becomes essential. Context-sensitive algorithms capable
of  real-time  adjustment  help  ensure  that  ethical  safeguards  remain  effective
as  operational  complexity  increases.  Taken  together,  these  recommendations
translate  the  proposed  hybrid  ethical  framework  into  concrete  design  and
operational  principles,  offering  practical  pathways  for  ensuring  that  AI-DSS
strengthens,  rather  than  undermines,  human  moral  agency  in  military  crisis
decision-making.

Conclusion

The integration of AI-DSS represents a transformative shift in military crisis
decision-making.  While  these  systems  enhance  decision-making  through
computational  speed,  pattern  recognition  and  data  integration,  they  also
introduce  complex  socio-technical  dynamics  that  risk  eroding  human  moral
agency  and  accountability,  fundamentally  challenging  traditional  notions  of
human control. The key challenge lies in balancing the efficiency of AI-assisted
decision-making with the imperative of ethical oversight, ensuring that human
moral judgement remains central in decisions involving the use of force.

This article advances the field of military AI ethics in three significant ways.
First, it advances a hybrid exemplar ethical framework that integrates elements
of virtue ethics, deontology and consequentialism, specifically adapted to meet
the demands of military crisis decision-making. This pluralist, context-sensitive
approach presents a novel pathway for safeguarding human moral agency amid
the  rising  autonomy  and  complexity  of  AI.  Second,  it  articulates  normative
design criteria for evaluating and preserving human moral agency in AI-assisted
military operations, including decision accountability, trust calibration and the
quality  of  humanûmachine  interaction.  Rather  than  attempting  to  quantify
moral agency in a reductive sense, the framework offers practical benchmarks
for assessing how AI-DSS design and deployment shape human ethical respon-
sibility under operational pressure. Third, the article moves beyond theoretical
analysis  by  offering  concrete  institutional  and  technical  safeguardsùsuch  as
mandatory  ethical  override  mechanisms,  dynamic  trust  calibration  systems
and specialized training programmesùto strengthen human oversight, counter
cognitive  vulnerabilities  and  ensure  AI-DSS  serve  as  instruments  of  ethical
support.

As AI-DSS become increasingly embedded in military operations, military
organizations  must  deepen  their  understanding  of  the  evolving  relationship
between human decision-makers and AI systems. Rather than treating AI-DSS
as passive tools or technical aids, they should be understood as shared cogni-
tive  entitiesùshaping  and  shaped  by  the  human  actors  they  support.  Future
research should prioritize empirically testing these ethical frameworks through
controlled wargaming exercises, field studies of humanûmachine interaction in
operational contexts and cross-cultural analyses of AI-DSS deployment. These
efforts  are  essential  to  ensure  that  AI  enhances,  rather  than  erodes,  human
ethical judgement and accountability in the automated battlespace of the future.
