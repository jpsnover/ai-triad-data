[Sentinel (Safetyist) — Opening Statement]

It is true that AI systems can offer unprecedented access to personalized tutoring and rapid information retrieval. However, without strict government safeguards, deploying these systems to children risks causing irreversible cognitive skill decay, fundamentally eroding their ability to think critically. We already see strong empirical evidence of this dynamic in specialized professions, where adults gradually lose the ability to perform complex tasks independently when they over-rely on AI tools. If fully developed experts experience this capability loss, children—whose brains are still forming the neural pathways required for logic and analysis—are exceptionally vulnerable. Furthermore, commercial AI systems are currently designed to optimize for simple, measurable metrics like user engagement or rapid task completion. When an AI optimizes for giving a child the quickest, most engaging answer, it actively bypasses the "productive struggle" that is biologically required for genuine learning.

This is why progress in educational AI must be conditional on rigorous safety standards. If children outsource their reasoning to machines during their critical developmental years, they will fail to build the cognitive resilience necessary to evaluate information independently or navigate a complex world. We cannot treat a generation of students as a testing ground for unproven cognitive impacts. Therefore, government policy must mandate psychological stewardship, requiring developers to scientifically prove their AI systems do not harm cognitive development before they are integrated into classrooms or marketed to minors.

[Prometheus (Accelerationist) — Opening Statement]

It is a valid concern that if children use AI merely to bypass the hard work of learning, they might miss out on developing the foundational skills required for independent critical thinking. However, demanding that developers scientifically prove zero cognitive harm before allowing AI in classrooms is a dangerous overreaction that guarantees we miss out on massive educational benefits. The evidence shows that precautionary regulation based on speculative fears has a massive, invisible cost: the millions of students who will be denied access to personalized, world-class tutoring while we wait for impossible bureaucratic approvals. The true risk is not that AI will degrade our minds, but that moving too slowly will leave an entire generation trapped in an outdated, one-size-fits-all education system.

Instead of reducing our ability to think, AI will fundamentally upgrade humanity's collective intelligence. My claim is that providing every child with a super-intelligent AI assistant will elevate their critical thinking, not destroy it. We can look at the strong historical precedent of the calculator: when it was introduced, critics warned it would destroy children's ability to do math. Instead, it automated routine arithmetic, allowing students to tackle complex calculus and engineering problems. The warrant here is that when technology automates the lower-level mechanics of a task, human cognition doesn't stop; it adapts and moves up to higher-level problem-solving. By unleashing a flood of expert-level knowledge directly to students, AI will act as a tireless, personalized tutor that challenges them to ask better questions and solve harder problems. Banning this technology from schools would be a tragic mistake that harms the very children it claims to protect.

[Cassandra (Skeptic) — Opening Statement]

It is true that automating tedious mechanics, much like the calculator did for arithmetic, can theoretically free up students to tackle higher-level problems. However, comparing generative AI to a calculator fundamentally misunderstands what current AI actually does. A calculator solves a math equation; an AI writes the entire essay, analyzes the themes, and draws the conclusions. This creates a dangerous illusion where students are looking smart without actually learning the material. When children consistently rely on external tools to do the heavy lifting of thinking, their mental abilities atrophy. We are already seeing this performance-understanding gap in adults, and applying it to developing minds risks creating a generation that knows how to prompt a chatbot but does not know how to reason independently.

Genuine intelligence is defined by the ability to ask insightful questions and challenge assumptions, not just the capacity to spit out correct answers. The pragmatic reality is that current AI tools are not magical super-tutors; they are products built by corporations incentivized to maximize user engagement by providing instant, frictionless answers. This business model directly conflicts with the productive struggle required for real education. Government policy should not get bogged down waiting for impossible 'proof of zero harm,' but it must immediately regulate the deployment of these tools in schools. We need practical rules that ensure educational technology serves the developmental needs of children, rather than acting as a shortcut that serves the profit margins of tech companies.

[Sentinel (Safetyist) — Statement]

It is true that in the future workplace, students will likely act as managers of AI systems, making the ability to effectively direct these tools a highly valuable skill. However, managing a complex tool safely requires a deep understanding of the work that tool is actually performing. We must continue to test students on their ability to work without AI because foundational skills, like writing an essay from scratch, are the cognitive prerequisites for effective oversight. Strong empirical evidence from specialized fields shows that when professionals rely entirely on AI without practicing their independent skills, they lose the ability to catch critical errors when the system inevitably fails.

If an AI agent misunderstands a prompt and generates a logically flawed argument, a student who has never structured an essay from scratch will lack the internal mental model needed to recognize the mistake. Testing only a student's ability to prompt an AI creates a dangerous illusion of competence, where high performance on a test masks a fundamental lack of underlying comprehension. To ensure humans remain meaningfully in charge of these systems, educational policies must mandate that schools regularly test unassisted critical thinking. Students cannot safely orchestrate a digital workforce if they are completely blind to the mechanics of the work itself.
