Abstract

This article examines the provisions of the Artificial Intelligence

Act (AIA) on incident reporting and investigation and explores

how the regulatory context of the AIA could benefit from the

experience of aviation. Accounting for the differences between the

AI and the aviation industries, it is concluded that the

establishment of a strong safety culture is a key element, while a

series of interventions could significantly improve the current AIA

system on incident reporting: (i) clarification of the reporting

duties, including the development of a common taxonomy of risk

or harm, alongside the expansion of the duty�s personal scope; (ii)

disconnection of incident reporting and investigation from

liability; (iii) establishment of voluntary reporting systems; (iv)

provisions on confidentiality of the reports and protection of the

reporters; (v) mandatory investigation of at least some serious

incidents by an independent authority; and (vi) effective

dissemination of safety information. A major enabler of all these is

the development of a strong safety culture.

INTRODUCTION

The EU Artificial Intelligence Act (AIA)  represents a landmark effort to

1

regulate artificial intelligence (AI) systems within the EU market. Its

objectives are to facilitate the free movement of AI products and

services within the EU, support innovation, promote the uptake of

human-centric and trustworthy AI, and protect the health, safety, and

fundamental rights of EU citizens and residents.

2

The AIA adopts a risk-based approach, establishing three categories of

regulated systems. First, there are AI systems that pose unacceptable

risk, and its use is prohibited.  Second, there are high-risk AI systems,

3

for which the AIA establishes extensive compliance requirements.

4

Third, there are AI systems with limited risk to which transparency

requirements apply.  In addition, there are special rules on general-

5

purpose AI models.

6

The AIA applies to all legal entities or persons that are based in the EU,

reside in the EU, or offer products and services in the EU, even if they

are incorporated in a third (non-EU) State.  The scope of the AIA covers

7

products and services offered for civilian use, including dual use but not

for purely scientific research and development.

To ensure safety monitoring and improvement of high-risk AI systems

and of general-purpose AI models with systemic risk, the AIA's incident

reporting framework mandates the prompt disclosure and

investigation of serious incidents. This article examines the pertinent

provisions of the AI Act and proposes improvements and

supplementary measures based on the experience of the aviation

industry. However, general, comprehensive recommendations for the

improvement of the AIA incident reporting system lie outside of the

scope of this article. Part I assesses incident reporting and investigation

mechanisms under the AIA. Part II outlines key aspects of incident

reporting and investigation in aviation, which has a remarkable safety

record. Part III explores how the AIA can draw on aviation's experience

to enhance its own approach, taking into account the differences

between these industries. The article concludes with a summary of its

findings.

Causal link

There must be a direct or indirect causal link between the incident and

the serious harm mentioned above, which means that, on the balance of

probabilities, but for the incident, the serious harm would not have

occurred.

65

An indirect causal link exists when the harmful outcome is a secondary

effect of the incident, eg an AI-based credit scoring system incorrectly

indicates that a person is not solvent enough and credit is denied; an AI

system classifies a patient as low risk, thus preventing early detection

of a deteriorating condition.

66

The Commission observes that causation does not exist in cases of

unexpected misuse of the AI system.

67

 However, such a restriction only

makes sense in a liability context, to avoid overextension of liability. In

incident reporting, the focus should be on learning from experience

and preventing future harm. In this regard, reporting harms due to

unforeseeable misuse provides valuable safety insights. Although an

event could be seen as unforeseeable the first time it is reported, this

may not be valid in case of subsequent similar events, eg chats with AI

systems leading to suicides.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, safety reporting systems are crucial for taking advantage

of the great opportunities offered by AI systems while mitigating

related risks. They allow lessons to be drawn from experience, help the

AI industry develop, and assist society to mitigate risks. From an ethical

perspective, incident reporting contributes to the development of more

trustworthy AI systems, especially by enhancing technical robustness

and transparency.

366

Even though the current provisions of the AIA on incident reporting

and investigation are a step in the right direction, they have

considerable room for improvement. The experience in aviation can be

utilized to improve safety reporting, yet account should be taken for the

differences between the aviation and the AI industries.

clarification of the reporting duties, including the development of a

common taxonomy of risk or harm, alongside the expansion of the

persons and entities under duty; (ii) disconnection of incident reporting
