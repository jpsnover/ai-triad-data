Summary

Issue

How will advances in artificial intelligence (AI) shape the future of war? Despite significant public interest
in AIÆs potential to revolutionize warfare, researchers are still in the early stages of exploring how AI will
change warfighting. In this report, we offer a conceptual framework and preliminary assessment to help
set the terms for a more systematic debate about AIÆs potential to change how militaries will fight and win
future wars.

Approach

In our analysis, we assume that AI delivers on its goal of removing the limits of human intelligence as a
constraint on military operations, and we examine the consequences of this assumption. We use a ôbuild-
ing blockö conceptual framework to identify essential component competitions in warfare and to evalu-
ate how AI could affect them. We assess four competitions revolving around (1) quantity versus quality,
(2) hiding versus finding, (3) centralized versus decentralized command and control (C2), and (4) cyber
offense versus cyber defense. We identify ways in which AI could affect key drivers and trade-offs that will
shape each building block.

Key Findings

Our analysis of the four building block competitions suggests that the U.S. military might need to change
important  aspects  of  how  it  traditionally  operates  to  exploit  AIÆs  potential.  Because  AI  technology  is  still
maturing, our hypotheses are necessarily tentative.

ò  Quantity could gain a significant edge over quality. The relative cost-effectiveness of quantity could
improve as uncrewed systems become cheaper and more capable. Advances in autonomy and robot-
ics might make it feasible for militaries to field platforms in numbers that were previously financially
impractical  without  dramatically  sacrificing  performance.  The  combination  of  more  precise  robotic
mass and affordable robotic mass could create a cost advantage over exquisite platforms and weapons
(i.e., very capable but very expensive systems), even though the latter will still have a role in future fights.
ò  More-sophisticated hiding could help offset advances in finding, but this will require new approaches
and investments in deception. AI will improve finding by quickly fusing and analyzing intelligence
from proliferated sensors, but analysts sometimes overstate the extent of these advantages while over-
looking countermeasures that are available to the hider. Militaries can leverage AI not only for finding
but also for hiding, such as by creating AI ôfog of war machinesö that orchestrate sophisticated decep-
tion campaigns with large numbers of autonomous decoys. Alongside other countermeasures, such as
attacking the enemyÆs sensors, the result will be an especially contested measure-countermeasure race.
The relative edge between hiders and finders will depend on such factors as the kind of information
sought, the domain and physical operating environment, and which side can better bring mass to bear
in fielding sensors or decoys.

ò  Mission commandùa hybrid of centralized and decentralized C2 modelsùwill remain desirable.
AI would not change the reasons mission command has advantages over more-centralized or more-
decentralized C2 paradigms, which are rooted in having the right information at the right places to
make certain time-sensitive decisions. Access to information, not just cognitive capacity, acts as a limit-
ing factor.

ò  Cyber defenses will benefit from AI in ways that could make battle networks more resilient against
cyberattacks in the long term. AI could help address challenges with scale, speed, and effectiveness
that currently limit the cyber defense and give the cyber offense a structural edge. However, the offense
will also benefit from AI, especially in the short term, and attackers will always retain some ability to
penetrate networks.

ò  Militaries that fail to embrace mass and deception might be at a serious disadvantage in a world of
advanced AI. Relying on small force structures of exquisite capabilities is quickly becoming a liability
rather than an asset. Advances in sensing networks and long-range strike capabilities mean that mili-
taries must place deception and counter-reconnaissance at the center of how they operate to limit their
losses. Shifting to greater mass will require new approaches to U.S. defense production and sustainment.

Implications

Militaries  that  embrace  significant  changes  to  their  operational  concepts  and  force  structures  will  have
advantages over those that simply use AI to make their existing warfighting approaches incrementally better.
Exploiting AIÆs military potential will be as much an organizational challenge as a technological one because
it could require making disruptive changes to the ways in which the U.S. military has traditionally organized
and operated.

ò  The United States should begin investing now in mass and new capabilities for deception. Oppor-
tunities to capitalize on the trends that we identify in this report are available today, even if AI is still
maturing. The United States should increase its investments and experiments in key areas, such as by
developing new types of attritable uncrewed systems and developing new AI tools for deception.

ò  The United States needs to allocate scarce resources under the assumption that it will face sophis-
ticated and adaptive adversaries. There might be temptations to use AI to double down on existing
approaches in the hope that the United States will have an unassailable AI first-mover advantage, allow-
ing it to operate in ways that defy the trends we hypothesize. The United States should instead allocate
scarce resources toward approaches that will provide durable long-term advantages.

ò  The United States needs a plan to manage the transition to an AI-enabled force. Important ques-
tions remain about the implementation details of leveraging AI; for example, what might a new balance
between exquisite capabilities and robotic mass in the U.S. force structure look like, how can the United
States field AI tools to units in a thoughtful way that balances speed and the need for trust and reli-
ability, and how can the United States train both human personnel and AI tools in symbiotic ways that
reinforce strengths and offset weaknesses?

Introduction

How will advances in artificial intelligence (AI) influence the conduct of war? There is a growing belief among
policymakers and analysts that AI will transform the future of war and could even reshape the international
balance of power.1 Despite significant public interest in AIÆs potential to revolutionize warfare, researchers
are still in the early stages of understanding how AI will actually change warfighting.2 Commentators some-
times posit that AI will abruptly lead to superintelligence and superweapons without fully considering the
broader military context in which these new technologies would operate. In this report, we propose a con-
ceptual framework to help set the terms for a more systematic debate about AIÆs implications for the future of
war by breaking down the broad question of how AI will shape warfare into discrete ôbuilding blocksö that
are foundational to modern military operations. We then provide a preliminary assessment of how AI could
change the dynamics in four critical building block competitions. We predict that the characteristics of AI-
enabled warfare will reward militaries that invest in capabilities and operational concepts that leverage mass,
deception, mission command, and cybersecurity.

Assumptions About AI

Because AI is still an emerging technology, our assessment about how AI could shape military operations
is necessarily tentative and rests on assumptions about the quality and breadth of its future performance.
Militaries already apply artificial narrow intelligence (ANI) to discrete tasks under specific conditions, and
ANI sometimes exceeds human performance. In the future, artificial general intelligence (AGI) might con-
sistently match human performance across a wide variety of cognitive tasks and do so reliably under diverse

One framework for guiding choices about priorities is to look at building block competitions in military
affairs. These building blocks are fundamental military-technological competitions that are critical to how
militaries fight and win wars.28 If AI is going to cause a broader revolution in warfare, it will rest on the
microfoundations of how AI changes specific ways that militaries operate. Building blocks provide a frame-
work for analyzing what changes in relative advantages might look like.

How Artificial Intelligence Could Reshape Four Essential Competitions in Future WarfareBuilding block competitions highlight choices and trade-offs. Militaries must prioritize certain ways of
operating over others.29 We focus on four building block competitions in this report. First, we look at quantity
versus qualityùwhat is the relative advantage of having more forces or better forces? Second, hiding versus
findingùwhat is the relative advantage between scouting and reconnaissance and deception and counter-
reconnaissance?  Third,  centralized  versus  decentralized  command  and  control  (C2)ùwhat  is  the  relative
advantage between consolidating decisionmaking at higher echelons and delegating it to forces closer to the
tactical edge? Fourth, cyber offense versus cyber defenseùwhat is the relative advantage between attacking
computer networks and defending them?

We present these building block competitions in binary terms to identify clear trends in the direction
of how AI might shift areas of relative advantage toward one end of the spectrum or the other in each com-
petition. This does not mean that the U.S. military faces all-or-nothing choices in these competitions. For
example,  regarding  the  quantity-versus-quality  building  block  competition,  AI  and  robotics  are  already
combining in ways that reinforce the advantages of lower-cost uncrewed systems relative to exquisite plat-
forms, but this does not mean that exquisite systems have no place in future battlefields or that the U.S. mili-
tary should only invest in cheap drones. The core argument is that the cost-effectiveness of quantity versus
that of quality is changing, and the U.S. military should reconsider the extent of its emphasis on exquisite
capabilities and how to leverage robotic mass. Although the U.S. military cannot focus on only one end of
the spectrum while ignoring the other, the U.S. military needs to set priorities and make bets on how AI will
empower certain approaches over others because resources are finite. Investing a marginal dollar toward a
larger U.S. force structure would compete with investing in qualitatively better capabilities. Given the mag-
nitude of the military challenge that China poses, the United States can no longer pursue overmatch across
the board; instead, it must establish priorities while guarding against spreading its resources too thin by
hedging on everything.30

We chose these building blocks because we believe that they are central to modern military operations
and  that  they  will  remain  central  in  the  future  as  technology  evolves.  Modern  battle  networks  integrate
(1) sensors that observe the battlespace and find targets (e.g., reconnaissance drones or imaging satellites),
(2) shooters that generate kinetic and nonkinetic effects (e.g., cruise missiles or cyberattacks), (3) commu-
nications links that enable critical information to flow through the network, and (4) C2 elements to make
sound and timely decisions using that information.31 Even in a world with advanced AI, the U.S. military will
still grapple with familiar questions, such as how to scout for enemy forces while protecting its own forces
from enemy sensors and how to protect the computer networks that provide the electronic backbone of its
operations. Some questions that appear new, such as how to integrate a new generation of drones into its force
structure and how to leverage AI as a decisionmaking tool, still evoke classic debates in defense planning,
such as how to strike the balance between quantity versus quality and how to manage the trade-offs of differ-
ent ways of exercising C2 over fielded forces.

Quality Versus Quantity

How will advances in AI influence the relative advantage between quantity and quality in military opera-
tions? A recurring theme in military history is that new technologies have sometimes allowed one side to
offset a numerical disadvantage by creating a qualitative edge, such as U.S. breakthroughs in stealth and pre-
cision strike networks during the ôSecond Offsetö in the latter half of the Cold War.1 The U.S. militaryÆs force
planning in recent decades has relied heavily on building this type of qualitative edge by fielding smaller
numbers of increasingly capable exquisite platforms. The extent of this emphasis on quality has raised peri-
odic concerns that the U.S. military might struggle against an adversary, such as China, that prioritizes mass
in its own force structure.2

We argue that AI could produce greater relative advantages for quantity over quality. This stands in con-
trast to many technological innovations in the past, which tended to benefit quality. There are two key ele-
ments to this prediction. The first is that advances in AI and robotics are improving the performance of
cheaper uncrewed systems. Robotic mass can offer good-enough performance to meet some mission require-
ments without needing to match exquisite performance. In the context of precision strike, Michael Horow-
itz calls this trend ôprecise mass:ö Cheaper one-way attack drones now offer a level of precision previously
reserved for exquisite missiles and strike aircraft.3 This trend toward lower-cost but good-enough robotic
systems will apply to other applications as well.

The second element of our argument is that robotic mass will increasingly offer a cost advantage over
exquisite systems, reflecting the trend toward ôaffordable mass.ö4 In the past, pursuing quantitative superior-
ity might have been desirable in theory but cost-ineffective in practice because it required making large sac-
rifices in qualitative performance to afford a much bigger force structure, potentially making the total cost
required to achieve the same effect higher than for using exquisite systems. In the future, the combination
of precise mass and affordable mass through using AI-enabled robotic systems might change the underlying
cost-effectiveness considerations of the quantity-quality competition.

Because AI will enable advances in both quantity and quality, our key analytical challenge was developing
a framework to adjudicate which side of the ledger will benefit more. The goal was to evaluate the claim that
AI could give quantity an edge over quality in a principled way that produced clear predictions, identified

How AI Can Help Both Quantity and Quality in Air Combat

The war in Ukraine has demonstrated the potential for militaries to generate robotic mass on a scale that was
previously difficult to imagine. But many of the drones deployed in Ukraine are basic systems, and they are
often vulnerable to basic attacks. By some estimates, Ukraine lost as many as 10,000 drones per month in
2023 because Russian forces jammed the radio-frequency links required for UkraineÆs operators to control
the drones.6 Advances in AI, however, could make it possible for drones to operate effectively without human
control. In this chapter, we shift the focus from the first-person-view drones that are common in Ukraine to
more-advanced uncrewed fighter aircraft that could be important for air-to-air combat in future conflicts.
AI could enable militaries to significantly increase the number of combat aircraft in their force struc-
tures by reducing costs in several ways. Fielding AI-enabled autonomous aircraft could enable aircraft to
be  smaller,  lighter,  and  less  expensive.7  The  extent  to  which  these  systems  are  cheaper  depends  partly  on
their requirements, such as the quality of their sensing systems, their ranges, and their payload capacities. If
militaries wanted to develop extremely long-range, low-observable, and highly maneuverable uncrewed air-
craft with large payloads and advanced radar, then the cost savings from removing a human pilot from the
cockpit might be small. This means that militaries need to think differently about capability requirements in
force design to realize cost savings. Put differently, it requires choosing to use AI to build numerous cheaper,
autonomous fighters rather than exquisite, expensive ones. Policymakers might be more willing to reduce
demanding requirements that have historically driven up costs partly because losing aircraft would no longer
mean the loss of human life. On average, the unit price will likely decrease if militaries embrace this new
approach, and economies of scale could produce a cost advantage for building larger numbers of aircraft.8

In addition to potentially lowering the marginal cost per unit of some aircraft, there are other ways that
AI could reduce costs and, therefore, free up resources to increase force structure size. Using AI would reduce
human capital requirements for skilled pilots, which are a major determinant of effectiveness and a limiting
factor for scaling up the size of air forces.9 Without the need to continuously fly aircraft to train human pilots

How Much Quality Is Enough to Overcome Greater Quantity?

We  use  Lanchester  equations  as  a  framework  to  evaluate  the  consequences  of  changes  in  quantity  versus
quality for combat outcomes. These equations describe battles whose outcomes depend on attrition. Specifi-
cally, they are differential equations for modeling battles between two sides that last until one side loses all
its forces. There are several variants of these equations, and we use the Lanchester Square Law because it best
matches the character of modern warfare, especially because a core assumption of the Lanchester Square
Law is that multiple units can concentrate their fire on one target.11 Other key assumptions of the Lanchester
Square Law equations include the following:

ò  There is a homogenous composition of forces on each side (e.g., fighter aircraft face other fighter air-

the loserÆs entire force; battle outcomes are not based on maneuver or operational art).

ò  Both sides can see and engage all the other sideÆs forces, so the battlefield is a target-rich environment

where it is possible to concentrate fire on any given target.12

To summarize, in the simplified world of Lanchester combat, there are only two ways to improve the

combat power of oneÆs force: increase lethality or increase quantity.

ò  Increase lethality. AI could improve the lethality of aircraft by helping aircraft fuse data and act to
exploit opportunities faster and more effectively than human pilots can. However, there are likely limits
on  the  magnitude  of  potential  lethality  improvements.  For  example,  achieving  increases  of  multiple
orders of magnitude would require breakthroughs in engineering to increase the number of potential
kills that finite platforms can produce, which makes it less plausible that AI by itself will enable a revolu-
tion in lethality. Additionally, if such qualitative improvements as improved response times or maneu-
verability increase one sideÆs survivability, that will decrease the other sideÆs relative lethality. However,
the nature of Lanchester combat means that chasing lethality offers less leverage to a force planner than
pursuing quantity.

ò  Increase quantity. AI might enable levels of good-enough autonomy that, when combined with pro-
curement of aircraft in sufficient numbers, can prevail even over opponents that are more lethal on a
per-unit basis. For example, the USAF would not need to field a collaborative combat aircraft (CCA)
with AI that is as lethal as a crewed Chinese fighter to realize an advantage. If the USAF could field
twice as many CCA than Chinese fighters in a battle, then the CCA could win, as long as their lethality
is not less than one-quarter of that of the Chinese fighters.

If the Lanchester Square Law accurately captures key dynamics for air-to-air engagements enabled by new
AI breakthroughs, then quality will face an uphill battle against quantity. One of these equationsÆ benefits is
that the equations clearly identify the frontier at which advances in quality could, in fact, overcome disad-
vantages in quantity. We can visualize how much of a quality advantage would be necessary to overcome a
given numerical disadvantage (obviously, if one side has both a quality and quantity advantage, then that side
wins). Figure 2.1 shows a graph of the Lanchester Square Law. In the blue region below the curve, the more

What Are Potential Limiting Factors on Embracing Mass?

Limiting Factor 1: Cost

If the Lanchester Square Law suggests that quantity has such a strong advantage over quality, why have more
militaries not focused their force structures more squarely on mass? One potential reason is that the assump-
tions of the Lanchester Square Law are difficult to map onto the dynamics of human combat, causing the
Lanchester Square Law to overstate the advantages of quantity. But if the assumptions more closely resemble
robotic warfare, the relative advantages of quantity might increase in the future.

Another potential reason is that generating enough mass is expensive. If a poorer country is generally the
one trying to rely on sheer numbers to offset the qualitative advantages of an opponent, then it must generate
enough force to counterbalance its lethality disadvantage. Relying on sheer numbers can be quite expensive,
especially for such systems as fighter aircraft. This is especially true because, as our break-even analysis high-
lights, a mass strategy still requires fielding units that have a sufficient baseline of lethality, which drives up
cost. The logistical burdens of supporting a much larger force structure could also increase costs, although
further analysis is necessary to compare these costs with those of supporting exquisite aircraft.

This cost consideration suggests that quantity might have a combat advantage at the tactical level, but
generating a much larger force structure might be cost-ineffective and financially unsustainable at the stra-
tegic level. Even if the unit price is cheaper, the cost of the total force structure might be more expensive. In a
long-term competition in which neither side can afford to dramatically outspend the other, investing in mass
might be impractical.

We can see how relying on quantity can cost more than relying on quality by looking at the historical
example introduced earlier. Although the P-51D could defeat the Me 262 by deploying in greater quantities,
this strategy of relying on quantity to overcome quality might have cost the United States more than Ger-
many. Using cost-estimating relationships that are based on the aircraft characteristics, such as weight and
speed, we estimated that an aircraft as capable as the Me 262 costs about 1.5 times more than an aircraft as
capable as a P-51D.18 Figure 2.3 adds a horizontal line (reflecting the cost ratio for an Me 262 relative to a
P-51D) to the earlier break-even analysis from the previous figures. In the red region above the curve, the
quantitatively larger force of P-51Ds still prevails over the smaller force of Me 262s, but the victorious P-51D
force costs more than the Me 262 force. That is, the United States could win the battle, but it would pay dis-
proportionately more for the victory. It is only in the relatively small purple region that is above the curve

produce Germany. In a competition between two countries with equivalently strong defense industrial base
capacities, that might not be the case.

Limiting Factor 3: Generalizability to Other Domains

Although our analysis focuses on air-to-air engagements, quantity might have growing advantages in other
domains as well. The naval domain is a clear area for future extensions of this analysis. Advances in ChinaÆs
space and long-range strike capabilities are posing a major challenge to large U.S. surface combatants, reinforc-
ing long-standing calls for the United States to revisit its mix of large surface ships versus smaller, uncrewed,
and undersea capabilities.24 Extending the Lanchester equationsÆ assumptions to naval warfare requires some
modification because of the importance in the missile age of timing salvo pulses.25 There are large advantages
of firing first in naval warfare that the Lanchester equations do not fully capture. Additionally, there are other
aspects of naval warfare that require different kinds of analysis, such as how having fewer or no crew on board
changes the ability to conduct maintenance and battle damage control on a platform. We still expect robotic
mass to create new opportunities in naval warfare, but these are reasons to be cautious about extrapolating
conclusions from our air-to-air engagement examples to naval operations without additional analysis.

Conclusion

We hypothesize that AI could give quantity a relative advantage over quality. Fielding large numbers of good-
enough robotic fighters at scale could improve the cost-effectiveness of relying on mass over quality. Our
analysis focuses on the context of air warfare, but this trend could apply to other domains as well.

The growing advantages of quantity over quality represent a reversion, in some ways, to long-term histori-
cal trends. For most of history, militaries have viewed mass as a key factor in giving one side an edge on the
battlefield.27 Militaries could sometimes offset quantitative disadvantages if they could leverage technologi-
cal innovations faster or better than their opponents. British colonial troops armed with the Maxim gun
in colonial conflicts serve as an infamous example.28 These types of qualitative advantages were generally
short-lived and did not change the larger preference for quantity over quality once a technology had diffused.
Toward the end of the Cold War, however, the United States pioneered advances in stealth and precision-
strike networks that created overwhelming and long-lasting qualitative advantages. Although such exquisite
capabilities as stealth aircraft significantly drive up costs, their performance advantages have made it more
cost-effective to rely on fewer but better platforms and weapons for the past 50 years. Improvements in robot-
ics and autonomy are starting to erode these advantages. As lower-end uncrewed systems become more capa-
ble without a correspondingly large rise in cost, relying on a small number of qualitatively superior platforms
will become less cost-effective. Although exquisite platforms certainly have a role in future battlefields, the
optimal mix between quality and quantity could increasingly favor quantity.

In  a  competitive  environment,  the  quantity-quality  competition  will  continue  to  evolve  over  time.  If
China can also embrace an AI-enabled quantity approach, then its geographic and industrial base advan-
tages could provide it with a powerful edge in an AI-enabled quantity-based competition. In such a world,
the United States might need to pursue strategies to break free of Lanchester dynamics to regain an advan-
tage (such as by breaking the assumption of simultaneous engagements and creating a first-look, first-shot,
first-kill advantage).

Hider Versus Finder

How will AI affect the relative advantage between hiders and finders in the scouting competition?1 There is
a long-standing belief among certain analysts and policymakers that technological advances will eventually
create a ôtransparent battlefield.ö2 Some now believe that AI could make this vision a reality by fusing and
analyzing intelligence from proliferated sensors.3 The head of the U.S. Army Futures Command predicted
that, by 2040, the ôability to hide, which is fundamental to how we fight, [will be] impossible.ö4 Advances
in AI certainly have the potential to make reconnaissance more effective and to close kill chains faster, but
visions of AI providing finders with decisive advantages rest on assumptions about an ideal environment for
the finder and overlook countermeasures available to the hider.

Hiders will also benefit from AI, and militaries will not stand still as their adversaries get significantly
better  at  reconnaissance.  Some  countermeasures  are  low-tech,  such  as  attacking  sensors  and  controlling
movements  or  electronic  emissions.  Other  countermeasures  are  high-tech,  such  as  fielding  autonomous
robotic decoys or developing an AI fog of war machine, which is an AI tool that can help plan and orchestrate
elaborate deception campaigns designed to maximize the finderÆs confusion and uncertainty.5 Although AIÆs
potential to enable finding has received the lionÆs share of attention, AI could grant hiders a cost-effective
option  to  offset  improvements  to  an  enemyÆs  sensing  under  certain  conditions.  The  United  States,  there-
fore, cannot count on AI-enhanced situational awareness providing a transparent battlefield because of these
countermeasures. The United States also needs to invest in its own automated military deception to similarly
maximize its opponentsÆ confusion and uncertainty.

How AI Can Help Finders

The typical vision of how AI will help finders is through information-fusion systems, which combine large
amounts of data from various sources to build up an increasingly detailed picture of the state of the world.
AI  can  help  militaries  field  more  sensors  and  collect  more  data.  For  example,  hundreds  or  thousands  of
AI-enabled  drones  could  work  together  to  form  resilient  ôtargeting  meshesö  that  provide  sensing  in  con-
tested areas.8 AI can also help automate data-processing. This could include automated target recognition for
drones at the tactical edge. It could also mean replacing teams of human intelligence analysts with automated
systems that can operate at a speed and scale that is impractical for humans.9 For example, a new generation
of small satellites collects terabytes of imagery data every day, and making sense of this much data within an
operationally relevant time frame is only possible with automation.10 Taken together, AI could help militaries
field larger and more-capable sensing networks, as well as analyze the data that flow through those networks
more quickly and effectively.

From the finderÆs perspective, these advances in AI-enabled sensing can make kill chains faster and more
lethal. Figure 3.1 illustrates the links of the kill chain. In 1996, USAF Chief of Staff General Ronald Fogle-

systems and AI-enhanced decision-support systems could also help connect the fix/track, target, and engage
links, such as by helping manage battle networks that dynamically cue strike assets to engage time-sensitive
targets once sensors locate them.

How AI Can Help Hiders

The key task for a hider is to complicate a finderÆs operational picture by creating uncertainty. This does
not necessarily require literally hiding targets in the sense that they are not visible, although concealment
is sometimes an option. We instead focus on deception as a method to counter improvements in finding by
aggravating the challenge of reasoning under uncertainty. This means that the hiderÆs goal is not necessar-
ily to conceal any one target but to create a needle-in-the-haystack problem in which the finder cannot sort
through large numbers of confusing and ambiguous signals (while also needing to detect stealthy and con-
cealed forces) fast enough to be militarily useful. At baseline, the finderÆs operational picture already involves
an extensive array of possible targets with varying probabilities of location and identification accuracy. The
goal of a fog of war machine is to present the finder with the largest possible array of suspected targets while
decreasing confidence about the informationÆs quality.

Some deceptive countermeasures are low-tech and do not require AI. These include such measures as
attacking and jamming enemy sensors, timing movements, controlling emissions, generating false electronic
signals, deploying basic decoys, using operational-level feints, and engaging in tactical-level concealment and
dispersion. If militaries take these measures seriously, even lower-tech actors could create significant chal-
lenges for an AI information-fusion system.

Other deceptive countermeasures are high-tech, and militaries could leverage AI to enhance them. An AI
fog of war machine is planning and battle-management software that could help human operators develop
and  execute  elaborate  schemes  for  deception.  Doing  so  could  include  integrating  the  low-tech  measures
described above alongside additional sophisticated methods, such as operating large numbers of autonomous
decoys and conducting cyberattacks designed to either corrupt data or plant misleading information. A fog
of war machine could also help make decisions about how to selectively reveal true information in ways that
increase collective uncertainty, as well as how to operate real forces in ways that make them look like decoys
or that exploit seams in an enemyÆs sensing networks. Figure 3.2 shows how the use of a fog of war machine

information-fusion system might improve, the adversary can decide in real time on ways to degrade and pol-

These methods can make it harder for the finder to close kill chains. Again, the attacker needs to accom-
plish multiple tasks to close the kill chainùfind, fix, target, engage, and assess. The hider can use deception
to break the find, track, and assess links. For example, the hider can use camouflage and concealment to
make it more difficult for an adversary to find its forces; it can use mobility, decoys, and jamming the finderÆs
sensors to make it more difficult to track its forces; and it can use emplaced decoys or spoofed signals to com-
plicate battle damage assessments.17 Deception is a multilevel game in which multiple players can employ dif-
ferent modes of deception on different scales. Hiders can degrade the findersÆ operational picture by creating
imperfect information, inserting false information, or overloading the system with information.

There  are  multiple  pathways  by  which  an  AI  fog  of  war  machine  could  exploit  deception  for  military
effect. One approach is to create false information, such as fake forces, to convince the enemy to misallocate
its real resources. Allied efforts in Operation Fortitude during World War II exemplify this approach. The
Allies created a fake army group to convince the Germans that the primary Allied landing spot for the even-
tual invasion of France would be along the Pas-de-Calais instead of at Normandy.18 Decoys, such as inflatable
planes and tanks and fake signals traffic, helped accomplish this goal, misleading Hitler into misallocating
resources away from the defense of Normandy and supporting the success of the Allied landing in June 1944.
Another  approach  to  deception  centers  on  the  use  of  real  forces  to  mislead  the  adversary.  The  Soviet
Union favored this approach in World War II. Simultaneously with the Allied landing in Normandy, the Red
Army embarked on Operation Bagration against the German Army Group Centre. Soviet deception opera-
tions convinced Germany that the next Soviet offensive would target Army Group North Ukraine rather
than Army Group Centre.19 This threat was very credible because there were actual Soviet armies posed to
attack in Ukraine rather than Belarus.

AI could enhance both approaches to deception and create novel ways to combine them. Moreover, AI
stands to make it possible to attempt these kinds of deceptions with previously inconceivable speed and scale.
Operation  Fortitude  required  many  months  of  planning  and  preparation,  but,  in  the  future,  such  efforts
might come together in weeks or days.

When Will AI Help Hiders or Finders More?

There are three variables that will shape the conditions under which hiders or finders gain an advantage.
First, mass plays a key role. Mass can make deception more effective by creating so much noise that the finder
cannot make sense of its environment; however, sufficient mass on the finderÆs side might neutralize some
forms of deception. If the finder floods an area with enough sensors and aggressively destroys anything that
might be a real target, then it could end up destroying the relevant targets despite uncertainty about which
targets are real.

But there are also constraints to a finder taking a scorched-earth approach that leaves nothing untouched.
The finder has a finite inventory of strike platforms and munitions, so using them recklessly could create
capacity problems, such as running low on weapons. The hider could also employ defensive interceptors and
counter-battery fire to further complicate the finderÆs ability to execute the kill chain without putting its own
forces at risk. Because the hider can shoot back when the finder exposes its forces to attack a decoy, the finder
might want some confidence that it is exposing its forces for a good reason. Indeed, the hider-finder dilemma
works both ways for a deceiver that has its own long-range strike complex and information-fusion system.

Second, the amount of uncertainty the hider can create depends on the kind of information it is trying to
conceal. A fog of war machineÆs greatest utility is creating uncertainty and confusion for the finder. In cases
in which the fog of war machine cannot actually hide the real target from the finderÆs sensors, it can create a
kind of shell game so that the finder is uncertain about which targets it senses are the real ones. This can work

Although assessing the implications of using AI for nuclear deterrence is beyond this reportÆs scope, we
note that preserving a secure second strike is a comparatively easier task for the hider than many other tasks.
This is because (1) the finder needs to locate all or nearly all the hiderÆs nuclear forces in a narrow time frame
and with a high degree of confidence to enable a successful disarming strike, (2) great powers have a variety
of mobile nuclear delivery systems operating in multiple domains, (3) there are strong incentives to invest in
deception and defenses to protect those strategic forces, and (4) incremental attrition is of limited utility to
the attacker (unlike in conventional contexts).

Third, there is variation across different domains and physical environments. Wider and more-cluttered
search areas lend themselves to hiding, such as ground forces operating in urban or forested terrain. Urban
terrain has more physical site-blocking structures that hiders can use to hide from the finderÆs sensors and
more civilian activity that could create false positives, such as uncertainty for the finder about whether it has
found a civilian or military vehicle. Smaller and emptier areas lend themselves to finding. Forces operating
in narrow geographical areas reduce the search radius and the amount of uncertainty the hider can create.
Forces operating in empty geographical areas, such as ground forces operating in the desert or aircraft flying
against  the  uncluttered  background  of  the  sky,  make  finding  them  easier.  Deception  is  not  impossible  in
these domains because militaries can still employ tools, such as decoys, but it is more challenging.

Practical implications will depend not just on the domain but also on the exact circumstances. For exam-
ple,  the  underwater  domain  mostly  favors  hiding  because  of  physics,  but  certain  sensors  can  detect  even
stealthy craft over short-enough distances. A well-defended maritime chokepoint could therefore prove a for-
midable obstacle even for sophisticated hiders. The dynamic relates to relative knowledge-quality problems:
A small area, even one in a challenging domain (such as the undersea domain), can be well characterized and
therefore favor finders, while a large area in a comparatively simpler domain (such as the air) can be challeng-
ing to monitor at scale and therefore favor hiders at times.

Taken together, there are specific conditions under which hiders or finders will most likely gain an edge.
The hiderÆs job is easiest when the hider can better leverage large numbers of decoys, when it is trying to pre-
vent the finder from having high confidence about information that must be highly precise and accurate, and
when it is operating in physical environments that are larger and more cluttered. The finderÆs job is easiest
when the finder can better leverage large numbers of sensors, when its goals require less-precise informa-
tion and enable greater tolerance for uncertainty, and when it is searching for targets in smaller and emptier
physical environments.

Conclusion

Although advances in AI and the proliferation of sensors will enable militaries to conduct more-effective
reconnaissance, commentators sometimes overstate the extent of these trends. Militaries will likely invest
in deception and counter-reconnaissance to offset improved scouting. Some of these countermeasures will
be low-tech, such as attacking sensors and controlling electronic emissions. Others will be high-tech, such
as  fielding  a  new  generation  of  autonomous  decoys  and  developing  AI  tools  for  deception  planning  and
battle management.

Because of advances in finding, the U.S. military will have to invest far more in deception than it has
in the past. This will require a mindset shift from the past three decades in which the U.S. military had

Investing in sophisticated decoys will require allocating scarce resources in the face of competing priorities.
Some  deceptive  tactics,  such  as  limiting  movement  and  controlling  emissions,  also  decrease  operational

cognitive capacity challenge. It could do so by analyzing and making sense of large amounts of data for human

Why Mission Command Will Remain Desirable

Mission command is a hybrid of centralized and decentralized C2 approaches. Foundational to mission com-
mand is the idea of ôcentralized control, decentralized execution.ö13 Militaries choose different C2 arrange-
ments in response to trade-offs, and one issue that mission command helps address is having access to the
right information at the necessary time and place to make a sound decision. There is a trade-off between the
proximity of forces to tactical engagements and their access to different kinds of information (see Figure 4.1 for
a visual representation of this trade-off).14 As distance from the forward edge of the battlefield increases, situ-
ational awareness about tactical engagements and what is happening with the forces in contact with the enemy
decreases. Put simply, the farther you are from the front lines, the less you know what is happening on the front
lines. By contrast, situational awareness about the operational-level environment increases because more atten-
tion and different data sources become available. The closer you are to the front lines, the less you know and
have time to think about what is happening across the entire theater. Another key information asymmetry that
occurs across every echelon is the existence of tacit knowledge. Tacit knowledge is the shared contextual knowl-
edge among a staff or local commanders that is rarely explicitly written down. For example, a local commander
might know that one subordinate force element is more rested or adept for a particular type of operation.

These information asymmetries mean that neither centralized nor decentralized decisionmakers have
access to all the information they need about the best way to operate without relying on the other. Mis-
sion  command  leverages  the  informational  advantages  of  different  echelons.  Theater-level  commands
make operational-level decisions about managing the campaign as a whole. Forward forces make tactical
decisions about how to implement centralized directives and achieve local goals in ways that support the
broader campaign.

Conclusion

Advanced AI will reshape C2, but its impact will be more evolutionary than revolutionary. AIÆs likely benefits
include automating a great deal of staff work that is currently laborious and time-consuming, potentially
enabling humans to make better and faster decisions. Improving the backbones of C2 systems, including
communications networks and military intelligence analysis to make sense of the environment, will help
with  that  process.  AI  might  also  let  people  command  autonomous  platforms  in  ways  that  resemble  how
people currently command other people, making it practical to employ uncrewed systems both at unprec-
edented scale and in novel ways.

However, AI is unlikely to change the informational constraints that make mission command preferable
to significantly more-centralized or more-decentralized approaches to C2. Human and machine intelligence
both face the same challenge in access to the right information at the right place and time. Centralized and
decentralized C2 arrangements each offer value because different actors have access to different information.
Theater-level  commands  have  a  better  picture  of  the  overall  operational  environment,  and  tactical  com-
mands have a better picture of what the forward edge of the battlefield looks like. Mission command will
continue to work well because it combines the best of both approaches, allowing each echelon to make deci-
sions best suited to the informational picture it has available. Removing the limitations of human cognitive
capacity on military operations does not fundamentally change this dynamic.

Cyber Offense Versus Cyber Defense

How will AI affect the relative advantage between cyber offense and cyber defense? AI will improve both
in absolute terms, but we argue that AI will likely create greater marginal benefits for cyber defenders over
the long term. The key reason is that AI could help address three factors that currently limit cyber defense
capabilities: speed, scale, and effectiveness. Defenders face resource constraints that limit their ability to dis-
cover vulnerabilities and defend networks relative to the size of the attack surface, and this gives attackers an
advantage.1 AIÆs potential to (1) enable defenders to produce less error-prone code in the first place, (2) scan
defendersÆ software for vulnerabilities faster and more effectively than they could previously, and (3) develop
and implement security patches more rapidly and cheaply could disrupt the economics of cybersecurity in a
way that generally benefits the defender.2 Defenders could use AI to speed and scale up these tasks in ways
that erode the structural advantages that attackers enjoy.3

We hypothesize that cyber defense will benefit more than cyber offense ôin the limitöùthat is, in the long
run and as the technology matures and diffuses. This prediction is conditional on defenders taking advan-
tage of the opportunities that AI provides by investing adequately in AI-enabled defenses. In the short term, if
cyberattackers leverage AI tools effectively but defenders do not, the offenseÆs existing advantages could grow
even larger.4 But, in a long-term equilibrium in which both sides leverage the technology with equal skill, we
predict that advances in AI will benefit the defender relatively more than the attacker because AI can help
offset some of the weaknesses of existing cyber defense methods and shift the underlying cost-effectiveness
of the competition. This does not necessarily mean that the defender will gain an advantage over the attacker

Gaining access to computer networks first requires an attacker to identify vulnerabilities in the defenderÆs

contain V vulnerabilities, which are errors in the programming that an attacker can exploit. The number of
vulnerabilities in a system increases as the system becomes more complex because having more lines of code
will tend to create more opportunities for errors, and some subset of those errors will result in vulnerabili-
ties. Both the attacker and the defender are searching for vulnerabilities, which the attacker wants to exploit
before the defender can find and patch those vulnerabilities. For the purposes of the model, assume that both
sides are independently searching for vulnerabilities so that each side is essentially making a random draw
from the set of V vulnerabilities that exist at any given time. The chance that the attacker or defender finds
a given vulnerability is therefore 1/V for each side. The chance that they both find the same vulnerability is
1/V2. When V is large, the probability that the defender finds the same vulnerability as the attacker is low.
Closing the gap between what the attacker and defender will both find requires the defender to allocate vastly
disproportionate resources as V grows larger. This dynamic contributes to the attacker having a structural
advantage over the defender in vulnerability detection.7 Figure 5.1 visualizes this advantage. The shaded gray
region below the curved line shows the difference between (1) the probability that the attacker or the defender
finds a given vulnerability and (2) the probability that both find the same vulnerability.

AI could help the defender produce less error-prone code that has fewer vulnerabilities, thereby reduc-
ing the size of the attackerÆs asymmetric advantage. If the number of vulnerabilities (V) decreases, then the
chance that the defender finds the same vulnerability as the attacker increases (1/V2). There are two oppor-
tunities to find and address vulnerabilities: making pre-deployment fixes to software before it is fielded and
making post-deployment patches to correct lingering errors.

How AI Can Help Patch and Exploit Cyber Vulnerabilities

The attacker still needs to exploit vulnerabilities after it finds them, and the defender still needs to design and
deploy patches to fix vulnerabilities. The delay between detecting a vulnerability and patching it gives the
attacker a potential window of opportunity to achieve its desired objectives. How quickly the attacker can do
so depends partly on whether it needs to develop new code or whether existing off-the-shelf tools work for
exploiting a specific vulnerability.

AI could benefit cyber defenders by speeding up their reaction times once they discover vulnerabilities.
On average, defenders are already slightly faster at writing patches than attackers are at developing exploits,
but defenders face limitations in the time it takes to deploy those patches.20 Implementing a fix is by far the
most expensive and time-consuming challenge for the defender. The defender has to not only develop the fix
but also ensure that the update does not inadvertently cause other problems; for example, the cybersecurity
company CrowdStrike released an update in 2024 that interfered with other critical functions, resulting in
millions of computer outages globally.21 AI has the potential to execute this cycle at a much faster pace.22 In
addition to developing the patches themselves, AI could speed up implementation by testing how patches
interact with other critical software, generating the necessary documentation for the change (e.g., requests,
approvals, and records), and helping maintain configuration plans. These are all tasks that make continu-
ous defense so difficult today, and AI could reduce these burdens that slow the tempo and reduce the scale at
which humans operate. Although the attacker might still retain a structural advantage in the probability of
finding any one vulnerability before the defender, speeding up the development and deployment of patches
could further offset that edge and enable the defender to mitigate the effects of vulnerabilities that do occur.

faster  responses  to  and  analysis  and  forensics  of  attacker  movements,  as  well  as  the  operationalization  of
lessons learned across more systems, more quickly. AI could also automate the generation of patches; the

too imprecise.23 It is for these reasons that, in the long run, the defense will likely benefit relatively more than
the offense. However, this will only occur if militaries and companies fully embrace the defensive potential
of AI technologies and commit to adopting them.

expose the AI to a direct cyberattack. Ensuring that AI agents and systems are safe and secure will be para-

approaches to deception to offset improvements in finding, (3) not change the advantages of mission com-
mand over more-centralized or more-decentralized C2, and (4) make the cyber defense more cost-effective
by  addressing  some  of  the  challenges  that  currently  limit  the  cyber  defense  relative  to  the  cyber  offense.
Taken together, our analysis suggests that the U.S. military should look for ways to use AI in capabilities
and operational concepts that leverage mass, deception, mission command, and cyber resilience. Because
of the deep uncertainty concerning how AI and other technologies will continue to develop in the coming
decades, these are tentative hypotheses.

Summary of Predictions

First, AI could give quantity an advantage over quality. The core logic is that AI will enable militaries to
field  larger  numbers  of  good-enough  uncrewed  systems  at  a  cost  advantage  over  exquisite  systems.  AIÆs
ability to make uncrewed systems more capable is significant on its own, but it is the combination of pre-
cise mass with affordable mass that will change the underlying cost-effectiveness of quantity versus quality
and have the most-significant implications. Exquisite platforms and weapons will still have a role in future
conflicts, but militaries that shift toward greater numbers of attritable uncrewed systems will likely gain a
significant advantage.

Second, AI could help the hider orchestrate deception efforts that offset AI-enabled advances in finding.
The core logic is that finders face limits in the extent to which AI sensor fusion can create a transparent bat-
tlefield; hiders, meanwhile, have opportunities to leverage AI to aggravate these challenges for the finder and
keep the scouting competition contested. Reasoning under uncertainty is structurally challenging for both
humans and machines, and advances in AI do not necessarily resolve this problem if hiders can increase the
signal-to-noise ratio in the information that the finderÆs AI is trying to process. Whether the hider or finder
ultimately has the edge in this competition will depend heavily on variables that include each sideÆs ability
to leverage (1) mass in particular times and places, (2) the kind of information that both sides are trying to
uncover or conceal, and (3) the physical domain and operating environment. The overall result will likely be
a contested measure-countermeasure race between hiders and finders.

Third, AI is unlikely to displace mission command with highly centralized or decentralized approaches
to C2. The core logic is that a key factor in discouraging more-centralized or more-decentralized C2 models
is access to the right information, not just access to more brainpower. Forward and rear command elements
often have access to different types of operational and tactical data that they need to make different kinds of
decisions. AI will support more-effective C2, but it is more likely to reinforce mission command as a C2 para-
digm than to replace it.

Fourth, AI could benefit the cyber defense more than the cyber offense. The core logic is that AI can help
address some of the limitations in scale, speed, and effectiveness that currently hinder defenders and give
attackers an edge. The task of defending very large attack surfaces against highly skilled attackers that can
choose how and where to attack places a large burden on the defenders, which requires investing dispro-
portionate resources in cyber defense to offset the attackersÆ advantage. The most important change that AI
could generate for cybersecurity would be enabling defenders to produce much more secure code in the first
place, which would reduce the size of the exploitable attack surface and reduce the extent of attackersÆ struc-
tural advantage. Cyberattackers will also benefit from using AI to support their operations, such as finding
vulnerabilities and developing exploits, so attackers will retain the ability to penetrate networks at certain
times and places, especially those networks that do not adequately invest in AI-enabled defenses. But over the
long term, AI could reduce the magnitude of the attackerÆs advantages relative to the balance today if militar-
ies seriously commit to leveraging AI for cybersecurity.

Implications for the U.S. Military

If mass and deception will be central elements in future wars, the United States will need to make significant
changes to its existing force structure and operational concepts. Since the end of World War II, the U.S. mili-
tary has prioritized quality over quantity and treated deception largely as an afterthought.1 How might the
United States redesign its force structure using the principles of mass and deception?

U.S. force planning should shift toward a greater emphasis on mass while retaining a high-low mix.
Embracing mass will require U.S. force planners to shift from focusing on small numbers of exquisite sys-
tems to developing a wider range of attritable and expendable uncrewed systems acquired in larger numbers.
Instead  of  focusing  on  one-to-one  replacements,  the  United  States  should  examine  how  combinations  of
different systems can provide greater cost-effectiveness than one system alone. This will require a mindset
shift in terms of how the U.S. military thinks about what attributes to prioritize in the procurement pro-
cess.  For  lethality,  a  weaponÆs  individual  ôprobability  of  killö  might  become  less  important  than  a  salvoÆs
probability of kill. For example, one exquisite cruise missile might be more capable than any single one-way
attack drone, but a salvo of ten AI-enabled drones might have a comparable battlefield effect at a fraction
of the price.2 For survivability, the United States will need to accept decreased performance and increased
attrition of uncrewed systems if it wants to lower costs and free up resources to build a larger force. This will
require defense planners to resist the temptation to insist on performance and capabilities that mimic those
of exquisite crewed platforms because doing so could negate the cost savings. However, investing in uncrewed
systems that have lower costs relative to exquisite systems does not mean that all new capabilities should be
cheap in absolute terms. Additionally, the United States should not abandon exquisite capabilities entirely,
especially in areas in which it still has large advantages, such as undersea warfare. This dynamic will create
tensions between continued investments in exquisite systems and new investments in mass. Further research
is necessary to determine what mix of attributes and performance requirements an AI-enabled force should
have for different mission areas.

U.S. force planning should incorporate new requirements for deception. The United States will need to
change its approaches to force planning and procurement to better leverage deception. The U.S. approach has
often been to design its force structure around large, exquisite platforms and then ask afterward how it can
hide them. This makes deception more difficult because it is harder to design cheap decoys that effectively

Some of AIÆs most significant implications could lie in its effects on the underlying economics of war-
fare. Armies, navies, and air forces are exceedingly expensive. From the 18th through 20th centuries, tech-
nological advances in industrialization, metallurgy, synthetics, and mass production combined to change the
capabilities and costs of weapons in ways that facilitated new tactics and force structures.7 In the 21st century,
advances in AIùalong with such supporting technologies as robotics and additive manufacturingùcould
do the same.8 Twenty-firstûcentury weapon systems have hardware and software components, and AI could
create major changes in the development and deployment cycle of such software. If AI succeeds in automating
cognitive labor, there might also be large implications for military personnel management and the number
of humans required to support military operations. Similarly, advances in robotics and declining costs could
usher in a new relationship and division of labor between humans and machines in warfare. Although it is
tempting to focus on exotic new capabilities, such as autonomous drone swarms, more-disruptive changes
could come from cost reductions in producing and maintaining a range of new and old capabilities and how
that shift changes the relative cost-effectiveness of different platforms and warfighting approaches. In sum,
advanced AI is significant not just because it can replace humans for discrete tasks but because it can more
fundamentally change what capabilities and approaches are cost-effective.

The advantage might go to the side that best executes the transition to an AI-enabled force. In addi-
tion to determining the right mix of capabilities, a key area for future research is how to manage the transi-
tion between the existing U.S. force structure and the one the U.S. military needs in order to win wars in the
future. One long-standing concern that has inhibited military innovation in the past has been the fear that
political authorities will call on militaries to fight when the militaries are in the middle of incorporating new
innovations.9 The concern is that being caught during the transition is the worst of both worlds, when the
military is neither prepared to fight in the way that had worked in the past nor ready to fight in the way it
believes will be important in the future. Managing this transition requires a plan for addressing the organi-
zational and cultural elements of adaptation, which includes developing thoughtful processes for how AI is
fielded and integrated into units, monitoring how machines gain and maintain trust, and allowing variations
in how units use AI-enabled capabilities.10 Successfully navigating the AI adoption process might be even
more challenging and important than achieving the initial innovative breakthroughs with advanced AI, and
it is far from clear that the United States will have an edge in the adoption competition.11

How Artificial Intelligence Could Reshape Four Essential Competitions in Future WarfareRemaining Uncertainties and Next Steps

In this report, we explore the potential consequences of technological breakthroughs that would allow AI
to reliably and effectively substitute for human cognitive capacity in military operations. The predictions
we make are necessarily tentative because AI is still maturing. However, AIÆs use in the war in Ukraine and
recent USAF capability demonstrations suggest that the U.S. military does not need to wait until AGI is at
hand to realize many of AIÆs benefits.

There are several areas of uncertainty that deserve further analysis in future research. First, we are more
confident  about  the  direction  of  the  trends  we  discuss  than  the  magnitude  of  the  changes.  For  example,
more-detailed  analysis  on  the  quantity  versus  quality  competition  is  necessary  to  understand  the  size  of
the cost advantage of affordable mass over exquisite systems. The magnitude of the advantage might vary
depending on assumptions about the different types of platforms, the requirements of different missions,
and the attributes of an adversaryÆs forces. This kind of detailed analysis would help translate our general
predictionùthat quantity will have a growing advantageùinto specific recommendations about how many
and what kinds of lower-cost systems the U.S. military should pursue.

Second, our analysis does not speak to speed and how long it will take for militaries to integrate AI into
their forces and realize these advantages. There might be a striking difference in how militaries fight in the
pre- and post-AI eras, but it might take many years or potentially even decades for militaries to reach the new
equilibrium. The most-significant barriers to adoption could be organizational rather than technological.
Developing a plan to overcome those organizational barriers requires further attention.

Third, our analysis highlights that AIÆs impact on military operations depends on its integration with
supporting technologies. For example, AIÆs effect on the quantity-quality competition is mediated through
its integration with uncrewed systems.12 AI is, fundamentally, software, and its impact on the physical world
depends on integrating it with hardware. Advances in other technologies could have important implications
for  the  four  building  block  competitions,  such  as  quantum  sensing  for  the  hider-finder  competition  and
directed energy for the quantity-quality competition. These supporting technologies deserve detailed moni-
toring and analysis. AI might be at the heart of integrating all these technological advances, but developing
new operational concepts will require an understanding of the larger ecosystem of technologies and how
those capabilities can all work together to create new and better ways of operating.

Estimating the Cost Ratio Between an Me 262 and
a P-51D

In Chapter 2, we explored a historical example of quality versus quantity by comparing the German Me 262
fighter with the U.S. P-51D fighter. In discussing why quantity-based strategies can be more expensive than
quality-based ones, we compared the cost of an Me 262 with the cost of a P-51D. In this appendix, we explain
how we made that cost comparison.

We compared the relative costs by using cost-estimating relationship equations. Cost-estimating relation-
ships use attributes of a system to estimate how much the system will cost. Cost-estimating relationships are
developed through a statistical analysis of past production. The ratio we use is not an exact historical esti-
mate of the unit price for each aircraft. It is a comparison of the costs that are required to build aircraft as
capable as the Me 262 and the P-51D using their performance characteristics, holding all else equal. We used
a RAND-developed cost-estimating relationship from 1972 in which the total cost is the sum of the engineer-
ing, development, tooling, flight-test operations, manufacturing labor, materials, and quality control costs.1
We held constant the factors that were unrelated to aircraft performance, such as the labor costs, the total
number of airframes produced, and the monthly production rate. The two parameters we varied were the
maximum speeds and weights of the aircraft. We emphasize that this is not a historical cost estimate of the
actual cost difference between the Me 262 and the P-51D. This is because the United States and Germany
would have had different production costs, such as labor costs, that were unrelated to aircraft performance.
But the cost-estimating relationship is still useful in providing a notional comparison of aircraft that have
comparable differences in capabilities similar to those between the Me 262 and the P-51D.

Using this cost-estimating relationship, we estimated that an aircraft as capable as an Me 262 would cost
about 1.5 times more than an aircraft as capable as a P-51D. If, in an aerial battle, the U.S. P-51Ds need to
outnumber the German Me 262s by more than 3:1 to offset the 9:1 German lethality advantage (see Figure 2.2
in Chapter 2), then one P-51D would need to be less than one-third of the price of one Me 262 to come out
cost neutral. But the price of one P-51D that we estimated was closer to 1/1.5 that of an Me 262. As a result,
fielding the required number of P-51Ds to prevail over the Me 262s would be more expensive. For example,
to win a battle against ten Me 262s, the United States would have to commit more than 30 P-51Ds, which
would cost more than twice as much as Germany committed to its ten Me 262s. The calculation we used is as
follows: (30/10) ╫ (1/1.48) = 3/1.48 ~ 2. This illustrates how quantity can overwhelm quality but at a high cost.
