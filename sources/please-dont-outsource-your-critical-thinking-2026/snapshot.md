It was January 2025 when I decided to stop using Instagram. It was right after [Mark Zuckerberg announced that Meta would end its third-party fact-checking program](https://www.npr.org/2025/01/07/nx-s1-5251151/meta-fact-checking-mark-zuckerberg-trump). After doing so, I sent messages to several friends using WhatsApp (ironically also owned by Meta), telling them of my decision and asking to connect by chat message. Over chat messages exchanged, I’ve learned that one of my friends was living in another city, and was about to marry, another shared with me that he needed to reduce the use of social media and spend time with his wife and daughter. Exchanging messages with my friends felt great, as if we were again close friends.

I guess it was not so hard for me to stop using Instagram, since I have taken a similar decision in 2014, when I quit my facebook account. At that time I realised how much time I was spending, and how much I was comparing myself with others. I know that people tend to post only the bright side of their lives and much content there was a "make up", version, but I could not help feeling illogically jealous of other people’s lives. In social media, almost everyone have successful careers, cook like 3 star Michelin chefs, are in perfect relationships, or go on vacations in perfect and exotic places. Deep down I knew, this is not real life, so I decided to unplug and focus on what is real. Later on I discovered that this unconscious behavior of comparing myself with others is also [felt by many](https://www.cmaj.ca/content/192/6/E136) (Abi-Jaoude et al., 2020),

It is true that I am still using LinkedIn for professional purposes, but I’m increasingly aware that even there the feed is engineered for engagement rather than the quality of content. But now, after a detox period from social media, I am trying to be more selective with the content I consume on the web. I started to read more books, search for content in blogs and websites, seek information from mainstream media, independent media sources, and really look for information that would challenge me to see things from multiple perspectives.

So, It might look like this essay is about social media addiction, but is not. It is about something quieter: the slow erosion of the habits of mind that allow us to navigate a world of competing narratives, misinformation, manufactured urgency, cognitive overload, and accelerating technological change. This erosion did not start now and is not attributed to a single technology. Our relationship with critical thinking was not broken at once, but rather in a gradual process that has been happening in stages. At this precise moment in history, it is of utmost importance and urgency to acknowledge that, so we can preserve what is left of our ability to think critically to make sense of such a complex reality, navigate the world with consciousness, resist manipulation, and ultimately participate in how the future is being shaped.

## Why bother researching? Just google it!

Starting with Search Engines, and our instant ability to find whatever we want. Research for knowledge became so easy and allowed us to quickly find almost anything we wanted to know in a few web searches. I used to joke and say that we were outsourcing our memory to Google and Wikipedia, because if you did not remember or knew something, you could simply open your browser and find a huge amount of information about it.

We no longer need to think deeply about the sources of information provided. We don’t need to read different arguments and make our judgement about its relevance. Since the search engine brings a huge volume of sources, we outsource our choice to the algorithm. Besides removing our genuine choice and making us susceptible for manipulation, we also stop being able to deeply read and analyse content. We scan through results not to evaluate deeply, but to skim broadly. The muscle of sustained attention began to atrophy not from the absence of information, but from its overwhelming presence.

There is the impairment of our cognitive ability to focus and concentrate in deep reading — which is the ability to read longer texts, at a slower pace to increase comprehension or enjoyment in the reading activity. The article written by Nicholas Carr in 2008 called “[Is Google Making us stupid?](https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/2008/07/is-google-making-us-stupid/306868/) became a popular one in which Nicholas described his difficulty to engage in longer readings that previously were effortless or enjoyable after being exposed daily to the Internet and the use of search engines. [His articles sparked a heated debate among scholars, neuroscientists, journalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Is_Google_Making_Us_Stupid%3F) about the effects of the internet on our reading patterns and ability to concentrate and interpret textual information. It is not a surprise that [since 2000, the rate of children who read for fun has plummeted, and a study published in 2023 suggests that digital reading is negatively affecting kids’ ability to comprehend texts](https://bigthink.com/the-present/digital-reading-fails-kids/) (Pomeroy, 2024).

## How to melt our children’s brains

Since my son was born in 2018, my wife and I started an exercise to be self-aware of our use of mobile devices and screen time. We had always believed that children are highly influenced by the example of parents. In our home country — Brazil — there is a strong internet culture, and many parents allow children to get entertained using tablets or mobile phones. I recall one time when we visited the country and went for dinner with family. Our son was very small, perhaps 3 years old and was at the dinner table reading a book. The waiter stopped at our table and said:

> “A book! That is so rare! It’s been a long time since I last saw a child without a tablet.”

As crazy as it may sound, it is true. Children are having access screens from very early ages, and that has a deep impact on their brain and cognitive development. Researches show that when a child gets bored, their imagination and creativity is then activated so they can create something fun, whether this is a story, a game, a drawing and other forms to explore their surroundings. This lack of external stimuli activates what we know as divergent thinking which is a vital process for innovation and creative problem-solving. What happens to children’s creativity when we never allow our them to get bored? This question brought up by Paulette Delgado’s in the article “[The Lost Art of Boredom: How Screen Time Is Killing Creativity](https://observatory.tec.mx/edu-news/the-lost-art-of-boredom-how-screen-time-is-killing-creativity/)”, seems to capture the essence of the current problem faced by parents, educators and children. When a child is given a screen they turn into passive consumers of external stimuli being bombarded into their brains, and that kills their motivation and capacity to get creative. Additionally it may also hinder children’s emotional resilience [as highlighted by the psychologist Jenifer Delgado](https://massanarevista.com/2025/07/10/aburrirse-tambien-educa-el-papel-del-aburrimiento-en-la-creatividad-infantil/?utm_source=chatgpt.com).

> “Boredom allows children to connect with their essence and develop an emotional resilience that will be useful to them in adult life.”

## A new drug affecting kids: social media crap

In 2020 we moved to Spain, and we found what we considered a healthier environment where children were encouraged to spend more time playing outdoors. We also felt that parents, in general, shared our belief about the harmful effects of screen time for small children. We also felt hopeful that this environment would help us protect our son from the harmful exposure to social media and its additive effect with mental health implications. [The recent lawsuit involving Meta’s CEO Mark Zuckerberg](https://www.ms.now/news/zuckerberg-testimony-meta-social-media-addiction-trial) reignited the discussion about the connection between Social Media algorithms, addictive content, and mental health of end consumers.

[A comprehensive review of several cross-sectional studies](https://www.cmaj.ca/content/192/6/E136) conducted in different countries pointed out for a correlation between the increase of Social Media usage among children and adolescents and the increase of mental health issues, increased anxiety, sleeping disorders, self-harming behaviours, suicidal ideation and death by suicide (Abi-Jaoude et al., 2020). For example, the review shows that over the last decade in Canadian Hospitals there was a relative increase of 62% in mental distress issues while admissions for other issues decreased by 14%. More alarming is the rate of your girls showing self-harming behavior that went up by 110% from 2009 to 2014, and the increase of suicides among the young population to become second biggest cause of youth death in 2015. In the US, numbers from CDC also indicate a similar phenomena as [shown by a study compiling data from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11763352/). It demonstrates that suicide among children and adolescent between 10 and 19 years old rose in 2021 to become the third leading cause of death only behind unintentional injury and homicide. Let alone the number of hospitalizations of injuries related to suicidal attempts and self harm behaviour. [Another study points out that suicide rates have increased 47.5% since 2000, making it the second leading cause of death in U.S. adolescents prior to 2020](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10784535251328925). It also evidences that depression among adolescents (between 15 and 19 years old) is ranked among top illness causes globally.

Social media platforms amplified its addictive power by engineering ways to increase user engagement by exploring polarizing content, novelty, and social validation over informative, analytical, or trustworth factual information. The algorithm was optimised to show what would keep you scrolling rather than what would make you better informed, and it became the primary way hundreds of millions of people encountered information about the world. The consumption of information from traditional sources — newspapers, books, essays — gave space to fragments of information, headlines without articles, and short stories that are in many cases edited out of context. There are studies showing that the [excessive use of social media is associated with impaired attention, reduced working memory, and diminished problem solving skills](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12887-025-06041-5) (Naik et al., 2025). All key cognitive capabilities required for critical thinking and making sense of complex information.

Said that, it is not a coincidence that legislator in different countries — such as [Australia](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cwyp9d3ddqyo), [Denmark](https://apnews.com/article/denmark-social-media-ban-australia-1e96a3df3276cc2033a6f04effb89f51), [France](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/jan/27/france-social-media-ban-under-15s), [Greece](https://www.reuters.com/sustainability/society-equity/greece-soon-announce-social-media-ban-children-under-15-government-source-says-2026-02-03/), [Malaysia](https://www.nbcnews.com/world/asia/malaysia-says-plans-ban-social-media-users-under-16-starting-2026-rcna245562), [Slovenia](https://www.reuters.com/world/europe/slovenia-preparing-law-ban-access-social-media-minors-under-15-2026-02-05/), and [Spain](https://www.webpronews.com/spains-digital-childhood-protection-act-inside-europes-most-aggressive-social-media-age-restriction-framework/) — are studying measures to protect children and young populations from Social Media, in an attempt to compensate for the lack of effective guardrails implemented by the Social Media companies. It is hard to deny how harmful Social Media has become. It’s documented, and it is in the media: [the latest lawsuit faced by Meta shows undeniable evidence that Instagram was intentionally designed to be addictive and to target adolescents and pre-adolescents](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c5y42znjnjvo).

Those numbers and facts describe a generation of children who grew up with smartphones before they had the emotional architecture to manage them. And our collective failure as a society to enforce regulations that would protect them from a system designed to profit at all costs.

This only illustrates how a technology meant to connect people deviated from its vision as result from loose or non-existent regulation. This is now one of the most pressing and concerning topics with AI rapid advances and scaling. The difference between a toddler handed a screen to avoid boredom and an adult who asks AI to avoid thinking is not one of nature, but of scale.

## Don’t waste your time learning: “just ask AI”

Everything described above — attention fragmentation, passive consumption, retreat from primary sources, the outsourcing of cognition — is about to be amplified by AI on an unprecedented order of magnitude. The effects on our ability to learn, think critically and retain knowledge is also a motive of study and concern.

Please, don’t get me wrong, I am not saying AI tools aren't useful. [They surely can accelerate research and be a useful learning resource, but if misused or used excessively they can have the opposite effect and destroy our ability to learn](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/education/is-ai-making-us-smarter-or-just-making-us-look-smart-).

If you are using AI assistants you know: they don’t just surface information. They synthesize, frame, and present it with the fluency and (over)confidence of an expert. They remove the friction from thinking and trying to connect complex concepts and ideas — and that friction is a vital element for the learning to happen. Saveedra et al. (2025), cited the work of the renowned neuroscientist Barbara Oakley to explain how the learning process works and how AI can influence these processes: “When you first learn something new, you use what scientists call declarative memory: Every step requires full attention. With repeated practice, those brain pathways become automatic, shifting to procedural memory. For example, when you first learn to drive, every movement demands complete focus. Now you navigate traffic while talking. [Oakley and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.11015) have applied this neuroscience to understand AI’s impact. When AI does the thinking for us, this crucial brain transformation never happens. The mental pathways that create genuine expertise simply don’t form.”

So relying heavily on AI-ready answers and its promise of high productivity can hinder our ability to learn and retain knowledge. [For instance Bjork & Bjork (2011) explain a distinction between performance and learning.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/284097727_Making_things_hard_on_yourself_but_in_a_good_way_Creating_desirable_difficulties_to_enhance_learning) According to their research higher performance cannot be a good predictor of learning, as experiments evidence that improved performance might not be directly linked to learning and knowledge retention. Let’s put this way, memorising a sum and knowing its answer by heart does not ensure you learn how to sum up things.

[As highlighted by Savedra et al. (2025)](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/education/is-ai-making-us-smarter-or-just-making-us-look-smart-): “*Looking good in the moment (performance) is different from actually changing what’s stored in your brain long-term (learning). *[*When a student uses ChatGPT to write a flawless essay, their performance looks stellar. But has real learning happened? Often, the answer is no.*](https://twitter.com/)*”*

This transition from searching the web for content, to consuming answers from AI impares our judgment as explored by Pondiscio (2025). According to him, when using search engines you still need to think about keywords, explore the content brought by the search result and judge, based on your interpretation of results and your knowledge, what result is valuable and which result may be less useful. You must know something to judge the results, whereas in AI the judgement element is removed. It does not simply retrieve you with results, but rather it makes an assessment of what is relevant and how to communicate on your behalf. As he points out “AI, used uncritically or as a shortcut, does the opposite. It trains us to trust rather than test what we read. Its convenience invites intellectual passivity. The effect is not enlightenment but rather a collective anesthesia.”

The overuse of AI in education is already measurable. [A nationwide survey conducted in the US highlights that 90% of college faculty believe GenAI will diminish students’ critical thinking skills; 74% say it will affect the value of academic degrees for the worse](https://www.aacu.org/newsroom/national-survey-95-of-college-faculty-fear-student-overreliance-on-ai-and-diminished-critical-thinking-among-learners-who-use-generative-ai-tools) (Gerlich, 2025). In the Tech Industry there are reports showing that junior programmers who learned to code with heavy AI assistance are struggling to debug problems independently or understand the logic of code they didn’t write themselves. Students produce flawless essays they cannot explain. Researchers generate literature reviews on topics they have not actually read in depth. The pattern is consistent: AI allows us to look smart without learning. Over time the gap between what we can produce with AI and what we can actually know without it grows wider. This has major negative implications not only on how we learn things at work but also this may impact how education as a whole system works.

For instance, Pondiscio [explores the work of the American educator E. D. Hirsch](https://thenext30years.substack.com/p/the-illusion-of-learning-the-danger) and use this knowledge to analyse how AI technology applied to education may offer risks for our ability to learn as well as to teach. The “Teacher’s dilemma” describes the danger of AI misuse from the perspective of who teaches. For example, the practice of providing feedback on a student’s work is a foundational part of teaching and a complex one. The teacher must interpret student work, understand what it’s being communicated, whether the student’s understanding is accurate or not, and how to guide improvement. It relies on three core capabilities: the ability to understand the work from the student’s perspective, develop a human connection with the student, and be recognised by the student as someone with authority in the knowledge field, or that could push students to explore multiple perspectives of a problem or subject. If teachers excessively rely or misuse AI they risk losing all these three skills, and at some time frame their knowledge in what I recommend as relevant.

AI also relies on similar mechanisms exploited by Social Media to generate engagement, becoming indispensable in ways that raise questions about who controls knowledge in the future? And, what interests would it serve? This is perhaps one of the most important questions of our time. But here is an even more unsettling question: if people lose their ability to evaluate AI critically, how can they interrogate AI’s claims, notice its biases, recognize when its confident synthesis is concealing genuine uncertainty or corporate interest?

## Keeping Your Critical Thinking in Check

When it comes to disruptive technologies such as AI, we tend to be overly optimistic or pessimistic with what is being out there. Some say AI will “[solve the world’s most pressing problems](https://www.darioamodei.com/essay/machines-of-loving-grace)” while others will lead humanity to “[chaos and suffering (if not destruction)](https://www.darioamodei.com/essay/the-adolescence-of-technology)”. Although theories may be rooted in possible scenarios, they are usually amplified by excitement or fear. Note that a few industry players position themselves as the most ethical or “savior” choice (sometimes based on very arguable yet convincing press releases as we will explore in my upcoming article).

The reality? This is a complex, high-stakes game involving trillions in investments, deep ethical dilemmas around data privacy, control of global tech infrastructure, cybersecurity, tech weaponry, income concentration, mass surveillance, and more. You won’t find the truth on social media feeds or in corporate press releases. You’ll find it by rebuilding the habits of mind this essay has described, and by choosing your sources carefully.

### Three Practices to Start Today

1. Question your own bias first: I learned this the hard way after quitting Facebook. I realized I’d been consuming content that confirmed what I already believed, never challenging my assumptions. Before you settle on what AI means for the future, force yourself to read the strongest arguments against your position. If you’re excited about AI’s potential, read the technical critiques. If you’re convinced it’s overhyped, engage seriously with the research.
1. Follow the money: When someone tells you AI will change everything, ask: who profits if this becomes a common widespread belief? Is this a polished demo shown under controlled conditions, or something tested at scale in real conditions? When companies overpromise and constantly redefine “success” ask yourself why the goalposts moved. These narratives aren’t reliable and are meant to please investors in the technology multi-billion-dollar game. We have seen several examples, from OpenAI statement that it would remain a non-profit organization not to bend its safeguards for money, to Anthropic promise to ensure risk mitigation before scalling potentially dangerous technology.
1. Go to the source: I started doing this after realizing how much gets lost in AI summaries. Read the actual research paper, check who funded the study. When an article cites research, click through and read it yourself. By doing so you will find other angles that not brought in AI summary, and may even provide you additional insights for iterating over your answer or solution.
1. Dedicate some time for offline reading: it may sound obvious or even nostalgic, but I do actually recommend some time dedication to read actual books as one of the key activities to keep your brain healthy. Visiting bookstores and spending time researching the book shelfs could be surprisingly fun and spark your creativity.
1. Using AI to enhance quality of my work rather than purely automate work : I am using AI in some of my work activities, but I am trying to educate myself not to rely on it and become dependent to form my solutions or answers. I am actually finding quite productive to use AI to analyse the work I did, and get feedback on things I can improve on. It also help me to find other works that would compliement mine or even challenge what I have done. So instead of asking AI to do the work for me, I am doing the work and asking AI for feedback, so I can then do more work to make it better. In a sense, I am shifting the focus away from AI as a work accelerator to use AI as a work quality enhancer.

### Read What Companies Promise with pragmatism

Once you’ve built a foundation of independent perspective, then read what AI companies publicly commit to:

- Google AI principles and yearly progress report
- Anthropic purpose and Claude’s constitution
- Open AI charter
- Microsoft AI Responsible Principles

But read them with pragmatism and reflect about questions such as : What specific commitments are being made here? What enforcement mechanisms exist? How would I actually know if these principles were being violated? Who has the power to hold them accountable, and have they ever used it?

## Think as an act of courage

Václav Havel in his essay *The Power of the Powerless*, written from within a totalitarian regime, describes what he called “normalization” — the slow process of accepting what feels wrong simply because it has become normal, out of comfort or lack of courage. However, the concept applies to current days: the normalization of outsourcing our thinking, and the fact that many of us do not challenge that out of convenience or fear.

As Havel maintained: **truthful words and actions of citizens matter, and each one of us has the responsibility to be a bit more courageous than we want to be.**

We have power to influence how technology evolves. But only if we use it now. Not later, after the decisions have been made. Not after we’ve become too dependent to choose differently.
