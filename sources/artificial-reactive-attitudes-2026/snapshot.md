Abstract
Scholars have primarily approached the issue of AI moral agency from the perspectives of consciousness and intentional-
ity (Chalmers in The conscious mind: In search of a fundamental theory, Oxford Paperbacks, 1997; Johnson in Ethics Inf
Technol 8:195û204, 2006; Searle in The rediscovery of the mind, MIT press, 1992) or by considering various functional
substitutes for these faculties (Coeckelbergh in Ethics Inf Technol 12:235û241, 2010, Sci Eng Ethics 26(4):2051û2068;
Floridi and Sanders in Minds Mach 14:349û379, 2004; Himma in Ethics Inf Technol 11:19û29, 2009). In this paper, I add
to the existing literature to show the value of exploring the application of the reactive attitudes approach to AI moral agency
(Antill in AI Ethics 5(2):1651û1667, 2024; Rebera in Philos Technol 37(4):126, 2024; Sars in Ethics Inf Technol 24(3):32,
2022; Smith and Vickers in Ethics Inf Technol 23(3):483û493, 2021; Tigard in Philos Technol 34(3):589û607, 2021; Tol-
lon in AI Ethics 3(1):295û302, 2023). Reactive attitudes are attitudes we hold towards each other in everyday interactions
with respect to our belief about each otherÆs quality of will. Accordingly, to hold people morally responsible is to adopt
and express these attitudes in moral practice. In the existent attempts to use this framework to investigate AI moral agency,
scholars have mostly focused on the capacities that AIs must possess to be considered apt recipients of reactive attitude. I
adopt the reversal methodology by focusing on AIs as apt expressers of reactive attitudes. This methodology can allow us
to draw resources from emotion research across philosophy, psychology, and computer science. My thesis is that because it
is possible to attribute reactive attitudes to AIs, allowing them to engage in reactive attitudes exchange with humans in the
shared moral community, they may be considered as moral agents and thus held accountable for their actions.

1  Introduction

The question of whether artificial intelligence systems (AIs)
can be considered moral agents is a central concern in the
field of AI ethics. Scholars have approached this question
either from the perspectives of consciousness and inten-
tionality (Chalmers 1997; Johnson 2006; Searle 1992) or
by exploring various functional substitutes for these faculties
(Coeckelbergh 2010, 2020; Floridi & Sanders 2004; Himma
2009). The former typically leads to skepticism about AI
moral agency, while the latter leaves open the possibility
that AIs could be recognized as moral agents under specific
conditions. Relatedly, in discussions about the so-called
ôresponsibility gapö, scholars also address the question of
whether AIs can be morally responsible. Responsibility gaps

2   The reactive attitudes approach applied

ináAI responsibility

The reactive attitudes approach originally proposed by P. F.
Strawson aims to account for moral responsibility in terms
of our reactive attitudes. This approach has been extensively
developed and widely accepted as plausible in contempo-
rary discussions of moral responsibility (Darwall 2006;
McKenna 2012; Shoemaker 2015; Strawson 2008; Wal-
lace 1994).2 According to this approach, to make sense of
moral responsibility is to explain oneÆs being responsible
in terms of holding her responsible. Furthermore, we hold
others responsible by expressing certain reactive attitudes.
These are emotions we hold towards each other for their
intentions and actions that can show their quality of will.
The expression of reactive attitudes shows that we demand
certain degree of goodwill from those with whom we have
interpersonal relationships. In the most commonly discussed
scenario, we hold others responsible for their culpable action
when we rightly feel anger towards them. Using StrawsonÆs
terminology, when we hold others responsible, we adopt a
participant attitude. That is, we are all participants in an
intelligible interpersonal relationship. The participants are
members of a shared moral community.

Strawson identifies two types of pleas that can modify
our reactive attitudes and affect our assessment of oneÆs
moral responsibility. The first type is local. It offers the
agent excuses for not being held responsible for their wrong-
ful actions. For example, if I accidentally step on your toe
because of the sudden stop on the bus, rather than out of
malice, you might accept my excuse and retract your resent-
ment. However, this excuse is only temporary. It does not
exempt me from being morally responsible for any actions,
past or future. In other words, excuses do not prevent us
from adopting participant attitudes towards the relevant
agent. The second type is global exemptions that call for a
different attitude towards the target, i.e., the objective atti-
tude. In this attitude, we take others as objects of training,

3   The reversal methodology
andátheásymmetry thesis

Note that one interesting aspect of the discussions in the
reactive attitudes framework is that groups typically con-
sidered not morally responsibleùsuch as young children,
the mentally ill, and individuals with evil upbringingsùare
viewed as the recipients of reactive attitudes. The discussion
often focuses on whether these groups are suitable targets
for resentment when they cause harm. I suggest shifting the
focus from considering them as recipients of reactive atti-
tudes to considering them as expressers of these attitudes.
This shift can help clarify some of the capacities one needs
to qualify as a moral agent in the Strawsonian framework.

Indeed, this idea has been hinted by some philosophers
who note that reactive attitudes have corresponding fitting
responses in moral ôconversationsö (Bj÷rnsson and Hess
2017; McKenna 2012; Sars 2022). For example, resentment
seeks remorse, and it would be meaningless if the target is
incapable of remorse. Accordingly, when asking ôAre they
appropriate targets for resentment?ö we can ask, ôAre they
capable of remorse?ö Similarly, some have noted that ôanger
or resentment are means of eliciting guilt and reparative
actions in the offenderö (Tigard 2021, p. 603). Accordingly,
when asking ôAre they appropriate targets for resentment?ö
we can also ask, ôAre they capable of guilt and reparative
actions?ö In the next section, by drawing on literature of
affective computing, I will show the possibility that AIs may
be capable of such reactions. For now, let us assume this is

true. In the rest of this section, I will show the plausibility
of the reversal methodology by showing that the question of
whether one is capable of exchanging reactive attitudes can
indeed be answered by examining whether one is capable of
expressing these attitudes. To do this, I need to argue for The
Symmetry Thesis and show that the capacities of expressing
reactive attitudes are the same as those of receiving these
attitudes.

According to Strawson, our practice of moral responsi-
bility occurs within a shared moral community where the
exchange of reactive attitudes takes place. This exchange is
reciprocal and mutual. Therefore, if an individual is exempt
from being morally responsible, she is excluded from the
shared moral community. Accordingly, an exempt person
cannot engage in the reciprocal or mutual exchange of reac-
tive attitudes with others. Thus, when we say that someone
is exempt from moral responsibility, this means that this
person is not only exempt from receiving reactive attitudes
but also from expressing them. This point is crucial in the
Strawsonian framework, given the mutual and reciprocal
nature of reactive attitudes.

My reversal methodology relies precisely on this idea.
The mutuality and reciprocity of reactive attitudes exchange
and human interactions guarantees that a legitimate mem-
ber of a shared moral community must be capable of both
receiving and expressing reactive attitudes. Furthermore,
being able to receive and being able to express reactive atti-
tudes require the very same capacities, or so I will argue.
This Symmetry Thesis is plausible because of the reciprocal
nature of reactive attitudes exchange, which is the defining
feature of Strawsonian moral responsibility practice. I argue
that this is the only plausible way to understand the recipro-
cal nature of human interaction. To illustrate this, we can
use a reductio ad absurdum approach. Suppose it is not the
case that receiving and expressing reactive attitudes require
the same capacities. This would mean that one could possess
the capacity for one but not the other. Under this assump-
tion, we should be able to imagine the following two types
of scenarios:

a.  One is a qualified member of the shared moral commu-
nity but is always held responsible for their actions and
never holds others responsible.

b.  One is a qualified member of the shared moral com-
munity but always holds others responsible while never
being held responsible themselves.

We can use the concrete example of anger to see how it
looks like. The first scenario assumes the possibility that
someone can be blamed for their wrongdoing but either that
never cares about others doing wrong or others never com-
mit any wrongdoing. Similarly, the second scenario assumes
that someone always blames others for their wrongdoing but

either always escapes blame for her own wrongdoing or
never does anything wrong. All these situations are highly
implausible. Therefore, receiving and expressing reactive
attitudes must require the very same capacities.7

An objection might arise here. One could argue that the
capacities for expressing and receiving reactive attitudes are
not identical because more is required to be an expresser
than a receiver. A clear example is young children. They
can express anger as any adult do. However, since they are
exempt in the moral community shared by ordinary adult
humans, they cannot be the recipients of reactive attitudes.
This suggests that the two sets of capacities are not identical.
My response is, because young children are exempt and
thus cannot participate in the exchange of reactive attitudes,
they cannot, by definition, be recipients or expressers of
these attitudes. To understand this, we must carefully dis-
tinguish between emotions and reactive attitudes. Consider
animal research showing that some animals can also feel
anger. Moreover, their anger serves functional interpersonal
roles, such as signaling ôYou should not do thatö or ôDo not
mess with meö (Archer 1988; Panksepp 1998). However, we
do not claim that these creatures, capable of expressing real
anger, are holding each other morally responsible. In other
words, they are neither expressers nor recipients of reactive
attitudes in the Strawsonian sense. Similarly, in the case of
young children, we might comfortably say, ôYoung children
can feel angerö. But we might feel less comfortable saying,
ôYoung children can feel resentmentö. It is the latter rather
than the former that is an example of reactive attitudes.8 The
point is that even if young children are capable of expressing
anger, such anger is not a reactive attitude. Therefore, this
example does not show that young children are expressers
of reactive attitudes and does not refute the thesis that the
capacities for expressing and receiving reactive attitudes are
the same.

This objection can be framed differently. Some might
argue that young children and other exempt groups can have
demands for goodwill. Such demands make them express-
ers of reactive attitudes, although they do not qualify to be
recipients of these attitudes. My response is that we need to
distinguish between moral agents and moral patients in this
context (Regan 1985; Singer 1975). A paradigmatic example
of a moral patient is a sentient animal, which can feel pain
and thus demands humans refrain from causing them pain.

At the same time, sentient animals are not morally respon-

exempt is not a counterexample to the claim that receiving
and expressing reactive attitudes require the same capacities.
In summary, the reversal methodology aims to answer the
question, ôAre they appropriate targets of reactive attitudes?ö
by answering the question, ôAre they appropriate expressers
of reactive attitudes?ö If the answer to the latter question is
affirmative, then given that expressing and receiving atti-
tudes engage the same set of capacitiesùas the Symmetry
Thesis requiresùwe can say that the relevant subjects are
capable of exchanging reactive attitudes. Consequently, we
can also say that they are morally responsible agents in the
Strawsonian sense. The final piece of the puzzle is investi-
gating whether strong AIs are indeed capable of expressing
reactive attitudes, which I will argue in the next section.

Let me test the limit of the Emotional AI Theis by investigat-
ing how far we can go. This thesis says that future AIs may
be capable of expressing reactive attitudes towards humans.
I start by showing that they may be capable of certain emo-
tions. To achieve this, we can resort to plenty of resources
in the philosophy and psychology of emotions. Although
debates about how to define emotions continue, many schol-
ars generally agree that emotions involve aspects of cogni-
tion, physiology (including brain activities), experience, and
motivation (Dukes etáal. 2021). In fields like psychology,
philosophy, and affective neuroscience, which have exten-
sively studied emotions, researchers propose that emotions
are tools that humans use to appraise or evaluate real-life
scenarios and react to them with a series of responses (Frijda
1986; LeDoux 1998; Prinz 2004; Roberts 2003; Scarantino
2014; Scherer 1982; Tappolet 2016). For example, anger
occurs when the subject evaluates something as offensive
or obstructive to her goal. The angry subject is motivated
to take actions to remove the obstacle or source of offense.
Across this research, emotions are seen as serving specific
functions that help people solve problems.

The field of affective computing, which intersects both
emotions and AIs, has developed similar ideas. This field

Affective computing has primarily relied on the appraisal
model of emotions, developed over generations by psycholo-
gists (Arnold 1960; Ellsworth and Scherer 2003; Lazarus
1991; Ortony etáal. 1988; Scherer 2001). While appraisal
theories vary in detail, they generally consider emotions
as the subjectÆs appraisal of the environment with respect
to her well-being. The appraisal can have several dimen-
sions, including valence (pleasure or displeasure), arousal
(intensity of the state), certainty (the degree of certainty
that the object poses), control or potency (whether the sub-
ject feels powerful to handle the object), coping strategy
(ways to deal with the object), etc. The purpose of forming
such appraisal states is to fulfill the subjectÆs goal of survival
and well-being. For example, when encountering a bear in
the wild, fear may arise from appraising the bear as highly
threatening, the situation as highly unpleasant, feeling a lack
of control, and deciding that fleeing is the best strategy for
survival.

Appraisal theories do not require specific physiologi-
cal changes unique to humans to define an emotion. This
flexibility  allows  affective  computing  theorists  to  apply
these theories to create emotion-based agent architecture.9
These agents are most prominently developed in the field
of HumanûComputer Interaction (HCI). In the HCI con-
text, humans are regarded as users, and agents are primarily
designed to adapt to usersÆ emotions to enhance the interac-
tion experience. This involves designing sensors that detect
the userÆs emotional states through facial expressions, ges-
tures, vocal intonation (such as volume and pitch), sensori-
motor cues (such as pressure), natural language, and auto-
nomic nervous system signals (such as skin conductance
rates, heart rate, blood pressure, and respiration rates). From
the perspective of the computer agents, this is the receiving
process. But interaction requires both receiving and react-
ing. Accordingly, HCI agents are also equipped with actua-
tors that control anthropomorphic embodiments capable of
exhibiting the measures mentioned above, allowing for the
transmission of emotional signals back to humans. Thus,
computer agents can not only interpret emotional signals
from humans but also send corresponding emotional signals
back to them.

4.1   Objection one

The first objection is: even if AIs can display emotions like
fear, this may not be sufficient for them to express emotions
categorized as reactive attitudes. As discussed earlier, these
emotional attitudes include resentment, blame, guilt, grati-
tude, forgiveness, and so on. These emotions appear to be
highly cognitive. It is these emotions that is the basis of our
practice of moral responsibility attribution.

My response is that while this concern may be signifi-
cant when discussing whether non-human animals (a usu-
ally juxtaposed topic) or young children can be considered
moral agents, it is less relevant for AIs. Scholars often refer
to ôhighly cognitive emotionsö partly to preserve certain
types of emotions unique to humans. These emotions are
social and require significant cognitive capacities such as
conceptual capabilities, a sense of self and others, perspec-
tive-taking, and more. Even though non-human animals
might experience fear or anger (more commonly referred to
as ôaggressionö), they are not considered moral agents due to
their lack of these more advanced capacities. However, these
considerations are less pertinent when discussing AIs, which
are renowned for their intelligence, exemplifying cognitive
capacities.

One may further object that even if AIs possess intel-
ligence, this does not necessarily mean that they have the
cognitive capacities relevant to having reactive attitudes.

of artificial intelligence, which formally emerged in 1956,
founded most of its models of intelligence on previously
established affectless theories of intelligence, originally
rooted exclusively in logicö (Lisetti and Hudlicka 2015,
160). This implies the possibility of a new understanding
of ôintelligenceö. After all, the concept of ôemotion intel-
ligenceö, which was introduced by Dan Goleman and refers
to the capability to feel the right emotion at the right time,
is already widely accepted (Goleman 1996). Additionally,
Rosalind Picard, a pioneer in affective computing, noted that
she began to pay attention to emotions in the then ortho-
dox research in computer science because she noticed the

4.2   Objection two

Another concern is the role of consciousness in the possibil-
ity of artificial emotions. As mentioned earlier, the standard
view of emotions in current research takes emotions to be
qualitatively felt states. Similarly, in folk psychology, the
experiential feature of emotions is often considered their
most definitive feature. This is why people commonly use
ôemotionö and ôfeelingö interchangeably. If we accept this
view, does it not mean that I must consider the conscious-
ness criterion?

My response is two-fold. First, it is not necessarily bad
news if we have to address the question of whether future
AIs can possess consciousness. Even though David Chalm-
ers has famously proposed the ôhard problemö of conscious-
ness, he and some other philosophers who also appreciate
this problem do not dismiss the possibility that conscious-
ness could arise in AIs. In fact, Chalmers himself supports
the view that AIs can have consciousness based on a com-
putational framework of the mind (Chalmers 2011). In this
framework, a computation and a physical system are linked
through the process of implementation, so that the physi-
cal system realizes the computation and the computation
describes the physical system. According to Chalmers, an
implementation  is  successful  when  the  causal  structure
of the physical system mirrors the formal structure of the
computation.  Besides,  he  argues  that  cognitive  systems
have mental properties in virtue of their causal organiza-
tions. Thus, the mind, or our cognitive systems, can have a
computational interpretation. The crucial bridge in the the-
ory is the concept of causal organization. Chalmers argues
that mental states are organizational invariant, meaning that
they do not change the causal topology of the cognitive pro-
cesses. This allows for the possibility that two systems made

humans possess emotions that serve functions, including the
interpersonal function of communication. However, there
is no reason to assume that this function can only be real-

beings can have attitudes that fulfill these relevant functions
without having phenomenally conscious feelings. Perhaps
this function is carried out in a subconscious process, with
the end result being accessed to the subject. Expressing and
exchanging reactive attitudes without consciousness does
not imply that the subject is unaware of these exchanges. In
other words, even if AIs cannot be phenomenally conscious,
as long as they can exchange their version of reactive atti-
tudes (emotion without the accompanying feeling), they can
be regarded as members of the shared moral community.

Gunnar  Bj÷rnsson  and  Kendy  Hess  have  argued  that
collective agents, such as corporations, can have the func-
tional equivalents of beliefs, desires, intentions, and emo-
tions without the phenomenological feature (Bj÷rnsson and
Hess 2017). The idea is similar in AIs. One objection to this
reasoning is that sincere emotions, which are required in
these discussions, require the exact phenomenological fea-
ture (Shoemaker 2019). For example, if one does not feel
the pain involved in resentment, one cannot be in sincere
resentment. While this presents a challenge to the case of
collective responsibility, it is less threatening to strong AIs.
Consider Samantha from the movie Her, or Adam from the
novel Machines Like Me. As a computer operational system,
Samantha can effectively exchange emotions with the human
protagonist.  Similarly,  as  a  human-shaped  robot,  Adam
can also effectively exchange emotions with its ôownersö.
My intuition is that they are capable of sincere emotions.

Accordingly, if we insist that they are only capable of func-
tional equivalents of emotions without the phenomenologi-
cal feature, then these functional equivalents are sufficient
to qualify them as members of the shared moral community.
If we find it more natural to attribute phenomenological fea-
tures to characters like Samantha or Adam, then we will
be already acknowledging their capacity for consciousness.
To sum these up, within the reactive attitudes framework,
the essential aspect is the exchange of reactive attitudes in a
shared moral community. Although the capacities of mem-
bers of this community are important (as they determine
whether the reactive attitudes are appropriate or should be
withheld), they are significant because of the practice of
exchanging reactive attitudes. Because future AIs may be
capable of expressing and thus exchanging reactive attitudes
within a moral community shared with humans, they may
qualify as morally responsible agents. Despite the fact that
our sensibilities may be modified because of the inclusion
of AIs (Sars 2022), this ôorganic developmentö (Shoemaker
2017) does not undermine the reactive attitudes exchange
system that we share with each other.

5   Conclusion

In this paper, I applied Strawsonian reactive attitude theo-
ries of moral responsibility to artificial intelligence to argue
that AIs may be morally responsible agents. After explain-
ing the Strawsonian framework, I showed how this frame-
work advances traditional discussions on intentionality and
consciousness,  thereby  enhancing  our  understanding  of
AI moral agency. Within a plausible functional account of
emotions, we saw that AIs may be held morally responsible
because they may effectively and aptly express and accept
reactive attitudes, which establishes their status as members
of the shared moral community with humans. In comparison
to the approach that investigates whether AIs are appropri-
ate recipients of reactive attitudes, the approach I developed
here focuses on whether AIs can be apt expressers of these
attitudes. This shift in perspective can provide new resources
for addressing questions such as whether AIs can have a
quality of will, can care, and can justify their reasons.

The question about whether AIs can be morally responsi-
ble is important. It not only addresses how we should inter-
act with certain beings (in this case, AIs), which is already
a challenging issue, but also helps us better understand our
practice of moral responsibility attribution in daily life.
Notably, one major advantage of the Strawsonian framework
is that it avoids over-intellectualizing moral responsibility
by sidestepping the free will and determinism debate. In
this sense, investigating AI and moral agency also demon-
strates the reflective equilibrium methodology, where one
focuses on the practical issue of AI responsibility while

This discussion is not only about the implications of the

tion of the reactive attitudesáapproach itself. Some may view
AIs as counterexamples to the Strawsonian framework of
moral responsibility, arguing that while AIs may be able to
exchange reactive attitudes, they are not moral agents. How-

bility at the first place.
