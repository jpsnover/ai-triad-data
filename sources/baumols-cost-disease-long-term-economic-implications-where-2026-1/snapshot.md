Education  has  long  suffered  from  what  economists  term

ôBaumolÆs  cost  disease,ö  a  tendency  for  costs  to  rise  faster

than  general  inflation  due  to  stagnant  productivity  in  labor-

intensive  sectorsá (Kimball  &  Luke,  2018?.  In  higher  education

this  phenomenon  is  sometimes  dubbed  ôBowenÆs  curseö

?Baumol,  2012?.  Essentially,  teaching

is  time  and

labor

intensive:  a  professor  in  the  2020s  still  needs  to  spend

roughly  the  same  amount  of  time  to  effectively  teach  or

mentor  a  student  as  decades  ago,  just  as  ôit still takes four

musicians nine minutes to perform BeethovenÆs String Quartet

in C minor, as it did in the nineteenth centuryöá?Kimball & Luke,

2018?.  In  contrast,  technology  has  dramatically  boosted

productivity  in  manufacturing  and  other  sectors,  enabling

fewer workers to produce more output.

The  crux  of  the  issue  is  that  educationÆs  core  service  cannot

be easily accelerated or automated without sacrificing quality.

Persistence of Cost Disease in
Education

BaumolÆs  cost  disease  theory  holds  that  sectors  requiring

direct  human  input  (like  teaching)  experience  relatively  low

productivity  growth  because  they  cannot  significantly  speed

up  or  substitute  the  human  labor  involvedá (Costa  Nunes  &

Nunes,  2024?.  In  education,  ôthe  labor  is  itself  the  end

productö?Kimball  &  Luke,  2018?,  a  teacherÆs  presence  and

interaction  is  fundamental  to  the  service.  No  matter  how

advanced  technology  becomes,  most  teaching  tasks  remain

emphasizing

interpersonal

words,  AI  cannot  conduct  a  parent-teacher  conference,

inspire  a  struggling  student,  or  manage  a  lively  classroom

discussion with the same effectiveness as a human teacher.

human  oversight  remains  necessary  to  ensure  accuracy,

appropriateness,  and  emotional  support  (Anderson,  2023?.

education  tends  to  keep  climbing  relative  to  other  sectors

because teaching still requires intensive human involvement.

BaumolÆs cost disease serves as a sobering reminder that not

all  problems  yield  to  technological  quick  fixes.  Education,  at

its  heart,  is  a  person-to-person  endeavor.  Thus,  even  the

most  sophisticated  AI  will  not  radically  increase  throughput

without risking the loss of human connection and quality. The

analysis  above  has  illustrated  why  AI  has  so  far  failed  to

resolve  the  cost  disease  in  education.  Consequently,  costs

continue to rise, straining schools and governments, while the

specter of declining quality and growing inequality looms if we

respond poorly.

"The situation calls for bold recognition that

education is a ôstagnantö sector that nonetheless

underpins all other progress. Rather than viewing the

rising cost of education as a crisis to avoid, we should

view it as a natural investment in our future, one that

can be managed and is affordable."
