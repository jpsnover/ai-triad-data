ABSTRACT
The  past  decade  has  witnessed  a  transformation  in  the  study  of  authoritarian
governance,  with  increasing  attention  paid  to  how  digital  technologies  reshape
strategies  of  regime  survival.  While  initial  research  focused  on  individual  digital
strategies  like  censorship,  internet  shutdowns,  or  online  propaganda,  emerging
scholarship  conceptualizes  the  broader,  meta-level  effects  of  digitalization  on
authoritarian  tactics.  Building  on  these  advancements,  we  introduce  the  concept  of
authoritarian  informationalism  to  capture  the  evolving  interplay  between  analogue
and  digital  strategies  of  surveillance,  manipulation,  and  information  control.  This
approach  emphasizes  the  centrality  of  information  flows  in  contemporary  political
power,  highlighting  a  shift  from  visible,  repressive  coercion  towards  more  covert,
preventive,  and  targeted  forms  of  digital  repression.  Authoritarian  informationalism
is  characterized  by  (1)  an  expansion  of  actors  �  from  states  to  private  entities  and
individuals;  (2)  increased  precision,  moving  from  reactive  to  pre-emptive  and  fine-
grained  control;  and  (3)  broader  scope,  extending  from  local  and  national  to
transnational  domains  and  across  regime  types.  Importantly,  this  transformation
blurs  traditional  boundaries  between  autocracies  and  democracies,  as  well  as
between  domestic  and  international  spheres.  Our  framework  and  the  contributions
to  this  Special  Issue  bridge  empirical  analyses  of  digital  tools  and  theoretical
debates  on  authoritarian  resilience,  illustrating  both  the  extension  of  authoritarian
informational power and the emergence of new vulnerabilities and dependencies.

Introduction

Over the past decade, empirical studies of modern authoritarianism have increasingly
examined digital strategies and their effects. Research spans a wide spectrum � from
blunt  and  overt  measures  such  as  complete  internet  shutdowns  and  censorship,1 to
more  covert  and  sophisticated  forms  of  filtering  and  manipulating  online  content,2
and the targeted monitoring of individuals.3

More  recently,  scholars  have  begun  to  investigate  the  broader,  meta-level  impli-
cations  of  this  expanding  digital  toolkit  for  authoritarian  resilience.  These  contri-
butions  all  share  a  common  goal:  to  conceptualize  how  digital  technologies
complement,  substitute,  or  transform  existing  strategies  of  authoritarian  survival.  In

Unlike traditional forms of authoritarianism, which rested heavily on overt and coer-
cive forms of repression, authoritarian informationalism thrives on covert repression
which is more targeted and less visible but, importantly, not necessarily less severe for
those affected. Crucially, this logic transcends the classical boundaries of regime type
and  frequently  operates  across  borders.  We  propose  that  the  transformation  of  tra-
ditional authoritarian strategies in the digital era unfolds along three key dimensions:
(1)  Actors;  shifting  from  monopolistic  state  control  to  the  involvement  of  private
actors and even individuals, (2) Precision; evolving from reactive and blunt measures
to preventive and fine-grained forms of control, and (3) Scope; expanding from local
domains to global arenas and across regime types. Considered together, we believe that
changes in these dimensions lead to a broader transformation in the logic of author-
itarian rule.

Thinking in terms of these dimensions is not only conceptually clarifying but also of
practical  relevance  for  empirical  scholarship.  It  encourages  researchers  to  move
beyond  cataloguing  individual  digital  tools  and  instead  to  ask  how  specific  technol-
ogies  or  tactics  map  onto  broader  changes  in  actors,  precision,  and  scope  and  with
what  consequences.  This  perspective  can  guide  case  selection,  help  in  comparing
across regimes, and sharpen measurement strategies � for example, by distinguishing
between state-led propaganda versus individual pro-regime influencers, between reac-
tive  versus  preventive  censorship,  or  between  domestic  versus  transnational  infor-
mation  collection,  processing,  manipulation,  and  control.  In  short,  the  framework
provides  a bridge  between  the  fast-moving  landscape  of  digital  technologies  and  the
more enduring theoretical debates on authoritarian resilience.

Our Special Issue brings together research that illuminates how information man-
agement in autocracies is being transformed in terms of actors involved, precision, and
scope. It contributes to broader debates on how digital tools complement or reinforce
established analogue strategies. Collectively, the studies demonstrate that digitalization
extends authoritarian informational power without changing its core objectives. At a

Authoritarian informationalism

Guriev and Treisman9 coined the term informational autocrats to describe a new type
of  authoritarianism  that  relies  less  on  overt  repression  and  ideology  and  more  on
manipulating information to maintain power. Instead of terrorizing the entire popu-
lation  with  bloody  repression,  informational  autocrats  work  on  shaping  public
opinion and controlling the narrative so that the population believes in the legitimacy
of the regime. Instead of cultivating a discourse of threat and fear by publicly jailing or
killing regime opponents, informational autocrats try to conceal violence and opt for
public communication strategies that emphasize economic performance and national
pride to build legitimacy. Instead of imposing a rigid ideology onto the masses, infor-
mational autocrats intervene surgically and want to appear as pragmatic and compe-
tent  rulers.  As  Guriev  and  Treisman  illustrate,  many  of  these  new  informational
autocrats came into power via elections and want to maintain a facade of democracy
while they covertly transform political institutions.10

While Guriev and Treisman focus on political regimes, the term �informationalism�
� originating in sociology � more broadly describes the revolution of how information
is produced, processed, and distributed in the digital age.11 Castells coined the term to
capture the transformation of industrialism and capitalism into a new mode of devel-
opment that is based on the generation and processing of information rather than the
production  of  material  goods.  Computers  and  digital  communication  technologies
drive  this  revolution;  however,  informationalism  is  not  just  about  technology  but
about a broader social and economic transformation that affects all aspects of society.12
Already in 2010, Jiang13 introduced the term authoritarian informationalism in the
context  of  China  to  describe  the  regime�s  emerging  internet  policies  and  regulatory
model  that  combines  elements  of  capitalism,  authoritarianism,  Confucianism,  and
informationalism. As per Jiang, the Chinese model of authoritarian informationalism
is characterized by a strong state presence in the digital  realm, with the government
actively  shaping  the  development  of  the  internet  and  using  it  as  a  tool  for  social
control.  At  the  same  time,  the  Chinese  government  has  embraced  market-oriented
reforms  and  allowed  for  the  growth  of  private  enterprises  in  the  digital  sector.14
This combination of state control and market-oriented reforms has created a distinc-
tive model of internet governance that is increasingly influential globally and also imi-
tated by other authoritarian regimes.15

We build on these works and suggest to extend the concept of authoritarian infor-
mationalism beyond China to capture a broader cross-regime logic of rule that empha-
sizes the surveillance, manipulation, and control of information as a central mode of
political  power.  Similar  to  Guriev  and  Treisman,16 we  argue  that  unlike  traditional
forms of authoritarianism, which rested heavily on repression and coercion, authori-
tarian informationalism relies on subtle techniques: online influence operations which
go  beyond  disinformation  to  artificially  amplify  regime  support,17 strategic  agenda-
setting,  algorithmic  filtering,  and  the  monopolization  of  digital  infrastructures.  In

sophisticated  techniques  of  manipulating  public  discourse  and  shaping  perceptions
of truth and legitimacy. In this regard, we rely on Glasius�18 practice-based definition
of authoritarianism which cuts across different regime types and focuses on practices
that  subvert  accountability  by  disabling  access  to  (reliable,  not  manipulated)  infor-
mation and citizens� voice. Apart from that, we illustrate three key features of author-
itarian  informationalism  that  highlight  how  this  new  concept  goes  beyond  existing
notions of digital authoritarianism and therefore helps to better understand the trans-
formation of authoritarian rule in the digital age.

First, digital authoritarianism is not a new phenomenon but rather a transformation
of existing authoritarian tactics through the use of digital technologies. We refer to this
as authoritarian informationalism to highlight that central to this transformation is the
fight over  monopolizing and  controlling  information flows.  We do  not  call  it  digital
authoritarianism  because the  term suggests  that  digital  technologies are  the  defining
feature  of  contemporary  authoritarianism.  While  digital  technologies  are  important
tools  for  today�s  authoritarian  regimes,  we  argue  that  the  phenomenon  cuts  across
digital and analogue realms and that it is the interplay between and effective merger
of offline and online tactics that is crucial to understanding the transformation of tra-
ditional authoritarian strategies and brings new actors into play.

Second, earlier work has argued that informational autocrats opt for less violent and
more  subtle  forms  of  control.19 We  argue  that  digitalization  has  transformed  tra-
ditional  authoritarian  strategies  of  maintaining  power  and  control  in  ways  that
make  them  more  preventive,  targeted,  and  less  visible.  However,  while  the  quantity
of  overt,  physical  repression  may  decline,  the  quality  of  repression  changes  in  ways
that make it more sophisticated, data-driven, and difficult to detect but not necessarily
less  severe  for  the  affected  individuals.  This  has  important  implications  for  how  we
understand and respond to authoritarian styles of ruling in the digital age.

Third,  authoritarian  informationalism  reaches  beyond  the  boundaries  of  single
regime types and countries. The observed practices of surveilling, manipulating, and
controlling  information  ecosystems  are  not  confined  to  autocracies  but  are  increas-
ingly creeping into democratic politics as well. The transnational dimension of author-
itarian informationalism is  also important,  as  authoritarian leaders seek  to influence
governance elsewhere.20

In the following, we illustrate how authoritarian informationalism transforms tra-
ditional authoritarian strategies of surveillance, manipulation, and control along three
dimensions:  (1)  Actors;  from  state  to  private,  from  organizations  to  individuals,  (2)
precision;  from  reaction  to  prevention,  from  blunt  to  fine-grained  control,  and  (3)
scope; from local to global and across regime types. Table 1 provides an overview of
these dimensions and their implications for the transformation of authoritarian rule.

Digitalization fundamentally transforms the precision and scope of the collection,
surveillance and processing of information by governments. The dramatic reduction
in the cost of data collection enables these regimes to gather vast quantities of infor-
mation  with  unprecedented  granularity  and  scope.  Critically,  these  technologies
address  one  of  the  core  dilemmas  authoritarian  rulers  face:  citizens�  reluctance  to
share  their  true  preferences,  leaving  the  rulers  uncertain  about  the  actual  level  of
support  they  have.25 While  new  technologies  enable  the  constant  monitoring  of
entire populations and the early detection of critical voices, they also make it possible
to  intrusively  spy  on  targeted  individuals  �  even  beyond  a  country�s  traditional
borders.

To collect information about its citizens at large, governments use various new tools
that improve the scale of traditional ways of monitoring.26 For instance, by deploying
e-government  platforms  to  deliver  administrative  services  online,  governments  not
only  streamline  bureaucratic  processes  and  foster  a  fa�ade  of  transparency  and
citizen participation, but also systematically capture detailed data on citizens� prefer-
ences and behaviours at scale. As shown in four post-Soviet cases, such digital initiat-
ives are strategically leveraged to broaden popular support and legitimize authoritarian
rule.27 Even  more  intrusive  is  China�s  Social  Credit  System  (SCS),  which  has  been

extend their reach transnationally, exploiting global data flows to surveil diaspora com-

Anstis37 illustrate  how  these  new  forms  of  more  covert  and  less  visible  repression
beyond national borders severely impact the lives of diaspora communities.38

In sum, the transformations in actors, precision, and scope of information collec-
tion  significantly  enhance  states�  capacity  for  surveillance.  The  expanded  scope
allows for mass data gathering across entire populations and geographic boundaries,
while  improvements  in  precision  enable  more  granular  and  accurate  monitoring  of
individuals  and  specific  groups.  Meanwhile,  the  involvement  of  a  diverse  range  of
actors  �  including  state  agencies,  private  spyware  firms,  technology  providers,  and
global social media platforms � facilitates access to advanced tools and transnational
data  flows.  Together,  these  developments  greatly  increase  the  volume,  variety,  and
detail  of  information  that  governments  can  acquire,  strengthening  their  overall
capacity for comprehensive and targeted data collection.

Manipulation

With  the  rise  of  authoritarian  informationalism,  the  actors  involved  in  information
manipulation are diversifying. Beyond state-run media, individual users and partisan
influencers can now amplify regime narratives, blurring the line between orchestrated
propaganda and grassroots expression.39 For instance, Osmundsen et al.,40 drawing on
a sample of US-American Twitter users, show that individuals who report hating their
political opponents are important amplifiers of fake news and derogatory content. At
the  same  time,  social  media  companies  play  a  problematic  role:  their  algorithms,
designed  to  maximize  engagement  and  revenue,  boost  polarizing  and  manipulative
content.41 For example, during Anti-Maduro protests in Venezuela in 2014, Venezue-
lan  legislators  used  Twitter  to  spread  competing  narratives  to  efficiently  manage  the
conflict.42 This  convergence  of  state,  individual,  and  corporate  actors  complicates
accountability, requiring responses that address not only government censorship but

Contemporary  information  manipulation  has  expanded  dramatically  in  precision
and scope. Where regimes once relied primarily on centralized outlets like television
or  radio,  digital  technologies  now  enable  them  to  �flood�  the  internet  with  regime-
approved  and  increasingly  AI-generated  narratives  while  drowning  out  opposition
voices43 and making it increasingly difficult to discern fact from fiction.44 This strategy
enables authoritarian regimes to shape public opinion, bolster legitimacy, and reduce
threats to regime stability. As Roberts45 notes, this strategy raises the costs for users,
who must spend more time and energy to locate reliable information and distinguish
it from false or biased content. Experimental studies further demonstrate how challen-
ging it has become for individuals to separate fact from fiction, propaganda and disin-
formation  from  journalism,  thereby  underscoring  how  information  manipulation
increasingly blurs the truth.46

With respect to scope, the deployment of bots, trolls, and, more recently, AI-based
tools such as deepfakes illustrates the extensive capacity of digitally mediated mechan-
isms to disseminate and amplify narratives. Case studies highlight how bots and trolls
play  a  central  role  in  disseminating  propaganda  and  disinformation.  In  Russia,  for
example, bots were mobilized to counter large online and offline protests47 and to cam-
paign  against  opposition  leader  Alexei  Navalny.48 Authoritarian  actors  also  deploy
such  tools  beyond  their  own  borders.  During  the  2016  US  presidential  campaign,
Russian trolls amplified partisan messages to bolster Republican support49 while sim-
ultaneously  spreading  apolitical  content  to  divert  public  attention  from  politics.50
Another  tool  that  recently  gained  more  attention  is  deepfakes  �  AI-generated  hyper
realistic  fake  images  or  videos  �  that  have  been  used  to  manipulate  individuals�
opinions. For example, during India�s 2019 elections, several deepfake videos imperso-
nating prominent candidates went viral, casting doubts on the candidates� credibility.51
Deepfakes are also used by several far-right groups and parties in Western democracies
to spread disinformation and manipulate public opinion. In Germany, for example, the
far-right party AfD spread deepfake videos to manipulate public opinion and influence
electoral  outcomes  during  the  most  recent  elections  in  February  2024.52 Deepfakes
have  become  a  tool  increasingly  used  in  the  context  of  elections  in  recent  years  and
can be harmful not only to individuals� reputation but also to social institutions like
trust.53

Apart from the possibility to spread messages at scale, digital means also allow for
automatically personalizing content in a way that it caters to specific groups in society.
For instance, during Taipeh�s mayoral race in 2014, campaigners for independent can-
didate Ko Wen-je used crawler bots to gather data from Facebook pages, classify users
into interest groups, and send tailored messages to different groups encouraging them
to  vote  for  their  candidate.54 What  makes  computational  propaganda  particularly
effective is that it allows for identifying and targeting the small number of individuals
who  are  most  susceptible  to  manipulation.55 Generative  AI  further  amplifies  these
dynamics by enhancing the targeting and persuasiveness of fake news, disinformation,
and propaganda, ensuring the �right� narratives reach this group of the most suscep-
tible individuals. Combined with bots and sockpuppets that divert attention and dis-
credit opponents, these technologies allow for far more refined manipulation than the
broad, one-size-fits-all broadcasts of the past.

Control

Controlling information has been transformed by major shifts in the actor landscape.
Authoritarian  regimes  once  depended  mainly  on  state-run  media  and  government
censors  to  control  information  flows,  but  the  rise  of  digital  technologies  has  created
a  far  more  fragmented  and  multi-layered  landscape  of  actors.  Today,  private  firms
provide  much  of  the  infrastructure  necessary  to  access  the  internet,56 making  them
central  to  the  implementation  of  online  censorship  and  shutdowns.  At  the  same
time,  the  proliferation  of  content  providers  and  platforms  complicates  the  field  of
information  control.  While  some  states  operate  their  own  platforms  �  most  promi-
nently, China with platforms like WeChat, Weibo or Red Note � many authoritarian
regimes  rely  on  international  platforms  such  as  Facebook,  X,  or  TikTok,  making  it
more difficult to control and take down content. This shift has amplified contestation
between governments, global platforms, and civil society over who decides the bound-
aries of legitimate information � sometimes resulting in outright platform bans,57 data
localization laws,58 and pressure on platforms to comply with local censorship requests
or risk being blocked.

Digitalization  has  greatly  enhanced  both  the  scope  and  precision  of  information
control. Traditional approaches � often reactive  and blunt  � are giving way  to more
proactive,  preventive  strategies  that  suppress  information  even  before  it  is  created.
States  can  now  block  entire  platforms,  specific  domains,  or  even  individual  posts
and accounts, often within seconds. In China, for instance, �keyword blocking� auto-
matically prevents users from posting content that contains banned words.59 World-
wide,  governments  strategically  deploy  internet  shutdowns  during  contested  events
such  as  elections  or  protests  to  disrupt  potential  mobilization.60 Such  shutdowns,
whether nationwide or targeted at specific regions or demographics, can significantly
disrupt  information  flows  at  the  most  critical  moments.61 The  ability  to  disconnect
entire  communication  networks  swiftly  and  selectively  empowers  authorities  to
control precisely  who  receives � and  who  is denied  � access  to information,  at  what
times and in which locations.

Moreover, digitalization enables regimes to exercise sophisticated control over the
content itself. Advanced filtering and censorship mechanisms allow for the shaping of
public discourse by controlling which content becomes visible, effectively steering nar-
ratives and silencing sensitive topics without overtly shutting down entire information
channels.62 In  China,  for  instance,  censorship  is  highly  selective:  posts  that  criticize
government  performance  may  remain  online,  while  those  likely  to  inspire  collective
action  are  removed.63 Targeted  cyberattacks  on  opposition  websites  supplement
these  efforts,  effectively  incapacitating  critical  voices  in  a  focused  and  often  less
visible way.64

However,  even  as  technological  innovations  expand  states�  capacities  for  control,
the growing diversity of actors involved in the digital information ecosystem � particu-
larly global platforms and private infrastructure providers � complicates the exercise of
such control. This increasingly fragmented environment has prompted many author-
itarian  governments  to  supplement  technical  measures  with  legal  and  regulatory
instruments. Sweeping �fake news� and cybersecurity laws65 serve not only to crimina-
lize  dissent  but  also  to  reassert  state  authority  over  the  transnational  flow  of  infor-
mation, embedding censorship within the legal architecture of governance itself.

Overall, the changes in precision, scope, and actors involved in information man-
agement  contribute  to  a  transformation  in  the  logic  of  authoritarian  rule.  Rather
than  relying  primarily  on  overt  propaganda  and  coercive  censorship  designed  to
project  regime  strength,  digitalization  enables  more  subtle  and  persuasive  forms  of
influence  aimed  at  shaping  perceptions  of  truth  and  legitimacy.  Digital  platforms
blur  the  boundaries  between  state  and  non-state  messaging,  as  influencers,  trolls,
and seemingly independent accounts amplify regime narratives. This diffusion compli-
cates attribution, lowers the visibility of state involvement, and makes fabricated infor-
mation flows more difficult to detect and counter.

At the same time, digitalization does not solely strengthen authoritarian control � it
also  equips  activists,  journalists,  and  human  rights  organizations  with  new  tools  to
document repression and mobilize international pressure.66 These dynamics reinforce
a broader shift from blunt repression towards more preventive, fine-grained strategies
of control.67 By enabling regimes to anticipate and pre-empt dissent, digital technol-
ogies  reduce  the  need  for  overt  coercion,  allowing  authoritarian  rulers  to  maintain
power  while  minimizing  visible  repression.  However,  this  transformation  also  raises
significant  challenges  for  detection  and  accountability,  as  more  covert  forms  of
control become harder to identify and counter.

The contributions to this Special  Issue

The  seven  articles  in  this  Special  Issue  investigate  the  transformation  of  traditional
authoritarian  strategies  and  illustrate  how  authoritarian  regimes  use  digital  technol-
ogies  to  enhance  surveillance,  manipulation,  and  control.  The  contributions  cover  a
range  of  contexts,  including  Iran,  Russia,  Belarus,  Ukraine,  Thailand,  Saudi  Arabia,
and various African countries. Collectively, they examine the three aspects of author-
itarian informationalism: First, the shift from state to private actors and from organ-
izations  to  individuals  is  illustrated  by  how  modern  authoritarian  rule  relies  on
individual regime supporters,68 how individuals support internet voting despite its vul-
nerabilities,69 how foreign direct investment is used as a form of authoritarian collab-
oration  to  develop  digital  infrastructure,70 and  how  inviting  private  firms  into  the
digital  economy  can  also  constrain  authoritarianism.71 Second,  the  shift  from  mass
to  targeted  and  less  visible  repression  is  shown  by  how  online  smear  campaigns
reinforce  judicial  harassment,72 and  how  preventive  internet  regulations  restrict
digital  rights.73 Third,  the  shift  of  authoritarian  strategies  from  local  to  global  levels
and  across  regime  types  is  highlighted  by  how  authoritarian  practices  are  also
applied  by  democratically  elected  governments.74 Below,  we  briefly  summarize  the
main findings of each article and highlight how they contribute to our understanding
of authoritarian informationalism.

The contribution by Schmidt-Feuerheerd75 draws on approximately 100 interviews
with nationalist and patriotic journalists, intellectuals, and influencers in Saudi Arabia
to examine the phenomenon of how private individuals contribute to regime stability
through  what  the  author  calls  collaborative  authoritarianism.  Schmidt-Feuerheerd
finds that individuals who identify as regime supporters actively police dissenters on
Twitter,  generating  an  impression  of  societal  consensus  that  bolsters  the  legitimacy
of  the  regime.  This  is  particularly  notable  because,  unlike  closed  authoritarian
regimes such as China, which operate their own social media platforms, many author-
itarian governments allow � and rely on � Western-based platforms like X and Meta,

where they cannot directly control content. Instead, the regime depends on its individ-
ual and vigilante supporters to indirectly shape a narrative favourable to the incum-
bent.  This  is  possible  because  the  algorithms  on  these  platforms  incentivize  mob
attacks,  thereby  reinforcing  the  �impression  of  consensus  about  what  society
�thinks�.�76 Overall, the article illustrates how authoritarian regimes can leverage indi-
vidual regime supporters on global digital platforms to create a sense of legitimacy and
suppress dissent, even in the absence of direct control over the platforms themselves.
In their article, Burkhardt and Dollbaum77 use evidence from six surveys conducted
in  Russia,  Belarus,  and  Ukraine  to  show  that  voters  who  support  the  incumbent  are
more likely to endorse internet voting despite widespread concerns about its fairness
under authoritarian regimes. This is concerning because popular backing of an �instru-
ment  that  potentially  makes  fraud  easier  and  less  detectable�78 risks  obscuring  the
inherent vulnerabilities of digital voting and may contribute to normalizing a practice
fraught with risks. Without rigorous scrutiny of its impartiality, the unchecked expan-
sion of internet voting could undermine electoral integrity and ultimately strengthen
the legitimacy of authoritarian rule by masking fraud and manipulation. The contri-
bution  of  Burkhardt  and  Dollbaum79 is  another  example  of  the  shift  from  state  to
private  actors  and  from  organizations  to  individuals,  as  it  illustrates  how  individual
voters can contribute to the legitimization of authoritarian practices.

Conduit�s80 article illustrates the shift from state to private actors from a political
economy perspective. By looking into the case of Iran, Conduit highlights three mech-
anisms through which the longstanding economic context of the country enables and,
importantly, also constrains authoritarian informationalism, illustrating crucial conti-
nuities between how the regime traditionally tried to control the state�s economy and
how it also tries to use the economy to sustain its informational power in the digital
age.  First,  economic  tools  are  used  to  incentivize  private  actors  to  cooperate  with
the  regime  such  as  via  discounted  local  internet  data  and  industry  subsidies  for
firms  that  comply  with  state  demands.81 Second,  economic  tools  are  used  to  coerce
private  firms  and  the  population  into  using  technology  in  the  regime-prescribed
way.  For  example,  VPN  users  are  punished  and  using  foreign  internet  sites  is  more
costly.82 Third,  by  inviting  private  firms  into  the  digital  economy,  the  regime
becomes vulnerable to their interests, risking their disapproval. For example, the fre-
quent internet shutdowns Iran implemented in order to suppress dissidence inadver-
tently eroded the support from these private actors as it counteracted their interests.
Overall, this contribution highlights that while the primary goal of authoritarian infor-
mationalism  is  to  maintain  power,  the  shift  from  state  to  private  actors  providing
access  to  information  is  coming  with  new  vulnerabilities  which  also  constrain
(digital) authoritarian rule.

Garbe  et  al.83 investigate  the  conditions  under  which  authoritarian  states  accept
foreign  direct  investment  (FDI)  to  develop  their  digital  infrastructure.  They  find
that  while  authoritarian  regimes  are  generally  cautious  about  allowing  foreign
private  companies  to  operate  in  their  digital  space,  they  are  more  likely  to  accept
FDI from countries with similar political regimes when their rule relies on a substantial
amount of offline repression. They further illustrate that authoritarian regimes which
collaborate with foreign companies from other autocracies are more likely to expand
their  repression  online.  The  article  is  an  example  of  the  shift  from  state  to  private
actors, as it highlights the increasing importance of private actors in sustaining author-
itarian rule, and, crucially, how corporate actors from autocratic regimes can also help

tools  for  controlling  digital  information  spaces.  Such  legal  control  of  the  internet  is
justified  as  cybersecurity  or  privacy  protection,  yet  it  often  enables  punishment  of
online  expression  and  expanded  surveillance.  Drawing  on  original  data  on  internet
legislation  across  sub-Saharan  African  countries,  the  article  shows  that  governments
are  more  likely  to  enact  legislation  that  restricts  online  freedoms  preventively,
outside the spotlight of political events. Rather than domestic or regional unrest, the
regional prevalence of legal internet controls predicts the enactment of such restrictive
regulations.  When  governments  use  traditional  state  control  mechanisms,  especially
legal  instruments,  to  regulate  information  online,  regional  norms  appear  to  shape
the boundaries of permissible civil and political rights restrictions. This is particularly
important in the Global South, where many countries are still developing their frame-
works  for  internet  governance  and  are  looking  not  only  to  the  European  Union  but
increasingly to autocratic peers for guidance on policy matters.

The  article  by  Maerz87 looks  into  how  practices  of  digital  authoritarianism  are
creeping  into  democracies.  The  author  examines  how  democratic  governments  use
digital  technologies  to  surveil,  manipulate,  and  control  information  in  ways  that  go
beyond what is necessary for national security or public safety. The article finds that
democratic  governments  increasingly  adopt  practices  of  digital  authoritarianism,
such as official disinformation in social media, the abuse of defamation and copyright
laws, surveilling social media, and government access to personal data on the internet.
This  is  particularly  concerning  because,  as  Maerz88 finds,  it  undermines  core  demo-
cratic  institutions  of  democratic  accountability,  civil  liberties,  and  freedom  of
expression.

Conclusion

The  contributions  to  this  Special  Issue  illustrate  how  digital  technologies  are  trans-
forming  traditional  authoritarian  strategies  of  surveillance,  manipulation,  and
control.  The  articles  demonstrate  how  the  actor  landscape  is  diversifying,  with
private  firms  and  individual  regime  supporters  playing  increasingly  important  roles
in  sustaining  authoritarian  rule.  They  also  highlight  the  complex  interplay  between
offline  and  online  tactics  in  authoritarian  informationalism  and  show  how  these
tactics are becoming more preventive, targeted, and less visible while not necessarily

less  severe.  Furthermore,  the  contributions  illustrate  the  increasingly  transnational
nature  of  authoritarian  practices  and  how  they  cut  across  regime  types,  also  being
applied by democratically elected governments. Overall, the articles provide important
insights  into  the  nature  and  limits  of  authoritarian  informationalism  and  its  impli-
cations for understanding and responding to the challenges posed by authoritarianism
in the digital age.

Several  avenues  for  future  research  emerge  from  the  contributions  to  this  Special
Issue.  First,  while  most  of  the  contributions  focus  on  authoritarian  regimes,  there  is  a
need  to  further  investigate  the  creeping  of  authoritarian  practices  into  democracies.
This  includes  examining  how  democratic  governments  use  digital  technologies  to
surveil,  manipulate,  and  control  information  which  goes  beyond  what  this  Special
Issue has explored, as well as how these practices affect democratic institutions and pro-
cesses in the long run. Apart from that, there is a need to further explore the transna-
tional  dimension  of  digital  authoritarianism.  This
includes  examining  how
authoritarian regimes seek to influence governance elsewhere, how successful they are
in doing so, as well as how they cooperate with each other to share practices of author-
itarian informationalism and technologies. Last but not least, while the role of AI in auto-
mating and scaling these tactics is not directly addressed in the contributions presented
in this Special Issue, it is a pressing research agenda for the future. Understanding the
implications of AI for democracy is crucial for comprehending and responding to the
multifaceted challenges posed by authoritarian leaders in today�s interconnected world.
