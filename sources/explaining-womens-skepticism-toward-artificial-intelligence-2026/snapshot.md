Abstract
This article examines the gender gap in attitudes toward the adoption of AI in the workplace, with a focus on how gender differences in
risk orientation and risk exposure drive skepticism toward AI’s economic benefits. Using original survey data from ∼3,000 respondents
across Canada and the United States, we find that women consistently perceive AI to be riskier than men. We identify two key drivers
behind this gender gap: women’s higher general risk aversion and their greater exposure to AI-related risks. To establish a causal
relationship between risk and AI attitudes, we show experimentally that as the probability of net positive employment effects
decreases, women’s support for companies adopting AI falls more sharply than men’s. Finally, structural topic modeling of openended responses confirms that women express greater uncertainty about AI’s benefits and more frequently anticipate little to no
benefits. Given AI’s potential to exacerbate existing gender inequalities, our study highlights the critical importance of incorporating
women’s perspectives into AI policy-making. Policies that do not address gender-specific risks may not only reinforce existing
inequalities in employment and income but could also generate political backlash against AI adoption.
Keywords: artificial intelligence, gender gap, risk, public opinion

Significance Statement
AI technologies promise substantial economic benefits but also significant risks, including job losses and greater inequality. Not
everyone will benefit equally. Our study finds that women perceive AI as riskier than men. We show that these differences are partly
explained by women’s greater aversion to risk and higher exposure to AI employment risks. By experimentally manipulating the prob­
ability of net employment gains using a survey experiment, we further show that as the probability that AI adoption will create more
jobs than it destroys decreases, women’s support falls more steeply than men’s. These findings highlight the importance of addressing
gender-specific concerns in AI policies. Without such attention, AI may worsen existing gender inequalities, reduce women’s econom­
ic opportunities, and even lead to political backlash against the technology.

Introduction
AI technologies promise substantial benefits and significant
challenges for economic, social, and political systems. As AI
technologies become increasingly sophisticated, they offer op­
portunities for fostering innovation, improving problem-solving
and decision-making, and enhancing productivity. However,
their adoption also presents substantial risks, including poten­
tial job displacement, ethical considerations surrounding data
privacy and bias, and other concerns about autonomous
decision-making systems. Moreover, not all groups are equally
exposed to the risks and benefits of AI, and the technology has

the potential to exacerbate existing social and economic in­
equalities (1–5).
Women constitute one key group likely to experience distinct
distributional impacts from AI. Women are over-represented in
a range of administrative, clerical, and service jobs that may be
disrupted by AI technologies (6–8). Simultaneously, women’s
under-representation in science, technology, engineering, and
mathematics fields means they may have less access to highpaying AI-related jobs and leadership positions. As a result, ad­
vances in AI could widen existing gender pay gaps (9). Moreover,

Risk and the gender gap in attitudes
toward AI
Motivated in part by the consistent finding in the political econ­
omy literature on economic shocks that women are more opposed
to trade and other free-market policies than men (e.g. (31–36)), re­
cent studies have identified a significant gender gap in attitudes
toward the economic adoption of automation and AI (13, 37).a In
the most thorough examination of the gender gap to date,
Borwein et al. (13) examine a wide range of factors thought to ex­
plain attitudes toward economic shocks more broadly, as well as
factors specific to technological change. The former include dif­
ferences in general labor market insecurity, inequality attitudes,
ideological self-placement, and social status, while more
technology-specific factors include objective and subjective vul­
nerability to workplace automation, objective and subjective
knowledge of technology, and perceptions of technology’s societal
(sociotropic) threat (13). Despite the wide range of explanations
examined, the authors are only able to account for ∼40% of the ob­
served gender gap in attitudes toward automation and AI, with
only a few factors—in particular, women’s lower average levels
of technological knowledge as compared to men—discernibly re­
ducing the size of the gap (13).
In this study, we propose that differences in men’s and wom­
en’s risk orientation and exposure—factors largely unexamined
in research on gender and AI—may provide further leverage for
understanding the gender gap in attitudes toward AI’s adoption
in the workplace. Risk refers to the possibility for an action to
cause loss or harm, and is a central feature of decision-making
in contexts where different alternatives involve uncertain costs
and benefits (38, 39). We anticipate that perceptions of risk struc­
ture AI attitudes because the current landscape of AI is fraught
with uncertainty (40). On the one hand, economists generally per­
ceive technology to be a crucial driver of economic growth (41–43),
and there is considerable optimism that the more widespread
adoption of AI technologies could dramatically expand economic
output (2, 23). However, workers also face significant threats from
the adoption of technologies, which often produce substantial
economic disruption by complementing some workers’ skills,
while making others redundant (25, 26, 41). AI promises additional
potential benefits, such as the possibility of major innovations in
fields like healthcare and education, but also risks such as the dis­
semination of misinformation, environmental degradation, and
threats to privacy and security (1, 4).
In such a landscape, where gains and losses are anticipated but
uncertain, citizens’ attitudes will reflect, at least in part, how they

These distinct experiences may contribute to gender gaps in at­

change shows that women express greater skepticism than men
toward the economic adoption of automating technologies (13).
However, despite these earlier findings, existing research has
not fully explained the mechanisms driving gender differences
in attitudes toward technological change.
In this study, we focus on one previously overlooked factor:
risk. We argue that two components of risk—risk exposure and
risk orientation—make women more skeptical of AI’s benefits
than men. The first component, risk exposure, captures women’s
greater real and perceived exposure to economic displacement
and other outcomes of AI adoption. At the same time, research
also shows that how a person approaches risky situations depends
not only on factors in the external environment but also on their
general tolerance for making risky choices. This is the second
component of risk—what the literature refers to as risk orientation.
Women consistently exhibit lower risk orientation than men (14–
17 ), which may further reinforce women’s hesitance toward tech­
nology with uncertain consequences.
To examine how risk shapes men’s and women’s attitudes to­
ward AI, we draw on a survey of ∼3,000 respondents in the
United States and Canada, with an embedded preregistered sur­
vey experiment. To begin, we first provide evidence that gender
differences in perceptions of AI risks relate both to general risk tol­
erance (risk orientation) and exposure to workplace AI (risk expos­
ure). Next, we experimentally manipulate the probability of net
employment gains for a company adopting new generative AI
technology, showing that women’s support for workplace techno­
logical adoption is more contingent than men’s on employment
benefits being certain. We conclude with an exploratory analysis
of two open-ended questions asking respondents what they per­
ceive to be the biggest risks and benefits of automation and AI,
which further reinforces our findings that women express more
skepticism and uncertainty about AI’s economic benefits than men.
Our findings have important social and political implications.
The rapid advancement of AI technologies presents an empirical
reality that warrants examination. Recently, AI systems have im­
proved from solving 4.4% of coding problems in 2023 to 71.7% in
2024 (18), while workplace adoption among U.S. workers reached
46% in mid-2025 (19). However, these adoption rates show pro­
nounced gender disparities (20, 21), with women being approxi­
mately 20% less likely than men to use generative AI tools (21).
At the same time, labor market impacts remain heterogeneous
and uncertain. While some studies document productivity gains
for workers (22–24), effects vary substantially by exposure type,
with sectors facing “displacing exposure” experiencing employ­
ment and wage declines (25), and with potentially acute effects
for young workers (26). Women face higher exposure to AI across
both high-complementarity roles that could benefit from AI and
high-substitution roles at risk of displacement (27), though the longterm consequences of AI remain fundamentally uncertain (28).
Despite this uncertainty, gender differences in attitudes toward
AI matter for several reasons. First, if women’s lower support for AI
adoption translates into underuse of AI tools (20, 21), this will re­
duce women’s input in AI development. This matters not because
AI is inherently beneficial, but because these technologies are al­
ready being built and deployed, and exclusion from this process
means women’s concerns are less likely to be incorporated into
system design. Second, evidence already suggests differential
adoption is creating measurable disparities. Tang et al. (29) find

risk among women, as research shows that risks judged to be un­
known are approached with much greater caution (60).
Based on these insights, we hypothesize that women should
perceive AI to be riskier than men due to both their higher risk
aversion and their higher exposure to AI risk:
H1a: Women perceive AI to be riskier than men.
H1b: These perceptions are associated with women’s lower risk
orientation as compared to men.
H1c: These perceptions are associated with women’s higher risk
exposure as compared to men.
The uncertainty surrounding the outcomes of AI adoption—
particularly in terms of its potential benefits—amplifies the im­
portance of risk perceptions in shaping attitudes. When the prob­
ability of net employment gains from AI decreases, individuals
who are generally more risk-averse should become even more
cautious. Given that we expect women to have higher risk aver­
sion and to be more exposed to the potential risks of AI, we antici­
pate that their support for AI will decrease more sharply than
men’s when the benefits of AI are less certain.
H2: As the probability of net employment gains from AI adop­
tion decreases, women will reduce their support for AI to a greater
degree than men.

Data and methods
To test whether risk influences men’s and women’s attitudes to­
ward workplace AI, we fielded an online survey in November
2023, which included a survey experiment about AI. We surveyed
a total of 6,056 respondents from survey provider YouGov’s opt-in
panel. In the survey, half of our respondents answered questions
about generative AI, while half were randomly assigned to answer
questions about trade instead; in this article, we examine only the

confront risk in general (44). Studies delineating the role of risk in
political preferences and policy outcomes outline how two key as­
pects of risk shape people’s decision-making in contexts of uncer­
tainty. The first is a person’s pre-existing risk aversion,
understood as a general predisposition. The second is a person’s
risk exposure (or perceptions thereof), understood as the potential
costs or benefits (risks and returns) of a given choice for them (14,
15, 44). Following Ehrlich and Maestas (44), we refer to these ele­
ments of risk as risk orientation and risk exposure.
The first component of risk, risk orientation, refers to a person’s
broader tolerance for risk. A person’s risk orientation is often
understood to be a more or less stable personality trait, dictating
how they weigh gains and losses in the face of situational uncer­
tainty (45, 46).b Risk orientation is often defined in terms of a
person’s propensity toward sensation seeking; that is, a general­
ized preference for taking actions that provide novelty and excite­
ment (15, 46). Importantly, a person’s risk orientation is not
issue-specific; although it may vary somewhat by domain, it is
thought to shape their decision-making above and beyond the de­
gree of risk associated with a given decision (44, 45).
At the same time, the level of risk associated with a specific be­
havior (such as adopting technology) is also consequential for how
risk shapes decision-making, and is captured by the second com­
ponent of risk—risk exposure. While an individual’s baseline toler­
ance for risk conditions their attitudes toward uncertain policy
outcomes, so do their anticipated benefits relative to losses for a
given policy (15, 16, 44). Examining trade policy, Ehrlich and
Maestas (44) show that even when comparing workers with simi­
lar levels of general risk aversion, those who face probable losses
from trade (low-skilled workers) are significantly less favorable to­
ward trade than those who face probable gains (high-skilled work­
ers). Put differently, the latter group, whether or not they are
intolerant of risk, perceives greater personal benefits from trade
and has less reason to oppose it. The former group perceives
much greater losses, and these perceptions are compounded by
their pre-existing risk intolerance.
In the context of AI, there is reason to anticipate that women
will be both more risk-averse and more exposed to risk than
men. Studies examining differences in risk-taking consistently
find higher levels of risk aversion among women than men (15,
47–50). This does not mean that risk aversion is an inherent gen­
der trait; rather, women’s lower tolerance for risk is thought to im­
portantly emerge from social norms and social learning (17).
Among other factors, women’s greater perceived vulnerability to
physical violence is thought to sensitize them to threats in general
and heighten their sensitivity to risk in society (51, 52).
More broadly, social role theory posits that men and women are
socialized into distinct roles that shape their behaviors and atti­
tudes, which may also help explain gender differences in risk
aversion in general, and in the context of AI attitudes specifically.
Within the framework of social role theory, traditional gender
roles prescribe that men should be more agentic, assertive, and
open to risk-taking, whereas women are encouraged to be com­
munal, cautious, and attuned to potential harm (53–55). Such so­
cially constructed stereotypes and expectations may yield
divergent responses to AI, with women expressing higher per­
ceived risk and more cautious attitudes toward adoption, while
men may exhibit a higher tolerance for technological uncertainty.
Consequently, the gender gap in AI risk orientation is not purely
individual; it is also deeply embedded in broader societal norms
and the enactment of traditional gender roles.

read about a company making a decision to adopt generative AI
tools, and are then randomly assigned to read about different
probabilities that doing so will have a net positive effect on the
company’s total workforce. Probabilities vary from completely
certain (100%), medium-high certainty (70%), a coin toss (50%),
to high risk (30%). Our experiment stressed both the potential ben­
efits and risks of adoption, stating both the probability that the net
total effect on employment will be positive, and the associated
probability that it will be negative. We then ask respondents if
they support the company’s decision to adopt generative AI. To
the extent that women have heightened risk orientation and risk
exposure regarding AI, we anticipate they will be more receptive
to information about the potential downsides of AI technology.
Put differently, we expect that as net positive economic outcomes
become less certain, the gap between men’s and women’s atti­
tudes toward AI adoption will increase. See Supplementary
material for further details on the survey experiment.
In our final set of analyses, we explore how men and women
think about the benefits and risks of AI. For this purpose, we draw
on two open-ended survey questions that asked respondents, first,
what they thought are the biggest benefits of automation and AI,
and second, what they thought are the biggest risks. We analyze
the open-ended responses using the semi-automated structural
topic modeling (STM) approach developed by Roberts et al. (67).
STM allows us to identify common themes in responses and exam­
ine their association with covariates of interest. We include a cova­
riate for gender to determine whether men and women devote
different degrees of attention to different topics. With STM, the re­
searcher determines the appropriate number of distinct latent
topics, typically by jointly weighing model diagnostics and inter­
pretability concerns. We select models with 15 topics, which pro­
vides optimal trade-offs between higher semantic coherence,
higher exclusion, and lower residuals. See Figs. S4 and S5 for further

A common approach to measuring individual risk orientation
in surveys is to present participants with a series of lottery choices
(62 , 63). These choices offer varying levels of risk and reward, al­
lowing individuals to reveal their attitudes toward risk based on
their selections. Using a similar approach, we measure men’s
and women’s risk orientation by asking respondents whether
they would prefer a guaranteed win of $1,000, or a 50% probability
of winning $2,000 (64). The assumption is that risk-averse individ­
uals will prefer the guaranteed $1,000 over the risky lottery. In
contrast, risk seekers, who are drawn to uncertainty, will be will­
ing to accept higher risk for the possibility of a greater reward, fa­
voring the lottery over the certain $1,000.
Measuring risk exposure related to AI is somewhat more diffi­
cult. Because there is substantial uncertainty around generative
AI’s implications for the workplace, only more experimental esti­
mates of exposure to generative AI exist. In the absence of certain
measures of exposure to AI dislocation, our in-text analysis uses
post-secondary education as a broad proxy that has previously
been used to capture how well-positioned workers are to benefit
from technological change. Although we recognize that there are
important differences between earlier technological change and
recent developments in AI, most notably because of AI’s greater
capacity to engage in cognitive work, we maintain that education
remains one of the best available proxies for being well-positioned
to benefit from AI and respond constructively to dislocation.
Emerging research suggests that, while workers with at least a col­
lege education are more exposed to AI technologies than workers
without a college education because their work involves a higher
share of cognitive tasks (5, 8, 65), AI is also most likely to be comple­
mentary to workers in this group (8). Put differently, high exposure
does not necessarily mean high displacement for more educated
workers, who will likely experience productivity gains from the
greater integration of AI into their work, particularly if they pos­
sess the skills to leverage the technology (8).

ly similar patterns across our alternative measures of automation
and AI exposure. Across measures, women who are risk-averse
perceive higher levels of risk associated with AI adoption than
risk-averse men. However, as compared to women, risk-averse
men are much more sensitive to their levels of risk exposure, per­
ceiving higher levels of risk associated with AI when they are not
in a position to benefit—that is, when they face high exposure on
the task-based automation measure, low AIOE (i.e. they are em­
ployed in occupations that are likely to neither benefit nor lose
from AI), and low complementarity to AI.
Taken together, these findings suggest that women perceive
higher risks as compared to benefits associated with AI, and
they provide tentative evidence that these perceptions are partly
—though not fully—attributable to their latent risk orientation
and their exposure to risk.

from AI adoption ranges from a sure thing (100%) to a long shot

Textual analysis of open-ended questions on
risks and benefits
Finally, to gain insight into how men and women think about the
risks and benefits of AI technology, we conclude with analyses of
two open-ended questions in our survey: one that asks respond­
ents what they perceive to be the biggest benefits of automation
and AI, and one that asks them what they perceive to be the big­
gest risks. For both analyses, we employ structural topic models
(STM) with 15 distinct topics. In Fig. S11, we show all 15 topics re­
lating to the benefits of automation and AI, in decreasing order of
prevalence, as well as the stemmed words with the highest prob­
ability of belonging to each topic. Figure S12 shows the same ana­
lysis for all topics relating to risks.
On the question of the benefits of automation and AI, our analysis
reveals considerable uncertainty. The most prevalent topic (which
we label “don’t know”) captures this explicitly. Subsequent topics
also reveal that many respondents view AI as having a range of po­
tential benefits for both work and life, for example by increasing
workplace efficiency and productivity (“efficiency and cost sav­
ings”), making daily work and life tasks easier (“better problemsolving,” and “make life easier”), and generating scientific and
related advances (“science and medical advances”). However,
even when asked specifically about the benefits of automation
and AI, people also express considerable concern about the

downsides of the technology, with varying levels of confidence
(“not sure/no benefits,” and “no benefits”).
While Fig. S11 illustrates overall topic prevalence, the left panel
of Fig. 5 shows that some topics are more prevalent among either
women or men. In particular, topics expressing uncertainty about
the benefits of automation and AI (e.g. “don’t know”) and skepti­
cism or absence of benefits (“not sure/no benefits,” and “no bene­
fits”) are significantly more prevalent in women’s responses than
in men’s responses. Examples of representative responses for
these topics (i.e. responses with a high relative probability of fea­
turing in a given topic relative to other responses in the topic)h in­
clude statements made by women such as “I don’t know enough
about either [AI or automation] to speculate,” or “[I] don’t think
there is any real benefit….”
In contrast, the left side of Fig. 5 indicates that topics emphasiz­
ing the economic benefits of automation and AI are more preva­
lent in men’s responses than in women’s responses. Among
potential benefits, topics emphasizing increased productivity
and efficiency (“efficiency and cost savings,” and “productivity
and efficiency”), medical and scientific advances (“science and
medical advances”), time savings through the automation of
everyday tasks (“make life easier”), and broader improvements
to economic processes (“streamline processes,” “speed-up proc­
esses,” and “various benefits,” “automate mundane tasks”) are
more prevalent in men’s responses.
On the second question, pertaining to the risks of automation
and AI, several key themes emerge (see Fig. S12). First, many re­
spondents express concern about the potential risk of automation
for work and employment (“mass job loss,” “economic disparities,”
“loss of skills/jobs,” and “takes jobs”), and for humanity more
broadly (“loss of human interaction,” “humans lose control,” “AI
becomes sentient”). Second, as was the case for AI’s benefits, re­
spondents report substantial uncertainty about the technology’s
risks (“don’t know”). Respondents further express concern about
the potential use of automation and AI for unethical, malicious,
or illegal purposes (“privacy and data bias,” “mis/disinformation,”
and “nefarious/illegal uses”).

prevalence varies by gender. It shows, again, that topics express­
ing uncertainty about the risks of automation and AI (“don’t
know”) are more prevalent among women than men. As com­

Overall, we highlight two key patterns from our textual analysis.
First, compared to men, women’s responses devote a larger share to
topics expressing uncertainty about the benefits and risks of AI, as
well as to topics suggesting that AI is unlikely to generate significant
benefits. While we acknowledge that women’s greater proclivity to
express uncertainty about AI’s benefits and risks may in part reflect
that women are more likely than men to answer “don’t know” to
questions for which they are unsure (69), there are several reasons
why we also think we are capturing true uncertainty about AI.
First, we are examining open-ended questions, which makes guess­
ing on questions respondents know nothing about more difficult.
More significantly, as we report in Fig. S14, when we asked respond­
ents several factual questions about AI, men demonstrated higher
objective knowledge of AI than women. Thus, we contend that wom­
en’s greater reported uncertainty about the risks and benefits of AI
reflects, at least in part, true uncertainty. Given findings in the litera­
ture that low knowledge of a risk increases perceptions of its riski­
ness (60), we contend that women’s greater uncertainty about AI’s
risks and benefits is significant for understanding the gender gap
in risk attitudes toward the technology.

sociated with AI than women, they also perceive many more ben­
efits. We quantify this pattern in Fig. S13, which shows the
difference in the average number of words written by women rela­

more substantive words than women about the benefits of auto­
mation and AI (as measured after text preprocessing), while the
difference in words written about risks between men and women
is not significant. Because aggregate risk judgments depend on the
balance of perceived costs and benefits, this is likely to lead men to
view AI more positively in general (44, 60). This pattern likely also
reflects that men are more familiar with the technology, which
also reduces risk perceptions (60).

Discussion
Rapid developments in large language models, generative AI, and
related AI technologies present challenges for governments in bal­
ancing the benefits of these innovations with the need to regulate
their potential risks. Not all groups in society share the same opti­
mism about these technologies’ advantages, nor are they equally
well positioned to benefit from their upsides or protect themselves
from potential downsides. As governments develop regulatory
frameworks, they will be confronted with these different groups’
perceptions of these technologies.
In this article, we examined men and women as two groups
for whom the impacts of AI, and associated perceptions, differ
significantly. Beginning with observational analyses, we con­
firmed that women perceive AI to be riskier than men; this percep­
tion was particularly pronounced among women with higher
levels of risk aversion and greater exposure to the economic risks
posed by AI. Our experimental findings further reinforced these ob­
servations: when the probability of net positive employment effects
decreased, women reduced their support for AI adoption significant­
ly more than men. We also explored how men and women perceive
the risks and benefits of automation and AI through open-ended sur­
vey responses. Our text analysis revealed that women express more
uncertainty about AI’s benefits and more often raise concerns about
the risk of large-scale job loss.

Differences in risk perceptions among men and women matter
not only for their attitudes toward companies’ use of AI, they also
shape policy preferences. In Fig. S15, we show that women are
more supportive of government intervention to slow down AI
adoption, particularly when job gains from AI are less certain. In
contrast, men’s support for government intervention remained
relatively stable across different levels of risk. This is significant
because historically, similar disruptive economic shifts, such as
globalization, have influenced political attitudes, often leading
to increased calls for regulatory measures to manage perceived
risks and distribute benefits. Research on the politics of trade
shows that women, particularly those in office, can shift govern­
ment policy in a more protectionist direction when economic risks
are high (68). As AI becomes an increasingly salient political issue,
these gender-based differences in AI risk perception and policy
preferences may influence both the pace and nature of AI
regulation.
This study’s findings have several additional implications.
First, they highlight the need to consider gender differences in
risk perceptions when designing and implementing AI-related
policies. As women are more likely to perceive AI as risky and
are more supportive of regulatory measures, policymakers should
ensure that these concerns are addressed in policy discussions.
This could involve implementing policies that mitigate the risks
associated with AI, such as stronger protections against job dis­
placement, compensatory schemes, and measures to reduce gen­
der bias in AI systems.
Importantly, our research suggests that the adoption of AI could
exacerbate existing gender inequalities if women remain more
skeptical and less supportive than men. These emerging patterns
of differential adoption of AI technologies may create new forms
of occupational segregation that will persist regardless of whether
AI ultimately delivers its promised benefits or creates unforeseen
consequences. As AI technologies continue to reshape the work­
place, it is crucial to ensure that both men and women are able to
benefit equally from and engage with these advancements. This
might involve targeted education and training programs to increase
women’s participation in AI-related fields, as well as efforts to pro­
mote gender diversity in technology leadership positions, or other
policies that help mitigate the economic dislocations that rapid
adoption of AI technologies may produce in coming years.
