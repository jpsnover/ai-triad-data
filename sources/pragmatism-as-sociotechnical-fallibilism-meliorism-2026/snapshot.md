Abstract
Watson, M÷kander, and Floridi (Watson etáal., AI Soc, 2024) propose that contemporary discussions in AI ethics revolve
around two competing narratives, which they term sociotechnical dogmatism and sociotechnical skepticism. As an alterna-
tive to this dichotomy, they introduce sociotechnical pragmatism, which they describe as a constructive synthesis of these
opposing views. However, the distinction they draw between these narratives conflates their epistemological and ethical
dimensions, making it difficult to classify possible alternative positions. As a result, sociotechnical pragmatism, as formulated
by Watson etáal., does not fully account for the potential of pragmatism as an approach to inquiry, regulation, and governance
of technology. Rather than treating regulation as a reactive measure, pragmatism seeks to integrate inquiry into the devel-
opment process itself, ensuring that both the consequences of new technologies and their ethical implications are critically
examined in advance. In this manner, pragmatism as sociotechnical fallibilism and meliorism provides an alternative to a
large-scale societal experimentation with technology which treats harm as an inevitable cost of progress.

1  Introduction

Debates in the ethics of technology have been shaped by
diverging portrayals of technological development, with
some advocating for rapid progress as a force for societal
good and others emphasizing the risks, unintended conse-
quences, and ethical challenges that emerging technologies
present. David S. Watson, Jakob M÷kander, and Luciano
Floridi (2024) propose that contemporary discussions in
AI ethics are structured around two competing narratives,
which they term sociotechnical dogmatism and sociotechni-
cal skepticism. With a broad audience in mindùincluding
AI developers, the public, and policymakersùthe narratives
do not engage directly with specific issues that have been
central to academic discussions in AI ethics, such as bias
and discrimination, privacy and surveillance, or autonomy
and human agency. Rather, they are presented as broad per-
spectives on ongoing sociotechnical developments, includ-
ing  the  continued  advancement  and  deployment  of  AI,
and their wider ethical, social and societal consequences.
The first narrative focuses on technological progress and

2   Three narratives ofásociotechnical

development

Watson etáal. distinguish between two competing narratives
in AI ethics, emerging in response to rapid advances in
machine learning and the growing adoption of AI systems.
Although their account is presented as a review of AI ethics
discourse, it is less concerned with particular ethical chal-
lenges or approaches and more oriented toward overarching
narratives of sociotechnical change. While they acknowl-
edge key concerns in the AI ethics literatureùsuch as bias,
privacy, and fears surrounding artificial general intelligence
(AGI)ùas well as the range of normative ethical perspec-
tives adopted by researchers, they focus on overarching soci-
otechnical trajectories articulated in a wider body of litera-
ture, including policy documents, journalistic accounts, and
public statements by political and corporate actors. Within
this framing, they identify two dominant narratives, the first
of which they term sociotechnical dogmatism, which centers
on the technological progress and potential of AI systems
to generate positive social and societal outcomes. This nar-
rative underscores the benefits of continued development
and deployment of AI, arguing that these technologies can
drive innovation and solve complex societal problems. The

competing narrative, sociotechnical skepticism, focuses on
the harms that technologies have caused (and may continue
to cause) to individuals, groups, and the environment; it
calls for increased regulation and oversight of AI systems
to address their potential harms. This narrative takes a criti-
cal stance on new technologies and questions whether AI
can truly provide solutions to human problems. These two
positions are not tied to specific individuals or groups but are
instead presented as ideal types in contemporary discourse
on AI ethics. However, they connect sociotechnical dogma-
tism with proponents in Silicon Valley entrepreneurs, busi-
ness executives, and the policymakers and researchers who
advocate for the rapid advancement of AI technologies, and
associate sociotechnical skepticism with critical research on
technology, including datafication, algorithms, and AI.

Watson etáal. discuss topics in AI ethics where these
two narratives lead to contrasting conclusions, in particular
issues of fairness and justice as well as transparency and
explainability in the context of these technologies and their
application. They argue that both narratives are not so much
wrong as incomplete. While sociotechnical dogmatism is
blind to the unjust consequences and means of technological
progress, the narrative of sociotechnical skepticism leads
to overly broad opposition to technological advances that
could contribute to improvement and progress. Their alter-
native, which they call sociotechnical pragmatism, proposes
a ôconstructive synthesisö of the dogmatist and skeptical
narratives (Watson etáal. 2024, 6). Drawing on the philo-
sophical tradition of pragmatism, this alternative promotes
research, policy programs, practical solutions, and demo-
cratic deliberation. In their characterization, pragmatism
maintains that theories should be judged by their success
in real-world situations, and emphasizes the importance of
the reciprocal relationship which exists between social and
technical systems (including the history of their develop-
ment); they acknowledge that no single model can provide
conclusive guidance for how to evaluate particular technolo-
gies or design policies.

The salience of two alternative narratives surrounding
technological developments is difficult to deny. Indeed, it
may be that their emergence is not connected with the dawn
of the Industrial Revolution, as Watson etáal. suggest, but
traced far beyond the modern era. Although no specific
group or individual fully exemplifies either narrative, con-
flicting visions over technology and its ôadvancesö may have
been present in some shape or form as long as technologyù
or humanityùhas existed; already PlatoÆs Socrates recounts
a ôtale of the ancientsö regarding the Egyptian king Tha-
mus's criticism of the invention of writing as detrimental
to memory (Phaedrus 274cû275c). In recent years, critical
accounts have risen particularly in connection to algorithmic
technologies, datafication, data collection, and AI, and their
potential implications for human lifeùincluding concerns

However,  subsuming,  under  two  grand  narratives,  a
variety of critical and ôskepticalö perspectives as well as
more relaxed and optimistic attitudes toward technology,
comes at a cost. This is already evident in the terminology
employed by Watson etáal. While they argue that their terms

classical issues in ethics and epistemology, this choice easily

when considering their characterization of these narratives
through four theses they adapt from Danaher (2022):

1.  ôThe fact premise, which states that it is possible to

2.  The value premise, which states that there are value cri-

3.  The evaluation premise, which states that the evaluation

4.  The technological premise, which states that technology

Their motivation for using the labels of sociotechnical
dogmatism and skepticism for the alternative narratives is
partly based on the fact that they exhibit different stances
toward these propositions. While sociotechnical dogma-
tism accepts these four premises unconditionally, in Wat-
son etáal.Æs characterization, sociotechnical skepticism, in
its various forms, denies the truth of some or all of them.

However, sociotechnical skepticism as discussed by Wat-
son etáal. is associated with the view that the consequences
of adopting new technologies are detrimental, as well as
related  calls  for  increased  regulation  over  their  use  and
development. Such views objecting primarily to the third
and fourth premises are, of course, ample: there is no short-
age of ôcriticalö accounts focused on the problems incurred
by new technologies, having in recent decades been directed
at radio, television, mass media, and now algorithmic tech-
nologies and AI. Epistemologically speaking, however, these
are not skeptical views. Instead, they may amount to a full-
blown denial of the value of technological developments and
the availability of new technologies; in some cases, these can
be stances just as ôdogmaticö as (in Watson etáal.Æs terms) as
the sociotechnical dogmatism that they oppose.

Moreover, Watson etáal. situate approaches such as the
sociology of scientific knowledgeùwhich they characterize

as inspired by thinkers such as Michel Foucault and Bruno
Latourùunder the same skeptical banner. However, such
lines of inquiry seldom take an explicit stance on the four
premises delineated above. Rather, they tend to focus on the
question of how particular developments, such as scientific
theories, views of central social problems to be addressed by
society, visions, and imaginaries concerning technological
ôsolutionsö, and so on, have come to exist and dominate, for
example, following a Foucauldian genealogical approach.
Such  accounts  are  not  literally  skepticalùgenealogical
accounts, say, must stand on some ôfactsö. Instead, they are
largely disinterested with respect to producing the kind of
evaluative views concerning technologies that the four the-
ses could circumscribe.

Indeed, the epistemological position commonly called
skepticism is what Watson etáal. refer to as ôstrong skepti-
cismö, which challenges the first and second theses: skeptics
maintain that we do not have access to the (relevant) facts
or values. However, genuine skepticism results in uncer-
tainty over the possibility of reaching any solid grounds for
assessing the third and fourth theses. As Watson etáal. them-
selves note, views of this kind leave little normative ground
for policy interventions. Including such approaches within
the same narrative that is connected with the demand for
increased regulation results in a convoluted account of the
alternatives actually at hand.

Accordingly, we can arrive at a more refined account
of the narratives in question, as well as of the potential of
the pragmatism alternative, by drawing a clearer distinc-
tion between the first and second thesesùviews on which
mark the divide between different types of dogmatism and
skepticismùand the third and fourth theses, which represent
another kind of divide. Indeed, this will be taken up in the
following section by distinguishing two central tenets devel-
oped within the pragmatist philosophical tradition.

As  the  subsequent  discussion  hopes  to  demonstrate,
this is not a mere exercise in conceptual clarity. It helps to
understand the alternative pragmatism may offer in relation
to the narratives Watson etáal. distinguish and the different
theses that could underlie such alternatives. While Watson
etáal. describe sociotechnical pragmatism as an intermediary
between a clearly affirmative and clearly negative response
to the third and fourth theses, their view regarding how
pragmatism relates to the first and second premises is more
ambiguous. In connection with the first thesis, they argue
that pragmatists accept that ôfacts are not subjective but
relationalö (Watson etáal. 2024, 14) and that pragmatism
ôreplaces objectivity with intersubjectivityö (ibid., 15). For
Watson etáal., pragmatism suggests that knowledge is always
contextual and qualified in this sense. The second, value-
based premise receives less attention. They suggest that
ôwhat counts as morally acceptable is a revisable cultural
inheritanceö (ibid., 7) and that pragmatism does not expect

3   Pragmatism ináscience andáethics

The two concepts left unused are fallibilism and meliorism.
To fully appreciate their relevance to both the pragmatist tra-
dition and its potential as an alternative approach in AI eth-
ics and to technology in general, however, some background
is required. Pragmatism, typically seen as originating with
the work of the American thinkers Charles S. Peirce, Wil-
liam James, and John Dewey, provides an understanding of
scientific inquiry and its connections with ethical, societal,
and technological issues that has gradually become more
pervasive in philosophical debates. Around the turn of the
twenty-first century, pragmatism experienced a resurgence
across numerous debates in philosophy; in part this was
because it remained unencumbered by many assumptions
of early twentieth-century positivism, including reduction-
ism about meaning and normativityùassumptions that were
gradually overcome by later twentieth-century philosophy
(Rydenfelt 2022a). Often viewed as mediating between phil-
osophical extremes, pragmatism has more recently regained
new ground and attention in various areas of inquiry, includ-
ing ethical and societal issues.

The origins of pragmatism are commonly traced to Pei-
rceÆs maxim of pragmatism, a principled test for the meaning
(and meaningfulness) of theoretical judgments. In its early
formulation (though without the pragmatist label), Peirce
(1878) presented a maxim for the clarification of the mean-
ing of terms and sentences. To clarify the meanings of theo-
retical notions, we are to consider their conceivable effects
in conduct. Later, Peirce (1903) formulated his pragmatism
as the thesis that the meaning of any theoretical judgment
expressed in the indicative mood can be traced in terms of
the practical maxims that the acceptance of that sentence
enforces. Advocating for holism concerning meaning, Peirce
and the other pragmatists maintained that the practical con-
sequences of particular beliefs or judgements must be exam-
ined within the wider context of our other commitments.

The pragmatists approached questions of epistemology
and philosophy of science primarily through the central
concept of inquiry. They rejected both Cartesian skepticism
and the ensuing search for indubitable premises or a priori
foundations for science and knowledge, the pursuit Dewey
(1929) aptly called the ôquest for certaintyö. Instead, prag-
matists argued that our inquiries must begin with what we

do not presently doubt while recognizing that any of our
commitments may ultimately be subjected to doubt and criti-
cal scrutiny. Empirical inquiry is characterized by fallibi-
lismùone concept among many coined by Peirceùwhich
maintains that any of our views may be mistaken (Rydenfelt
2013; 2021a; 2023b).1

Contrary to long-standing misconceptions and misrepre-
sentations, pragmatism does not entail relativism or subjec-
tivism, a dismissal of truth or knowledge, a disregard for the-
oretical research, or a crude instrumentalism that conflates or
supplants truth or goodness with efficiency or ôpragmaticö
cash value. Peirce, James, and Dewey all argued that truth
is agreement with reality, each providing a slightly differing
account of how such an agreement is to be understood within
the practical terms of empirical and experimental inquiry.
For example, contending that truth, as the ôordinary episte-
mologistö would maintain, is agreement with reality, James
argued that the pragmatist ôinsists on being more concrete
and asks what such æagreementÆ may mean in detailö, and
suggested that true ideas ômust point to or lead toward that
reality and no other, and then the pointings and leadings
must yield satisfaction as their resultö (James 1909, 191).
These vague formulations were not intended to imply that
truth is simply ôwhat worksö as opposed to ôcorrespondence
to realityö; indeed, the idea that there is a separate pragma-
tist ôtheoryö of truth is largely Bertrand RussellÆs concoction
(Russell 1910). Instead, realizing that the practical markers
of truth, such as predictive success, vary significantly across
different fields of inquiry, James and Dewey deliberately
framed their ideas in flexible terms.

Resisting  reductionism  and  a  narrow  conception  of
empirical inquiry, the pragmatists advocated for an empiri-
cal approach to normative questions, resisting both an a pri-
ori approach to ethics and the reduction of normativity to
descriptive issues in the social sciences, psychology, or simi-
lar fields. They argued that although normative and descrip-
tive inquiries address distinct problems and questions, both
are  amenable  to  a  single  scientific  methodology.  Peirce
(1903) proposed examining normative issues within a triad
of normative sciences: logic, ethics, and esthetics. Dewey,
particularly in his writings of the 1920s and 1930s on eth-
ics, logic, epistemology, and the philosophy of science, pro-
vided the most comprehensive exposition of an empirical
and experimental approach to ethics. He argued that values
can be revised, and ethical norms subjected to empirical con-
firmation based on their observable consequences. In keep-
ing with general scientific inquiry, such empirical inquiry

Dewey extended this view to social philosophy in terms of
his conception of democracy. He was particularly concerned
with the development of social policy in an era where social
management  and  administration  often  pursued  unexam-
ined, predetermined goals, while the social sciences tended
to eschew evaluations, leading to purely descriptive results
(Dewey 1938, 487û489). Dewey advocated for transforming
the change and revision of social and societal practices into
more deliberate modes of social inquiry, conducted within
and through a community. Such inquiry involves systematic
empirical investigation into policies and practices, engaging
the public as a source of information and data on problems,
potential solutions, and the observed consequences of new
policies (Dewey 1927; Rydenfelt 2023a;  2024). DeweyÆs
vision has often been aligned with deliberative theories of
democracyùviz., identifying democratic processes with
informed, reasoned discussion and debate to solve shared
problems (e.g., Bohman 2010). However, this alignment
risks obscuring the experimental nature of DeweyÆs con-
ception of social inquiry and democracy (Rydenfelt 2019).
DeweyÆs vision contrasts with deliberative approaches that
rest on just discussion and debate. Dewey (1935) maintained
that just as scientific discovery relies on experimental obser-
vation guided by hypotheses rather than discussion alone,
democratic societies should adopt the scientific method to
address social issuesùincluding normative onesùthrough
empirical and experimental inquiry.

The conceptions of truth, inquiry, and reality proposed by
the classic thinkers of the pragmatist tradition have engen-
dered both realist and constructivist (and idealist) readings.
On the one hand, Peirce, James, and Dewey advanced the
view that reality is, in some sense, independent of our opin-
ions of it. In PeirceÆs account of the scientific method of
inquiry, that method is aimed at uncovering a reality that
exists independently of our beliefs or perceptions (Rydenfelt
2021a). Nevertheless, the pragmatists also proposed various
ways in which such a reality is malleable to human thought
and understanding. Early in his career, Peirce (1871) sug-
gested that while reality is independent of any particular
thought, it is not independent of thought in general, posi-
tioning him close to what is often called objective ideal-
ism (see Lane 2017, chapterá3). Dewey, in turn, has often
been read as a proponent of constructivism, which maintains
that reality is, in some way, dependent on our interactions
with it (Godfrey-Smith 2016). A central example of this
constructivist dimension is DeweyÆs account of the ôtrans-
actionsö between the ôknowerö and the ôknownö, where
both are transformed (Dewey & Bentley 1949a, b). Impor-
tantly, however, regardless of how we choose to interpret
the pragmatists on this scoreùhighlighting either realist
or constructivist elementsùthe same interpretation applies

across all domains of inquiry. For the pragmatists, there is
no categorical divide separating the subject matters studied
by ethicists and, say, physical scientists: it is not suggested
that the results of physics lend themselves more readily to
a realist interpretation, while those of ethical inquiry allow
for a more constructivist rendering.

The notion of pragmatism as a mediating position in
ontology and epistemology is a recurring theme in JamesÆs
1906 lectures published as Pragmatism (James 1907), where
James presented pragmatism as an alternative to two types
of intellectual temperaments: the tender-minded, charac-
terized by rationalism, idealism, optimism, and religiosity,
and the tough-minded, defined by empiricism, materialism,
pessimism, and irreligiosity. While tough-minded thinkers
are more attuned to the actualities of human life and experi-
ence, their worldview offers little room for the ideals and the
consolidation of religious notions. On the other hand, the
tender-minded, turning to idealist or religious metaphysics,
are disconnected from the realities of experience. James pro-
posed ôthe oddly-named thing pragmatism as a philosophy
that can satisfy both kinds of demandö, as it is ôreligious like
the rationalismsö while retaining ôthe richest intimacy with
factsö typical of empiricism (James 1907, 13). While James
attempts to strike a balance between the ontological and
epistemological commitments of the two intellectual tem-
peraments, the significance of the resulting mediating prag-
matist position is ethical. Despite their apparent differences,
both tough-mindedness and tender-mindedness, driven by
their respective pessimism and optimism, lead to a similar
outcome: inaction with respect to the actualization of ideals.
In contrast, pragmatism charts a middle path of meliorism
thatùagain put in religious termsùôtreats salvation as nei-
ther inevitable nor impossibleö, but rather ôas a possibility,
which becomes more and more of a probability the more
numerous the actual conditions of salvation becomeö (James
1907, 110).2 Despite the religious language employed, James
is clear that meliorism is not necessarily religious in nature;
instead, it remains open to the ideals which ôany one of us
in this roomö cherishes and ôis willing to live and work forö
(James 1907, 110). These ideals, meliorism maintains, are
not mere abstractions but live possibilities that can be put
into practice, achievable through sustained effort to reach
the conditions for their actualization.

4   The interplay ofáfallibilism andámeliorism

The notions of fallibilism and meliorism allow to set up
distinctions that clarify pragmatismÆs position with respect
to the two narratives Watson etáal. distinguish in terms of
the four premises derived from Danaher. Regarding the first
and second premisesùthe fact premise and value premiseù
pragmatism offers a fallibilist epistemological stance that
avoids the extremes of dogmatism and skepticism. It sug-
gests that there are no principled barriers to learning the
ways the world is, while also recognizing that there are no
indubitable beliefs that would act as a permanent founda-
tion for science. Inquiry proceeds through continual revision
and correction, grounded in experience and experimentation.
With respect to the third and fourth premisesùthe evalua-
tion premise and technological premiseùpragmatism pro-
poses another mediating position, meliorism. While neither
technological nor societal progress is inevitable, the human
condition is susceptible to active improvement. The alter-
natives here are sociotechnical optimism and pessimism.
Sociotechnical optimism accepts the latter two premises;
sociotechnical pessimism denies one or both of them, con-
testing either ôprogressö or, at least, its connection to tech-
nological developments.

Moreoverùbeyond an exercise in conceptual clarifica-
tionùthese distinctions help illuminate potential issues and
areas for improvement within the proposal for sociotechni-
cal pragmatism. Watson etáal. allude to pragmatism having
epistemological and ethical dimensions; both are certainly

present in their discussion. However, Watson etáal. primarily
rely on either considerations motivated by either fallibilist
or meliorist dimensions of pragmatism in their discussion,
rather than fully engaging with the interplay of the two the-
ses as pragmatism would propose. Maintaining a distinction
between the two dimensions allows us to see how they must
interact for pragmatism to provide a genuine alternative to
sociotechnical dogmatism and skepticism, as well as socio-
technical optimism and pessimism.

In parts of Watson etáal.Æs discussion, epistemological
considerations take center stage. This is most evident in
their treatment of the debate surrounding the potential of
explainable AI (XAI) to help humans understand and review
the opaque behavior of complex statistical models, such as
deep neural networks. Watson etáal. argue that the dogmatist
believes new and improved methods will eventually resolve
issues of transparency. In contrast, they connect the skeptical
narrative to the suspicion that XAI might offer misleading
explanations, creating a false sense of understanding that
could lead to faulty decisions and harmful consequences.
As a third alternative, they propose a ôpragmatic approachö
to XAI that begins with an agent in context, acknowledging
that XAI tools can inform decision-making under the right
conditions (Watson etáal. 2024, 14).

Watson etáal. claim that the distinction between (what
they call) dogmatism and skepticism ôreflects the longstand-
ing tension between realism and constructivism in episte-
mologyö (Watson etáal. 2024, 3). They associate sociotechni-
cal dogmatism with a belief in the objectivity and singularity
of truth, and attribute to sociotechnical skepticism a con-
trasting commitment to epistemological constructivism (or
subjectivism), which holds that ôtrue explanationsö ôcannot
exist and would be unknowable if they didö (Watson etáal.
2024, 14). However, framing the distinction between the two
narratives in these terms is problematic. It aligns realism
with the idea that the world is knowable and constructivism
with the idea that it is not. The reverse is often true, how-
ever: realists may well be skeptical about the knowability
of an ôindependentö reality, while constructivists argue that
reality becomes knowable precisely because of the human
element in its very constitution. The true tension lies not
between realism and constructivism but between skepticism
and dogmatism in an epistemological sense. As we have
seen, pragmatism proposes fallibilism as a middle ground
between these two extremes, maintaining that while there
are no principled barriers to the scope of human inquiry and
knowledge, none of our commitments should be regarded
as beyond criticism and revision. It is because the origi-
nal epistemological meanings of these terms have not been
retained by Watson etáal. that the pragmatist alternative
remains unclear.

This problematic framing has significant consequences
for both Watson etáal.Æs interpretation of pragmatism and its

AI & SOCIETY (2026) 41:1761û1773

1767

potential relevance to the debate on the transparency of AI
and the potential of XAI. Watson etáal. position pragmatism
as an alternative to both realism and constructivism, fore-
grounding a ôradicalö context-dependency of inquiry and
the view that facts are ôrelationalö rather than subjective
or relative. As already noted in connection with the first
premise, Watson etáal. maintain that pragmatists accept that
relevant facts can be assembled, yet argue that ôexplana-
tions are radically context-dependentöùeven suggesting
that pragmatism holds truth to be ônot as important as what
is usefulö (Watson etáal. 2024, 15). However, while Wat-
son etáal. seek to distinguish their understanding of facts
as ôrelationalö from relativism, their emphasis on radical
context dependence and the prioritization of what is use-
ful gives rise to concerns and aligns uncomfortably closely
with the skeptical narrative. Although pragmatism readily
acknowledges that different kinds of explanations may prove
useful in different contexts, shifting the focus from truth to
usefulnessùor, even more problematically, equating truth
with usefulnessùopens the door to the idea that explana-
tions are relative to particular practical contexts, and may
even be inconsistent with one another. When applied to the
issue of explainability in machine learning and AI, such an
account easily lends itself to the implication that our ability
to capture crucial aspects of how AI systems functionùin
turn enabling us to audit and validate their operations and
trace their potential significanceùwould hinge on whether
we  happen  to  pose  the  ôrightö  questions  or  demand  the
ôrightö kinds of explanations.

While pragmatism stresses both holism and the piece-
meal, evolving nature of inquiry, its fallibilism does not sug-
gest that we are restricted to purely contextual inquiries and
explanations, nor that facts are, in the relevant sense, rela-
tional. Although it acknowledges that inquiry is always pro-
visional and unfinished, pragmatism nonetheless allows for
the possibility of explanations that extend beyond immediate
contextual constraints; however fallible those explanations
may be. This clarification is central for understanding the
alternative that pragmatism offers. Grounded in fallibilism,
the significance of sociotechnical pragmatism in relation to
AI lies in its contention that, contrary to the skeptical posi-
tion, there are no principled barriers to explaining presently
opaque machine learning models. At the same time, how-
ever, pragmatism recognizes that there are no guarantees
that any limited number of inquiriesùlet alone the introduc-
tion of further technologies such as XAIùwill conclusively
resolve issues of explainability.

Another issue in the vicinity is that much of the relevance
of sociotechnical pragmatism as a contrasting narrative, as
Watson etáal. develop it, lies in its argument that we can
obtain greater insight into the consequences and implica-
tions of AI. Watson etáal. claim that the risks associated with
XAI are primarily ôepistemicö rather than ethical, a view

that aligns with broader AI debates, where transparency has
frequently been elevated to a value seemingly for its own
sake (Jobin etáal. 2019; see Munn 2023). Even more gener-
ally, Watson etáal. suggest that sociotechnical pragmatism
places procedural regularity and transparency at the core of
good governance, valuing ôjustification through informed,
rational, and honest discourseö (Watson etáal. 2024, 10).
However, the value of transparency is often instrumental
rather than intrinsicùas a means to understanding the con-
sequences and significance of AI systems and identifying
errors. While the problems involved may most immediately
be epistemic in nature, the risks usually extend beyond such
issues alone. Reliance on fallibilismùeven as elaborated
hereùshould not lead to an exclusive focus on transpar-
ency and procedural viability while neglecting the substan-
tive actionability of ethical ideals, resulting in fallibilism
without meliorism.

To be sure, Watson, M÷kander, and FloridiÆs proposal
does not lack what was above distinguished as the meliorist
dimension of pragmatism. This is particularly evident in
their proposal of sociotechnical pragmatism as advancing
regulation aimed at fostering AI technologies for socially
beneficial purposes while mitigating risks. For example,
discussing their second central case, the fairness of AI sys-
tems, Watson etáal. argue that a dogmatist narrative results
in attempts to ôautomate our way out of fundamental social
problems like systematic discrimination and structural ine-
qualityö (Watson etáal. 2024, 11). Such an approach is not
only problematic in itself but also overlooks the possibility
that fairness could be achieved without relying solely on
technology. In contrast, Watson etáal.Æs pragmatist perspec-
tive considers AI systems deployed in high-risk domains
on a case-by-case basis, and advances good regulation that
promotes both growth and pro-social outcomes.

Once again, the distinction between fallibilism and melio-
rism helps clarify the mediating role that pragmatism may
offer. Watson etáal. link restrictive regulation of techno-
logical systems to sociotechnical skepticism as one of its
implications. On the other hand, as just seen, they associate
sociotechnical pragmatism with regulatory efforts, making
it difficult to distinguish their proposed position from soci-
otechnical skepticism. As already argued, however, while
skepticism accurately describes the rejection of epistemo-
logical premises 1 and 2, the position contesting the third or
(at least) the fourth premise is better characterized as pes-
simism. Such pessimism is not necessarily tied to regulatory
efforts; rather, it suggests that technological systems do not
improve the evaluation of future developments, even in the
presence of regulatory interventions. In this Jamesian sense,
rejecting the third or fourth premise contributes to inaction,
as the negative outcome is perceived as inevitable. From
this perspective, the contrast between the consequences of
sociotechnical optimism and pessimism, on the one hand,

and pragmatism becomes clear: while both optimism and
pessimism lead to inactionùeither by assuming that tech-
nological progress will naturally resolve issues or the very
oppositeùsociotechnical pragmatism as a meliorist project
aims at improvement by active measures, such as the regula-
tory efforts of shaping technological development Watson
etáal. propose.

This  elaboration  of  the  alternative  provided  by  prag-
matism also exposes how the melioristic aspect of Watson
etáal.Æs proposal is structured around relatively fixed and
predetermined ideals. In their account, sociotechnical prag-
matists propose evaluating the design and use of AI systems
in terms of how they ôadvance or hinder specific normative
goalsö requiring policymakers to be clear about the norma-
tive ends they aim to achieve (Watson etáal. 2024, 8). While
pragmatists, in this view, are open to experimentation and
empirical determination of ôwhat does and does not work,ö
they argue that such experimentation is ideally conducted
ôaccording to public and predetermined criteriaö (Watson
etáal. 2024, 8). However, this risks conveying the impression
that meliorism is founded on pre-established ends, which are
then hoped to be enacted through experimentation and regu-
lation. In turn, this renders Watson etáal.Æs proposal suscepti-
ble to what might be characterized as regulatory optimism:
not only is regulation and policy-making seen as practically
capable of shaping the future, but the normative foundations
on which such efforts rest are also treated as readily avail-
able. Such an optimism overlooks the fallibilist dimension of
pragmatism with respect to normative starting points.

Indeed, Watson etáal. give relatively little direct attention
to how sociotechnical pragmatism approaches the second,
value premise. They suggest that ôwhat counts as morally
acceptable is a revisable cultural inheritanceö and that there
is ôno reason to believe that this inheritance should offer
theoretical closure,ö concluding that ôwe should not expect
normative tensions to be overcomeö by technological inno-
vation or regulation (Watson etáal. 2024, 15). However, the
formulation appears too strong, lending itself to the impli-
cation that such a prospect can be ruled out in advanceùa
stance that contrasts with fallibilism and borders on skepti-
cism. More broadly, rather than simply focusing on histo-
ricity of our ethical perspectives, pragmatism advocates an
empirical and experimental approach to values and norms.
Our ethical commitments are to be subjected to inquiry, not
merely used or applied as a guide in the process of evaluat-
ing and regulating technology. It is this interplay of fallibi-
lism and meliorism that pragmatism may further contribute
to debates on the ethical and societal significance of socio-
technical developments.

This contribution becomes especially relevant in light of
an emerging body of research examining how values change
in response to new technologies. Based on the notion that
technologies  can  expand  or  constrain  decision-making

options and enable new interpretations of moral situations
(Verbeek 2011), recent research has proposed that the adop-
tion and use of sociotechnical systems may lead to shifts
in moral beliefs and practices (van de Poel 2021), includ-
ing changes in central normative concepts such as truth and
trust (Danaher & Sµtra 2023). However, it is easy to assume
that such value change arises from difficulties in ôapply-
ingö existing ethical perspectives to new situations brought
about by technological and sociotechnical transformations.
Alternatively, value change may be understood as occur-
ring when new technologies provide information or insights
that alter how we perceive the world, prompting adjustments
in our practices to better align with our values. As already
suggested, however, pragmatism maintains that novel and
problematic situations themselves can serve as catalysts for
inquiry into the ethical theories and perspectives underlying
our particular values, exposing their potential limitations
and errors.

Indeed,  a  pragmatist  approach  to  value  change  has
recently been developed by Ibo van de Poel and Olya Kudina
(2022). Drawing on Deweyan concepts, they propose that
technology-induced value change can occur through the
reinterpretation of values in a specific situation (which they
term value dynamism), the subsequent modification of value
generalizations that influence future contexts (value adapta-
tion), or the emergence of entirely new values from indeter-
minate situations (value emergence). However, even here
there remains further room to explore the full potential of a
pragmatist and fallibilist perspective in this context. While
van de Poel and Kudina (2022) reference DeweyÆs experi-
mental approach to value change, their focus is primarily on
how values have already changed or may continue to do so
in response to new, indeterminate situations. Less attention
is given to how this revision of values could itself be under-
stood as, or actively shaped into, a deliberative process of
inquiry, akin to the employment of experimental methods in
other domains of research. A fallibilist stance on our norma-
tive starting pointsùincluding established ethical values,
norms, and principlesùcan serve as a counterbalance to
potential overconfidence in the activity prompted by melio-
rism, including regulatory efforts. The need for such a coun-
terbalance becomes particularly relevant when considering
the practical differences between Watson etáal.Æs proposal
and pragmatism viewed as sociotechnical fallibilism and
meliorism in light of a central counterargument.

Watson, M÷kander, and FloridiÆs proposal for sociotechnical
pragmatism involves both fallibilist and meliorist elements
that are central to philosophical pragmatism. The foregoing
discussion has highlighted how keeping these dimensions

distinct enables us to see how pragmatism offers an alterna-
tive to both skeptical and dogmatist (in the epistemological
sense) and optimistic and pessimistic (in the ethical sense)
approaches to the development of new technologies. These
distinctions also serve to illuminate the interplay of these
elements in the pragmatist approach, showing how the falli-
bilist element encourages inquiry into the values, norms, and
ideals that underlie melioristic activity. To further clarify the
practical difference between Watson etáal.Æs proposal and the
conception of pragmatism as sociotechnical fallibilism and
meliorism, we may now turn to a final consideration. With
these distinctions in hand, pragmatism can be shown capable
of addressing a challenge that could be levied against Wat-
son etáal.Æs proposal as an approach to sociotechnical issues.
This challenge comes from a direction that may seem
unexpected, namely, the issue of theodicy and its recent
criticism. The so-called problem of theodicy is typically
addressed in theology and the philosophy of religion. Since
antiquity,  philosophers  and  theologians  have  pondered
how an omnipotent, omniscient, and benevolent God could
allow horrendous evil and suffering in the world. Traditional
theodicies have sought to offer reasons for why God per-
mits such evil. However, it has recently been proposed that
such a ôtheodicist logicö is not confined to religious con-
texts but can be identified in a variety of non-religious set-
tingsùincluding political decision-making, administration,
social practices, and public institutions (Pihlstr÷m 2020).
The unifying feature of this theodicist logic is the effort to
justify, excuse or find meaning in the suffering of others by
appealing to a supposed ôcommon goodö that the suffering
allegedly serves. To combat this pattern and reject the the-
odicist presuppositions that underpin secular practices and
institutions, an anti-theodicist perspective that eschews such
justifications and excuses has been proposed for renewing
ethics within contemporary societies (Pihlstr÷m 2022).

At the first glance, the abstract criticism of theodicist
logic may seem distant from the issues at hand. To grasp
the relevance of theodicist logic in the context of sociotech-
nical development and AI, consider an alternative narrative
that expresses sociotechnical optimism and pessimism in
practical termsùone that foregrounds a tension between two
centers of power seeking to shape and govern the develop-
ment of digital technologies. This duality has been evident
in discussions on the regulation of social media platforms
and data-driven services (Tambini 2021; Schroeder 2025),
and features prominently in contemporary public discourse
on AI (MacCarthy & Propp 2021; Siegmann & Anderljung
2022; AlgorithmWatch 2024). In this narrative, one power
nodeùoften called ôSilicon Valleyöùof giant tech com-
panies rapidly develops and pushes new products and ser-
vices to the market, empowered by a ôpragmaticö attitude
of moving forward, seeing what happens, only later express-
ing regret or paying (often disproportionately small) fines

if necessary. The other power nodeùcall it ôBrusselsöù
consists  of  administrations,  bureaucrats,  policymakers,
and politicians attempting to devise new forms of regula-
tion for these technologies amid a deluge of white papers,
policy proposals, and lobbying efforts. While the relative
influence of these two forces has varied over time, the story
goes, Silicon Valley appears to have the upper hand with a
renewed push for deregulation and more relaxed regulation.
This trend is reinforced by the present U.S. administrationÆs
emphasis on deregulation, particularly under the banner of
protecting freedom of speech (Echikson 2025). The rheto-
ric of freedom and deregulation, however, may serve as a
pretext for further consolidating control over political and
societal discourse, as well as for expanding the extraction of
user dataùespecially within platforms owned by powerful
actors closely aligned with the administration.

This narrative, while certainly a rough sketch, enables
us to see how a pessimistic vision of our sociotechnical
futureùor a form of sociotechnical pessimismùcan build
upon various observations. Silicon Valley, driven by eco-
nomic  interests,  displays  little  concern  for  the  negative
impacts of their technologies on the human condition, as
documented in critical research. Moreover, its operations are
difficult to constrain by the means at the disposal of Brus-
selsùinsignificant sanctions and slow, cumbersome regula-
tory procedures often trailing far behind the availability of
new technologies. Even worse, Brussels has been infiltrated
with representatives of Silicon Valley, shaping regulatory
efforts with their economic influence, with business leaders
and executives expressing concerns over the implications
of regulation on innovation, economic growth, and global
competitiveness. It is a short step to the pessimistic con-
clusion that we live in the midst of a human and societal
experiment of an unprecedented scale. New technologies,
including AI, are being introduced at an unforeseen pace,
with the documented potential for excessively harmful con-
sequences, including actual human suffering. Meanwhile,
following a theodicist logic, these developments are justified
by claims about the potential benefits of these technologies
in terms of economic growth, scientific breakthroughs, and
overall improvement to the human condition.

A similar anti-theodicist criticism might be well raised in
connection with Watson, M÷kander, and FloridiÆs mediating
narrative of sociotechnical pragmatism that, by and large,
proposes that we observe how technologies develop, assess
their consequences, and regulate them accordingly. This nar-
rative is based on the view that we may successfully investi-
gate the operations of new technologies and their effects on
human life, while recognizing that our actions mayùbut do
not necessarilyùlead to improvements in the world. In prac-
tice, however, this position risks becoming indistinguishable
from a ôwait and seeö approach, grounded in the confidence
that emerging technologies can be understood, reviewed,

and assessed as they unfold and that potential harms can
concomitantly be addressed through regulation.

Nevertheless, also the vision of pragmatism as a com-
bination  of  sociotechnical  fallibilism  and  meliorism,  as
developed here, could be subject to a similar criticism. This
pragmatist perspective suggests that inquiry should engage
not only with the actualized and expected repercussions of
sociotechnical developments but also with their unintended
consequences, including the revision of values prompted by
problematic situations. Does this not similarly propose a vast
societal experiment where even devastating consequences
for individuals and, in the worst case, all of humanity, might
be explained and justified as necessary costs in the advance-
ment of inquiry?

Despite the structural similarities of these objections,
however, the differences between the two alternative prag-
matist approaches points to a significant difference in prac-
tice. Watson etáal.Æs proposal largely rests on two central
elements. The first is the rejection of both sociotechnical
skepticism and dogmatism, grounded in the belief that we
can understand how new technologies functionùwhether
through explainable AI or other methodsùand assess their
consequences. The second is the assumption that regulation
and policy-making can shape deployment of new technolo-
gies to improve outcomes and prevent worst-case scenarios.
As we have seen, in practice, the combination of these views
reflects a form of regulatory optimism. In the first instance,
understanding how technologies work is expected to take
place during or after their introduction. In the second, Wat-
son etáal. largely presuppose that the values guiding regula-
tion are reasonably well established, providing a foundation
for shaping the design, introduction, and use of emerging
technologies.

Pragmatism as sociotechnical fallibilism and meliorism,
as developed here, offers a different vision of approach-
ing technological developments. Much of our assessment
of  the  consequences  and  implications  of  new  technolo-
gies will inevitably take place after their introduction into
actual human practices. However, experimentation need not
unfold on the grand scale of entire societies or humanity.
Pragmatism instead encourages proactive inquiry, anticipat-
ing potential consequences before large-scale implementa-
tion. Moreover, extending fallibilism to normative questions
about the values and principles that should guide technologi-
cal development, this approach resists the assumption that
we must wait to see how values shift in response to techno-
logical change. Instead, it suggests that we can anticipate
how emerging situations may call for revisions to the values
that currently shape regulation and governance. As Watson
etáal. (2024, 8) nicely put it, pragmatism views emancipation
as ôneither inevitable nor finalö. What provides the possi-
bility of such emancipation, however, is a commitment to
research that does not simply react to technological change

but actively engages in anticipating and critically examining
its implications before they materialize. Pragmatism does
not attempt to justify or explain away potential harms as nec-
essary costs of progress; it resists a theodicist logic by insist-
ing that the ethical and societal implications of emerging
technologies are actively interrogated and adjusted before
large-scale deployment by means of controlled processes
of inquiry.

Indeed, by providing an alternative vision of democratic
governance of AI technologies, pragmatism as sociotech-
nical fallibilism and meliorism provides a basis for chal-
lenging the overall condition in which power over techno-
logical development is concentrated in the hands of a few
key actorsùsuch as Silicon Valley and Brussels of our ear-
lier narrative. Recent research serves to help illustrate this
alternative. Researchers have increasingly highlighted the
democratic implications of AI. For example, Taylor etáal.
(2024) argue that democratizing AI is essential for foster-
ing trust, equity, and fairness, particularly in the deploy-
ment of AI-enabled technologies within public administra-
tion, and advocate for a Maximum Feasible Participation
(MFP) approach to AI development. However, how such
democratization should be implemented, and how partici-
patory elements should be incorporated into AI systems,
remains largely unresolved. Instead, in line with the nar-
rative just discussed, AI development is largely driven by
technical experts and large corporations, while regulation is
similarly conducted by a relatively small group of political
decision-makers. As suggested by a recent study by Hedlund
and Persson (2025) examining the perspectives of various
expertsùincluding  technical  developers,  policymakers,
and other specialistsùforward-looking responsibility in AI
development is primarily understood in terms of ôcontri-
butionö that is closely linked to an actorÆs ability to effect
change in AI development, with influence frequently cited
by study participants as a key criterion for responsibility.

Sociotechnical pragmatism, informed by the principles
of fallibilism and meliorism, presents an alternative vision.
First, adopting this approach would require extending both
the examination of AI and decision-making beyond a lim-
ited number of expertsùsuch as technological developers,
major corporations, and policymakersùon the basis that
the consequences of technological development cannot be
fully anticipated from any number of restricted perspec-
tives. Instead, the assessment of emerging technologies and
of policies concerning their regulation should be distributed
across multiple actors and institutions capable of contribut-
ing to empirical inquiry by identifying risks, evaluating con-
sequences, and revising normative assumptions accordingly.
Second, this broader distribution of processes of review-
ing  and  making  decisions  concerning  new  technologies
aligns with DeweyÆs pragmatist conception of democracy
as a form of social inquiry, where democratic participation

is not confined to formal governance but involves collec-
tive problem-solving through empirical means. This view
acknowledges the indispensability of expertise in addressing
complex societal challenges that cannot be resolved solely
through public discussion and deliberation. However, while
processes of social inquiry may often be expert-driven, they
also involve affected individuals and communities as active
contributors. Applying this view of democracy to AI govern-
ance would entail treating affected individuals and commu-
nities not merely as passive recipients of new technologies or
policies but as sources of information and data on problems,
potential solutions, and the observed consequences of new
technologies and the policies regulating them. Advancing
such an approach to AI governance as social inquiry would
introduce a form of democracy that would break the nar-
rative of technological development as dictated solely by
industry priorities or static regulatory norms.

6   Conclusion

This discussion has sought to refine and extend Watson,
M÷kander, and FloridiÆs timely proposal for sociotechni-
cal pragmatism by clarifying its relationship to the broader
pragmatist tradition, particularly through the  notions of
fallibilism and meliorism. While Watson etáal.Æs proposal
incorporates both fallibilist and meliorist dimensions, the
way they frame the opposition between alternative narratives
introduces conceptual ambiguities that complicate the artic-
ulation of pragmatism as a distinct position. Their distinc-
tion between sociotechnical dogmatism and sociotechnical
skepticism conflates epistemological and ethical dimensions,
making it difficult to separate the proposed pragmatist stance
from both the inclination toward regulation that they associ-
ate with skepticism and the regulatory optimism that their
confidence in the capacity to examine and govern technology
appears to entail.

The pragmatist alternative in the study of technology, it
has been argued, is best understood through the interplay of
fallibilism and meliorism. Fallibilism offers an epistemologi-
cal stance that neither assumes definitive knowledge of the
function of new technologies and their repercussions, nor
denies the possibility of understanding them, as exempli-
fied by its stance toward the potential of explainable AI.
Meliorism, in turn, introduces an ethical orientation toward
improvement without assuming that progress is inevita-
ble. Together, these two dimensions distinguish pragma-
tism from varieties of both sociotechnical dogmatism and
skepticism, on the one hand, and sociotechnical optimism
and pessimism, on the other. Pragmatism does not presup-
pose that technological development will necessarily lead
to progress, nor does it withdraw into skepticism or inac-
tion in response to uncertainty. Instead, it treats inquiry as

an ongoing, iterative process of understanding and shaping
technological change, including the potential revisions to
values and norms that may emerge in response to the new
situations introduced by technological developments.

In the context of AI in particular, a central practical dif-
ference follows in relation to the regulation of these tech-
nologies. Watson etáal.Æs proposal tends toward regulatory
optimism,  assuming  that  governance  mechanisms  can
direct technological deployment in socially beneficial ways.
However, this assumes that the normative foundations of
regulation are already established, and risks making policy
interventions reactive rather than proactive. By contrast, the
interplay of fallibilism and meliorism resists the assumption
that values are fixed and instead treats them as subject to
inquiry. Rather than assuming that governance should adapt
to technological change as it unfolds, pragmatism suggests
that ethical inquiry should be integrated into the develop-
ment process itself, ensuring that potential consequences
and normative shifts are actively examined in advance. By
doing so, pragmatism as sociotechnical fallibilism and meli-
orism proposes a way to avoid the risks of a great human and
societal experimentation which treats harm as an inevitable
cost of progress. Moreover, the pragmatist conception of
democracy as social inquiry offers an alternative vision of
AI governance where the assessment of emerging technolo-
gies is distributed across multiple actors and institutions,
and affected individuals and communities are incorporated
as active contributors rather than passive recipients of new
technologies and policies.
