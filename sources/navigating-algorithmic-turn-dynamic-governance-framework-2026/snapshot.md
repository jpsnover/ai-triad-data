Abstract: Artificial Intelligence (AI) is rapidly recasting educational practice, yet
most institutions still govern its use through ad‑hoc or reactive measures. The resulting
policy vacuum threatens academic integrity, equity, and public trust. This study develops
and empirically validates the Dynamic AI Governance in Education (DAIGE) framework—a
four‑pillar model designed to guide proactive, ethical, and inclusive AI adoption in both K‑12
and higher‑education settings.
Methods: A convergent‑triangulation mixed‑methods design combined (i) an
NLP‑enhanced systematic review and global policy scan of 512 documents, (ii) qualitative
comparative analysis of twelve early‑adopter institutions across four continents, and (iii) a
three‑round Delphi study with 35 international experts. Latent‑topic modelling, cross‑case
synthesis, and consensus statistics (IQR, ΔMedian) ensured analytic rigour.
Findings: Five high‑salience policy themes emerged—academic integrity, data
privacy, teacher capacity, equity, and AI literacy—yet fewer than 10 % of institutions
reported formal generative‑AI guidelines. Case‑study sites that adopted multi‑stakeholder
co‑creation and tiered‑permission protocols achieved a 21‑percentage‑point higher teacher
and student buy‑in (χ² = 9.67, p < .01) and halved academic‑misconduct incidents within one
term. The Delphi panel reached consensus on 26 of 28 DAIGE elements, rating “Iterative
Governance & Continuous Improvement” and “Inclusive Stakeholder Co‑Creation” as the
most critical pillars.
Conclusions. DAIGE operationalises socio‑technical, diffusion, complexity, and
stakeholder theory into a practical roadmap that can close the gap between lofty ethical
principles and everyday classroom realities.
Implications. Institutional leaders, regulators, and EdTech developers can use
DAIGE’s indicators as accreditation benchmarks, audit tools, and product‑alignment guides,
ensuring that AI innovation advances educational quality and social justice in tandem

Introduction

Artificial Intelligence (AI) has become emblematic of the twenty‑first‑century
“general‑purpose technology,” reshaping virtually every sector of the global economy. Education
now stands on the leading edge of this transformation, with commentators heralding an
impending “AI revolution” in teaching and learning (WEF, 2024c). Personalised learning engines,
predictive analytics for student success, and automated administrative workflows already signal
AI’s capacity to extend human capability and widen access for diverse learners (Milberg, 2024).
Supranational agencies such as UNESCO and the OECD, along with national ministries, are
therefore prioritising AI‑driven innovation as a means of strengthening equity, efficiency, and
learner‑centred pedagogies (UNESCO, 2023; OECD/Education International, 2023).
Yet the same technologies that promise unprecedented gains introduce profound risks:
breaches of academic integrity, erosion of data privacy, amplification of algorithmic bias, teacher
deskilling, and the prospect of a widened digital divide, particularly in resource‑constrained
contexts (OECD/Education International, 2023; UNESCO, 2023). Economic volatility further
threatens equitable AI adoption, while sustainability imperatives demand that educational
innovations remain ethically and environmentally responsible amid rapid, unpredictable change
(Khan et al., 2024).

worldwide report possessing formal guidance on generative AI use (Partovi & Yongpradit, 2024).
Institutional responses therefore oscillate between blanket prohibitions and laissez‑faire
permissiveness (Elhussein et al., 2024), leaving the complex interplay of pedagogy, ethics,
equity, and stakeholder engagement only partially addressed.
Problem statement. Current AI policy development in education is predominantly
reactive and fragmented, lacking a coherent, adaptive, and theory‑informed governance
framework. Without such a framework, institutions risk inconsistent practice, inequitable
outcomes, and the under‑realisation of AI’s transformative potential.
Purpose and objectives. This study develops and conceptually validates the
Dynamic AI Governance in Education (DAIGE) framework—an integrative, ethically grounded
model designed to help institutions anticipate, implement, and iteratively refine AI initiatives. To
meet this purpose, we pose four specific research questions: RQ1. What core components,
success factors, and systemic barriers emerge from contemporary literature and practice
regarding AI policy in K‑12 and higher education? RQ2. How do pioneering institutions navigate
the ethical, pedagogical, and operational challenges of AI integration, and what best practices
can be discerned? RQ3. In what ways can socio‑technical systems theory, diffusion of
innovation, complexity theory, and stakeholder theory be synthesised into a resilient governance
model for AI? RQ4. Which elements of the proposed DAIGE framework achieve expert

model that aligns technological innovation with pedagogical integrity and social justice.

II. Theoretical & Conceptual Framework
The Dynamic AI Governance in Education (DAIGE) framework is intentionally grounded
in a plural‐theory foundation. A single lens cannot capture the intertwined technical, social, and
ethical currents that shape AI adoption in schooling; therefore, we weave together four
long‑standing strands of scholarship—Socio‑Technical Systems (STS) Theory, Innovation
Diffusion Theory (IDT), Complexity Theory, and Stakeholder Theory—to construct a
multi‑layered view that operates from the macro level of policy ecosystems down to the micro
level of classroom practice. Figure 7 (Theory‑Integration Venn) visually depicts the overlap of
these strands, while Figure 1 (DAIGE Framework) translates the synthesis into four practical
pillars.
2.1. Socio-Technical Systems (STS) Theory: Originating with Trist and Bamforth’s
(1951) seminal coal‑mining studies, STS theory maintains that organisational performance is
maximised when technological infrastructures and human systems are designed as a mutually
reinforcing whole. In educational AI, this principle warns against technological determinism:
large‑language models, adaptive tutors, or analytics dashboards must be co‑designed with
teachers’ professional agency, students’ developmental needs, and community norms in mind.
DAIGE therefore embeds human‑centric design, mandatory teacher capacity‑building, and
continuous pedagogical–technical dialogue as non‑negotiable design criteria.
2.2. Innovation Diffusion Theory (IDT): Rogers’ (2003) model explains why some
innovations cascade through a social system while others stall. Core attributes—relative
advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability, and observability—shape adoption rates. The
finding that fewer than 10 % of institutions hold formal policies on generative AI
(Partovi & Yongpradit, 2024) signals perceived complexity and weak observability of benefits.
DAIGE responds by recommending pilot programmes, clear communication plans, and

2.3. Complexity Theory: Schools and universities exemplify complex adaptive systems
(Davis & Sumara, 2006) in which myriad agents interact non‑linearly, producing emergent, often
unpredictable outcomes. Static, one‑off policies quickly become obsolete in such settings.
Complexity theory thus advocates simple governing rules, iterative feedback loops, and room
for local adaptation. DAIGE operationalises these principles through its fourth pillar—Iterative
Governance & Continuous Improvement—treating policies as living documents subject to
periodic review, bias audits, and stakeholder‑led refinements
2.4. Stakeholder Theory: Freeman’s (1984) Stakeholder Theory asserts that long‑term
organisational success depends on balancing the interests of all parties affected by strategic
choices. In the AI‑education domain, stakeholders extend from pupils and educators to parents,
technologists, policymakers, and the wider public. Neglecting any group risks resistance or
policy failure. DAIGE therefore mandates inclusive co‑creation, elevating student voice, teacher
expertise, and community values within every governance stage—from agenda‑setting to
post‑implementation evaluation.

3.1. Phase 1: NLP‑Enhanced Systematic Review and Global Policy Mapping:
Purpose (RQ1). To distill the core components, success factors, and systemic barriers in
contemporary AI‑in‑education discourse. Corpus. A total of 512 documents were retrieved
between January 2020 and March 2025 from Web of Science, Scopus, ERIC, IEEE Xplore,
SSRN, arXiv, EdArXiv, and organisational repositories (UNESCO, OECD, World Bank, EU). We
also harvested 118 publicly available institutional policies across six world regions.
Screening and coding. Title–abstract screening used ASReview’s active‑learning algorithm
(van de Schoot et al., 2021); full‑text eligibility was confirmed by two reviewers (κ = 0.82). Latent
Dirichlet Allocation (k = 5) (Blei, Ng, & Jordan, 2003) and BERTopic models then surfaced
dominant thematic clusters (Grootendorst, 2022), while BERT‑based named‑entity recognition
traced stakeholder roles, tools, and policy instruments (Devlin et al., 2019). Qualitative content
analysis (NVivo 14) (QSR International, 2023) extracted best‑practice exemplars that later
informed the Delphi prompts (Dalkey & Helmer, 1963).
3.2. Phase 2: Qualitative Comparative Case Analysis: Purpose (RQ2). To explore
how pioneering institutions navigate ethical, pedagogical, and operational challenges.
Sampling. Using maximum‑variation criteria (Patton, 2015), we purposively selected 12
institutions (six K‑12 systems; six universities) spanning North America, Europe, Sub‑Saharan
Africa, and East Asia. Each had been cited by UNESCO, WEF, or Phase 1 documents as an
early AI adopter. Data collection. We conducted 54 semi‑structured interviews (mean
length = 47 min) with administrators, faculty, IT staff, and student representatives; gathered 327
internal artefacts (policies, training slide‑decks, meeting minutes); and observed eight
policy‑committee meetings. All interviews were recorded, transcribed, and member‑checked.
Analysis. Iterative thematic coding (NVivo) achieved intercoder reliability of κ = 0.79. Patterns
were compared within and across cases, and a cross‑case synthesis matrix identified recurring
enablers (e.g., tiered‑permission models) and context‑specific nuances (e.g., mobile‑first AI
deployment in low‑connectivity settings).
3.3. Phase 3: Delphi Validation of the DAIGE Framework: Purpose (RQ4). To secure
expert consensus on the DAIGE pillars, propositions, and implementation pathways.
Panel. Thirty‑five experts representing academia, EdTech firms, ministries of education, and
NGOs completed at least two rounds; retention was 94 %. Procedure. Round 1 solicited
open‑ended critiques of the provisional framework. Rounds 2 and 3 asked panellists to rate 28
elements on a 5‑point Likert scale. Consensus was defined a priori as an inter‑quartile range ≤ 1
and stability across rounds (ΔMedian ≤ 0.5). SPSS 29 produced descriptive statistics; qualitative
comments were thematically clustered to guide revisions.

pace of policy adoption can be analyzed through the lens of institutional isomorphism (DiMaggio
& Powell, 1983). Institutions may adopt AI policies due to coercive pressures (e.g., accreditation

standards). Our findings suggest current AI policy adoption is primarily driven by mimetic and
early normative pressures, lacking the deep institutionalization that robust coercive or fully
developed normative frameworks would provide.

V. Conclusion & Recommendations
The mixed‑methods inquiry reported in this article set out to move the governance
conversation from scattered, reactive measures toward a coherent, theory‑driven practice for
responsible AI adoption in education. By triangulating an NLP‑enhanced global policy scan,
cross‑case analysis of twelve pioneering institutions, and a three‑round Delphi with 35 experts,
we produced and validated the Dynamic AI Governance in Education (DAIGE) framework—a
four‑pillar model that is simultaneously principled and adaptive. The evidence confirms all three
study hypotheses: multi‑stakeholder co‑creation markedly lifts teacher‑student buy‑in; tiered
permissions and AI‑literacy programmes reduce integrity breaches; and iterative review cycles
strengthen institutional resilience to fast‑moving technological change. In doing so, the study
advances the socio‑technical literature with a governance architecture that operationalises
human‑centric ethics, embraces complexity, and embeds continuous learning.

5.3. Future Research
The validation of the DAIGE framework represents an important first step toward
coherent, equity‑oriented AI governance; nevertheless, several empirical and conceptual
unknowns remain. We therefore outline four inter‑locking research programmes that build
directly on the study’s variables, propositions, and mixed‑methods protocol. Pursuing these lines
of inquiry will test the framework’s external validity, illuminate its boundary conditions, and
advance the broader field of AI‑in‑education scholarship.
5.3.1 Longitudinal Evaluation of DAIGE Implementation: A key limitation of the
present research is its cross‑sectional snapshot of early adopters. Future studies should follow a
cohort of diverse institutions over three to five academic years, assessing how sustained use of
the four DAIGE pillars influences (i) learning outcomes (standardised scores,
course‑completion rates), (ii) teacher workload and role evolution, and (iii) equity indicators
such as demographic gaps in AI‑tool access. Mixed‑effects growth‑curve models (Singer &
Willett, 2003) could trace change trajectories, while qualitative process‑tracing would capture
local adaptations. Such evidence is essential to verify whether the initial gains in
Policy Effectiveness and Pedagogical Integration reported here endure—or attenuate—over
time.
5.3.2 Cross‑Cultural “Glocalisation” Studies: The Delphi panel underscored the need
to adapt DAIGE to differing cultural, linguistic, and regulatory settings. Comparative case studies
across, for example, Nordic welfare states, Sub‑Saharan African LICs, and East‑Asian high‑tech
hubs could examine how local norms reshape interpretations of ethical compliance, student
agency, and data sovereignty. Employing critical policy discourse analysis alongside
stakeholder interviews would reveal both convergent principles and culturally specific inflections,
thereby refining the framework’s universality claim.
5.3.3 Political‑Economy Dynamics of AI Governance: Our findings hint at—but do not
fully unpack—the influence of EdTech vendors, philanthropic capital, and geopolitical
competition on institutional policy choices. Future work should map actor networks and funding
